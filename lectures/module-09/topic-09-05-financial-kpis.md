# Module 9 – KPI Management and Performance Measurement
## Topic 9.5: Financial KPIs

**Main learning objective:** Matutunan mong i-connect ang operational performance sa financial performance ng isang company.

Sa previous topics, pinag-aralan natin kung paano sinusukat ang performance ng demand, supply, production, at inventory. Ngayon, pag-uusapan natin kung paano nagiging actual financial results ang mga operational activities na iyon.

Ito ang importanteng shift sa perspective ng isang S&OP Analyst:

- Hindi sapat na alam mong nakapag-produce ang factory ng 100,000 units.
- Hindi sapat na alam mong 98% ang production attainment.
- Hindi sapat na alam mong 95% ang customer service level.
- Kailangan mo ring malaman kung kumita ba ang company, na-control ba ang costs, at sustainable ba ang inventory investment.

**A good operational plan must also make financial sense.**

# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What are Financial KPIs?

Ang Financial KPIs ay measurable indicators na ginagamit ng company para malaman kung financially healthy at successful ang business operations nito.

Sinusukat nila kung:

- Gaano kalaki ang revenue na generated ng company.
- Magkano ang ginastos para makagawa at makapagbenta ng products.
- Magkano ang natitirang profit pagkatapos ibawas ang expenses.
- Gaano kaepektibo ang paggamit ng company sa available capital.
- Gaano kalaki ang perang nakatali sa inventory.
- Naabot ba ang financial targets at budget.

Sa S&OP, ginagamit ang Financial KPIs para malaman kung ang proposed demand at supply plans ay financially achievable at aligned sa business objectives.

## 1.2 Why are Financial KPIs important in S&OP?

Imagine natin na may manufacturing company.

Nagkaroon ng increase sa customer demand. Nag-decide ang operations team na dagdagan ang production.

Resulta:

| Operational result | Financial implication |
|---|---|
| Higher production volume | Potentially higher revenue |
| Additional raw materials | Higher cash requirement |
| More finished goods | Higher inventory investment |
| Overtime production | Higher labor costs |
| Additional warehouse requirements | Higher storage costs |
| More customer deliveries | Potentially higher logistics costs |

Mapapansin mo na kahit positive ang increase sa production, may corresponding financial consequences.

Hindi automatic na kapag mas maraming products ang ginawa, mas maraming profit ang kikitain.

**Ito ang dahilan kung bakit kailangan ng financial perspective sa S&OP.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Understanding a Manufacturing Business Through Money

Imagine na may sarili kang manufacturing company na gumagawa ng electronic components.

Every month:

1. Bumibili ka ng raw materials.
2. Ginagamit mo ang materials para gumawa ng products.
3. Nagbabayad ka ng production labor at factory expenses.
4. Ibinebenta mo ang finished products sa customers.
5. Kinokolekta mo ang payment.
6. Binabayaran mo ang suppliers, employees, utilities, at other expenses.

Sa buong process na ito, may iba't ibang financial measurements.

Let's simplify:

### The financial flow of a manufacturing company

**RAW MATERIALS**  
Money spent to purchase materials

↓

**PRODUCTION**  
Materials + labor + manufacturing overhead

↓

**FINISHED GOODS INVENTORY**  
Products waiting to be sold

↓

**SALES / REVENUE**  
Money earned from selling products

↓

**PROFIT**  
What remains after applicable expenses

Pero may isang importanteng distinction:

**Revenue is not the same as cash received.**

Halimbawa:

Nag-deliver ka ng products worth ₱1,000,000 sa customer under 30-day payment terms.

- Revenue: ₱1,000,000, assuming the sale qualifies for revenue recognition.
- Cash received today: potentially ₱0.
- Accounts receivable: ₱1,000,000, assuming wala pang collection.

Meaning, kumita ka sa accounting records, pero hindi pa pumapasok ang cash.

Dito natin makikita kung bakit kailangang magkahiwalay ang revenue, profit, at cash flow.

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 The Five Fundamental Financial Concepts

Bago tayo mag-compute ng KPIs, kailangan malinaw muna ang five concepts na ito.

### A. Revenue

Revenue is the amount earned by a company from selling its goods or services during a specific period.

Simple explanation:

Ito ang halaga ng products na naibenta mo.

Example:

- Units sold: 10,000
- Selling price: ₱100/unit

Revenue:

\[
10,000 \times ₱100 = ₱1,000,000
\]

Revenue does not automatically mean profit.

### B. Cost

Cost refers to the monetary resources consumed or incurred to produce, acquire, or deliver goods and services.

Sa manufacturing, examples nito ang:

- Raw material costs
- Direct labor
- Manufacturing overhead
- Packaging
- Freight, depending on the accounting treatment

Example:

Kung gumastos ka ng ₱600,000 para makagawa ng products na naibenta mo, that amount may form part of your Cost of Goods Sold.

### C. Profit

Profit is the financial amount remaining after deducting applicable costs and expenses from revenue.

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱1,000,000 |
| Cost of Goods Sold | ₱600,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱400,000 |

May natira kang ₱400,000 after deducting COGS.

Pero hindi pa ito necessarily final company profit dahil may operating expenses pa.

### D. Margin

Margin expresses profit as a percentage of revenue.

Example:

\[
Gross\ Margin = \frac{400,000}{1,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
=40\%
\]

Meaning, for every ₱100 of revenue, ₱40 ang gross profit.

### E. Cash Flow

Cash flow measures the actual movement of money into and out of a business during a period.

Examples:

**Cash inflows:**
- Customer payments
- Loan proceeds
- Other cash receipts

**Cash outflows:**
- Supplier payments
- Payroll
- Utilities
- Taxes
- Equipment purchases

Important distinction:

| Concept | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Value earned from sales |
| Gross Profit | Revenue less COGS |
| Operating Profit | Gross profit less operating expenses |
| Cash Flow | Actual cash entering and leaving the business |

A company can be profitable but experience cash shortages.

For example, maraming sales pero delayed ang customer collections, habang kailangan nang bayaran ang suppliers.

This is one reason why working capital management is important in S&OP.

## 3.2 Gross Profit vs. Operating Profit

Let's examine the relationship.

### Illustrative Income Statement

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱1,000,000 |
| Less: COGS | (₱600,000) |
| **Gross Profit** | **₱400,000** |
| Less: Operating Expenses | (₱250,000) |
| **Operating Profit** | **₱150,000** |

*Illustrative figures, excluding other income, financing costs and taxes.*

**Gross Profit** measures how much remains after the cost of goods sold.

**Operating Profit** measures how much remains after deducting operating expenses as well.

For a manufacturing company:

- COGS: materials, production labor, manufacturing overhead and applicable production costs.
- Operating expenses: selling expenses, administrative expenses, and other operating expenses not already included in COGS.

Note: Actual classifications depend on the company's accounting policies.

## 3.3 Why Operational Performance Alone Is Insufficient

Let's compare two scenarios.

| KPI | Scenario A | Scenario B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Production attainment | 98% | 95% |
| Inventory turnover | 8x | 6x |
| Revenue | ₱10M | ₱12M |
| Gross margin | 35% | 25% |
| Gross profit | ₱3.5M | ₱3.0M |

Scenario B has higher revenue, but lower gross profit.

Why?

Because its cost structure is less favorable.

Scenario A also has better production attainment and inventory turnover.

This demonstrates an important principle:

**Operational efficiency, revenue growth, and profitability are related, but they are not interchangeable.**

# 4. Detailed Discussion: Key Financial KPIs

Now that we understand the fundamental financial concepts, let's discuss the actual KPIs used in manufacturing and S&OP.

For consistency, gagamit tayo ng one illustrative manufacturing company throughout this lesson.

### Our example company: ABC Electronics Manufacturing

*Illustrative monthly financial data*

| Business information | Value |
|---|---:|
| Units sold | 10,000 units |
| Selling price | ₱500/unit |
| Revenue | ₱5,000,000 |
| COGS | ₱3,200,000 |
| Operating expenses | ₱1,000,000 |
| Production volume | 12,000 units |
| Ending inventory | ₱1,200,000 |
| Budgeted revenue | ₱5,500,000 |
| Budgeted COGS | ₱3,400,000 |

*Assumed values for training purposes, not actual company data.*

## 4.1 Revenue

**Definition:** Revenue is the total monetary value of goods or services sold during a specific period.

Ito ang pangunahing measurement kung magkano ang business activity generated through sales.

**Formula:**

\[
Revenue = Units\ Sold \times Selling\ Price
\]

Where:

- Units Sold = Quantity of products sold.
- Selling Price = Selling price per unit.
- Revenue = Total sales value, expressed in currency.

**Example:**

ABC Electronics sold 10,000 units at ₱500 each.

Step 1: Identify the variables.

- Units sold = 10,000
- Selling price = ₱500

Step 2: Calculate revenue.

\[
Revenue = 10,000 \times 500
\]

\[
\boxed{Revenue = ₱5,000,000}
\]

**Business interpretation:**

The company generated ₱5 million in sales revenue for the month.

However, hindi pa natin alam kung profitable ang sales na iyon. Kailangan pa nating ibawas ang associated costs.

### Revenue Growth

Revenue Growth measures the percentage increase or decrease in revenue compared with a previous period.

**Formula:**

\[
Revenue\ Growth =
\frac{Current\ Revenue - Previous\ Revenue}
{Previous\ Revenue}\times100
\]

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Previous month revenue | ₱4,500,000 |
| Current month revenue | ₱5,000,000 |
| Increase | ₱500,000 |

Calculation:

\[
\frac{5,000,000-4,500,000}{4,500,000}
\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Revenue\ Growth=11.11\%}
\]

Interpretation:

Revenue increased by 11.11% compared with the previous month.

**Important consideration:** Revenue growth does not necessarily mean profit growth.

Possible reasons:

- Higher material prices reduced margins.
- The company offered discounts.
- More expensive products were sold at lower margins.
- Additional sales required expensive overtime or expedited shipping.

An S&OP analyst must evaluate both revenue and profitability.

---

## 4.2 Gross Profit

**Definition:** Gross Profit is the amount remaining from revenue after deducting the Cost of Goods Sold.

Formula:

\[
Gross\ Profit = Revenue - COGS
\]

Using ABC Electronics:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱5,000,000 |
| COGS | ₱3,200,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱1,800,000 |

Calculation:

\[
5,000,000-3,200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Gross\ Profit=₱1,800,000}
\]

Interpretation:

After accounting for the cost of products sold, the company has ₱1.8 million remaining to cover operating expenses and contribute to operating profit.

### Gross Profit Margin

Gross Profit Margin measures the percentage of revenue remaining after deducting COGS.

**Formula:**

\[
Gross\ Profit\ Margin =
\frac{Gross\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Substitute:

\[
=\frac{1,800,000}{5,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Gross\ Margin=36\%}
\]

Meaning:

For every ₱100 of sales:

- ₱64 goes to COGS.
- ₱36 remains as gross profit.

### Gross margin composition

| Component | Percentage |
|---|---:|
| COGS | 64% |
| Gross profit | 36% |

**S&OP application:**

Suppose demand increases by 20%, but the additional demand requires more expensive materials.

The revenue may increase, but gross margin may decline.

Kaya hindi dapat revenue alone ang basis ng decision kung tatanggapin ba ang additional demand.

---

## 4.3 Operating Profit

**Definition:** Operating Profit measures the profit generated from the company's core business operations after deducting COGS and operating expenses.

For our simplified example:

\[
Operating\ Profit =
Gross\ Profit - Operating\ Expenses
\]

Given:

- Gross Profit = ₱1,800,000
- Operating Expenses = ₱1,000,000

Calculation:

\[
1,800,000-1,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Profit=₱800,000}
\]

### Operating Margin

Operating Margin measures the percentage of revenue that remains as operating profit.

Formula:

\[
Operating\ Margin =
\frac{Operating\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

\[
=\frac{800,000}{5,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Margin=16\%}
\]

Interpretation:

For every ₱100 of revenue, ₱16 remains as operating profit after COGS and operating expenses.

**Gross Margin vs. Operating Margin**

| Measurement | Gross Margin | Operating Margin |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Revenue | Revenue |
| Deducts COGS | Yes | Yes |
| Deducts operating expenses | No | Yes |
| Measures | Production and product economics | Overall operating profitability |

Note: Operating profit is not the same as net income. Interest, taxes and certain non-operating items may still need to be deducted.

## 4.4 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

**Definition:** COGS represents the cost assigned to products that were sold during a specific accounting period.

Ito ang actual cost associated with the finished products na naibenta na.

For manufacturing companies, COGS generally includes:

1. Direct materials.
2. Direct labor.
3. Manufacturing overhead.

Examples:

| Cost component | Manufacturing example |
|---|---|
| Direct materials | Ceramic powder, PET film, chemicals |
| Direct labor | Production operators |
| Manufacturing overhead | Factory electricity, equipment depreciation, factory maintenance |

### Basic COGS Formula

For a manufacturing business, a common inventory-based formula is:

\[
COGS =
Beginning\ FG\ Inventory
+ Cost\ of\ Goods\ Manufactured
- Ending\ FG\ Inventory
\]

Where:

- Beginning FG Inventory = Finished goods value at the beginning of the period.
- Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM) = Total manufacturing cost of goods completed during the period.
- Ending FG Inventory = Finished goods value at the end of the period.

Let's calculate.

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning FG inventory | ₱500,000 |
| Cost of goods manufactured | ₱3,500,000 |
| Ending FG inventory | ₱800,000 |

Step 1:

\[
500,000+3,500,000=4,000,000
\]

Step 2:

\[
4,000,000-800,000
\]

\[
\boxed{COGS=₱3,200,000}
\]

This matches our example company's COGS.

**Why subtract ending inventory?**

Dahil hindi lahat ng products na nagawa ay naibenta.

Ang products na hindi pa naibebenta ay mananatiling inventory asset, subject to applicable accounting valuation rules, rather than being fully recognized as COGS.

**Common mistake:** Using total production cost as COGS.

Hindi sila automatically equal.

- Production cost: Cost of products manufactured.
- COGS: Cost assigned to products sold.

---

## 4.5 Production Cost per Unit

**Definition:** Production Cost per Unit measures the average manufacturing cost incurred to produce one unit of finished product.

Formula:

\[
Production\ Cost\ per\ Unit =
\frac{Total\ Manufacturing\ Cost}
{Units\ Produced}
\]

Suppose:

- Total manufacturing cost = ₱3,000,000
- Units produced = 12,000

Calculation:

\[
\frac{3,000,000}{12,000}
\]

\[
\boxed{₱250/unit}
\]

Meaning, the average manufacturing cost is ₱250 for every unit produced.

### Understanding Fixed and Variable Costs

Important ito kapag nagbabago ang production volume.

**Variable Costs**

Costs that generally change with production volume.

Examples:
- Raw materials
- Production consumables
- Certain direct labor costs

**Fixed Costs**

Costs that generally remain constant within a relevant activity range and period.

Examples:
- Factory rent
- Certain equipment depreciation
- Fixed salaries

Let's examine:

| Cost component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Variable manufacturing costs | ₱2,400,000 |
| Fixed manufacturing costs | ₱600,000 |
| Total manufacturing cost | ₱3,000,000 |
| Units produced | 12,000 |
| Cost per unit | ₱250 |

Variable cost per unit:

\[
\frac{2,400,000}{12,000}=₱200
\]

Fixed cost per unit:

\[
\frac{600,000}{12,000}=₱50
\]

Total:

\[
₱200+₱50=₱250/unit
\]

### What happens when production increases?

Assume the company increases production to 15,000 units without increasing its fixed costs.

| Description | 12,000 units | 15,000 units |
|---|---:|---:|
| Variable cost per unit | ₱200 | ₱200 |
| Total variable cost | ₱2,400,000 | ₱3,000,000 |
| Fixed cost | ₱600,000 | ₱600,000 |
| Total cost | ₱3,000,000 | ₱3,600,000 |
| Fixed cost per unit | ₱50 | ₱40 |
| Total cost per unit | ₱250 | ₱240 |

**Unit cost improvement**

The average production cost decreases from ₱250 to ₱240.

Why?

Because the same fixed manufacturing cost is distributed across more units.

This is called **fixed cost absorption** or the effect of production volume on fixed cost per unit.

But remember:

Higher production does not automatically improve profitability.

If the additional products cannot be sold, the company may simply accumulate inventory.

Also, actual fixed-cost allocation and inventory accounting depend on applicable accounting standards and company policy. Producing more than normal capacity does not necessarily allow all fixed costs to be capitalized into inventory.

## 4.6 Inventory Carrying Cost

**Definition:** Inventory Carrying Cost is the cost associated with holding inventory over time.

Kapag marami kang inventory, may financial consequences kahit hindi mo pa ginagamit o naibebenta ang products.

Typical carrying cost components:

| Component | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Storage cost | Warehouse space and storage facilities |
| Insurance | Insurance coverage for inventory |
| Handling cost | Moving and managing inventory |
| Obsolescence | Products becoming outdated or unusable |
| Damage and deterioration | Inventory losses due to damage |
| Capital cost | Opportunity cost of money tied up in inventory |

### Inventory Carrying Cost Formula

\[
Annual\ Carrying\ Cost =
Average\ Inventory\ Value \times Carrying\ Rate
\]

Where:

- Average Inventory Value = Average monetary value of inventory.
- Carrying Rate = Annual carrying cost percentage.
- Annual Carrying Cost = Annual cost of holding inventory.

Suppose:

- Average inventory value = ₱10,000,000
- Annual carrying rate = 20%

Calculation:

\[
10,000,000\times20\%
\]

\[
\boxed{₱2,000,000/year}
\]

Monthly equivalent, assuming an even annual cost:

\[
\frac{2,000,000}{12}
\]

\[
\boxed{₱166,666.67/month}
\]

**Interpretation:**

The company incurs an estimated annual carrying cost of ₱2 million to maintain ₱10 million in average inventory.

Important: The 20% is an illustrative assumption, not a universal industry standard. Companies use different carrying cost methodologies, and some components may already be recorded elsewhere in the financial statements. Avoid double counting them.

### Why is this important in S&OP?

Suppose the demand plan decreases, but the supply plan remains unchanged.

Result:

- More finished goods inventory.
- Higher inventory value.
- More working capital tied up.
- Higher carrying costs.
- Increased risk of obsolescence.

This is why demand and supply plans must remain aligned.

## 4.7 Working Capital

**Definition:** Working Capital represents the difference between current assets and current liabilities.

It indicates the company's short-term financial resources available to support its operations.

Formula:

\[
Working\ Capital =
Current\ Assets - Current\ Liabilities
\]

### Example

ABC Electronics has:

| Current assets | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | ₱2,000,000 |
| Accounts receivable | ₱3,000,000 |
| Inventory | ₱5,000,000 |
| **Total current assets** | **₱10,000,000** |

Current liabilities:

| Current liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts payable | ₱3,000,000 |
| Short-term loans | ₱1,000,000 |
| Other current liabilities | ₱1,000,000 |
| **Total current liabilities** | **₱5,000,000** |

Calculation:

\[
10,000,000-5,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Working\ Capital=₱5,000,000}
\]

### How does inventory affect working capital?

Let's assume the company purchases additional raw materials worth ₱2 million using cash.

Before purchase:

- Cash = ₱2 million
- Inventory = ₱5 million
- Current assets = ₱10 million
- Current liabilities = ₱5 million
- Working capital = ₱5 million

After purchase:

- Cash = ₱0
- Inventory = ₱7 million
- Current assets = ₱10 million
- Current liabilities = ₱5 million
- Working capital = ₱5 million

Notice something important:

**Working capital did not change, but its composition changed.**

The company converted cash into inventory.

This is why working capital alone cannot fully explain liquidity quality.

Inventory is a current asset, but it is not as immediately available as cash.

### Net Working Capital vs. Operating Working Capital

In S&OP, you may also encounter Operating Working Capital.

A common simplified definition is:

\[
OWC = Inventory + Accounts\ Receivable - Accounts\ Payable
\]

This focuses on funds tied up in normal operations.

Using our figures:

\[
OWC=5M+3M-3M
\]

\[
\boxed{OWC=₱5,000,000}
\]

The exact definition may vary by organization. Some companies exclude cash, financing items, taxes, or other balances.

## 4.8 Inventory Value

**Definition:** Inventory Value represents the monetary value of inventory held by the company at a particular point in time.

Ito ang financial value ng inventory, not simply the number of physical units.

### Physical Inventory vs. Financial Inventory

This distinction is extremely important.

| Measurement | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---:|
| Physical inventory | Quantity available | 10,000 units |
| Unit inventory cost | Cost per unit | ₱250 |
| Inventory value | Total monetary value | ₱2,500,000 |

Formula:

\[
Inventory\ Value =
Inventory\ Quantity \times Unit\ Cost
\]

Example:

\[
10,000\times₱250
\]

\[
\boxed{Inventory\ Value=₱2,500,000}
\]

### Why physical quantity is not enough

Consider two inventory categories:

| Product | Quantity | Unit cost | Inventory value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 | ₱100 | ₱500,000 |
| Product B | 5,000 | ₱500 | ₱2,500,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | | **₱3,000,000** |

Pareho silang 5,000 units.

Pero mas malaki ang financial investment sa Product B.

Therefore, inventory analysis should consider both:

- Physical inventory: How many units do we have?
- Financial inventory: How much money is tied up in those units?

### Inventory Valuation Methods

Companies may use different inventory valuation methods, depending on applicable accounting standards and policies.

- FIFO (First-In, First-Out): Assumes the earliest acquired inventory costs are assigned first to cost of sales.
- Weighted Average Cost: Uses a weighted average cost to value inventory and cost of sales.
- Specific Identification: Tracks the actual cost of individually identifiable inventory items.

Under IAS 2, inventory is generally measured at the lower of cost and net realizable value.

This means inventory value may need to be reduced if its expected selling value, less completion and selling costs, is lower than its recorded cost.

## 4.9 Budget Variance

**Definition:** Budget Variance measures the difference between actual financial results and the approved budget.

Formula:

\[
Budget\ Variance =
Actual - Budget
\]

Percentage variance:

\[
Variance\% =
\frac{Actual-Budget}{Budget}\times100
\]

### Example: Revenue

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Budgeted revenue | ₱5,500,000 |
| Actual revenue | ₱5,000,000 |

Calculation:

\[
5,000,000-5,500,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Variance=-₱500,000}
\]

Percentage:

\[
\frac{-500,000}{5,500,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{-9.09\%}
\]

Interpretation:

Revenue is 9.09% below budget.

**Unfavorable variance**

The company did not achieve its revenue target.

### Important: Favorable vs. Unfavorable

Hindi pare-pareho ang interpretation ng positive at negative variance.

For revenue:

- Actual higher than budget = Generally Favorable.
- Actual lower than budget = Generally Unfavorable.

For costs:

- Actual higher than budget = Generally Unfavorable.
- Actual lower than budget = Generally Favorable.

Example:

| KPI | Budget | Actual | Variance | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Revenue | ₱5.5M | ₱5.0M | -₱0.5M | Unfavorable |
| COGS | ₱3.4M | ₱3.2M | -₱0.2M | Favorable |
| Operating expenses | ₱1.1M | ₱1.0M | -₱0.1M | Favorable |

Notice that lower COGS is generally good, provided it did not result from underproduction, poor quality, or other damaging operational decisions.

**The context determines whether a variance is genuinely favorable.**

## 4.10 Forecast vs. Actual Financial Performance

Forecast vs. Actual compares the expected financial result against the realized result.

The difference between budget and forecast is important:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Budget | Approved financial target or plan |
| Forecast | Updated estimate of expected future performance |
| Actual | Financial result that has already occurred |

Example:

At the beginning of the month:

- Budgeted revenue = ₱5.5M.
- Latest forecast revenue = ₱5.2M.
- Actual revenue = ₱5.0M.

Comparison:

| Comparison | Result |
|---|---:|
| Actual vs. Budget | -₱500,000 |
| Actual vs. Forecast | -₱200,000 |

The company missed its budget by ₱500,000 and its latest forecast by ₱200,000.

### Why compare both?

Because they answer different questions.

**Actual vs. Budget:**

Did we achieve the financial target?

**Actual vs. Forecast:**

How accurate was our latest financial expectation?

For an S&OP analyst, this distinction matters because the budget may have been established months earlier, while the latest forecast should reflect updated demand and supply conditions.

Narito ang formulas na dapat mong maging comfortable gamitin.

| Financial KPI | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Units Sold × Selling Price |
| Revenue Growth | (Current Revenue − Previous Revenue) / Previous Revenue × 100 |
| Gross Profit | Revenue − COGS |
| Gross Margin | Gross Profit / Revenue × 100 |
| Operating Profit | Gross Profit − Operating Expenses |
| Operating Margin | Operating Profit / Revenue × 100 |
| COGS | Beginning FG + COGM − Ending FG |
| Production Cost per Unit | Total Manufacturing Cost / Units Produced |
| Inventory Carrying Cost | Average Inventory Value × Carrying Rate |
| Working Capital | Current Assets − Current Liabilities |
| Operating Working Capital | Inventory + AR − AP |
| Inventory Value | Quantity × Unit Cost |
| Budget Variance | Actual − Budget |
| Percentage Variance | (Actual − Budget) / Budget × 100 |

**Calculation reminders:**

1. Always use the same currency and reporting period.
2. Distinguish between units produced and units sold.
3. Use the appropriate inventory valuation basis.
4. Identify whether a cost is included in COGS or operating expenses.
5. Do not divide by zero when calculating percentage variances.
6. For carrying costs, ensure the average inventory and carrying rate refer to compatible periods.

# 6. Financial Impact of Operational Decisions

Dito na natin ikokonekta ang financial KPIs sa actual decisions ng S&OP.

## 6.1 Effects of Demand Changes on Revenue

Suppose customer demand increases from 10,000 to 12,000 units.

Selling price remains ₱500/unit.

| Description | Original | Increased Demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| Units sold | 10,000 | 12,000 |
| Selling price | ₱500 | ₱500 |
| Revenue | ₱5M | ₱6M |

Revenue increases by:

\[
6M-5M=₱1M
\]

This is a 20% increase.

But what if the additional demand requires discounts?

Suppose the additional 2,000 units are sold at ₱450 each.

New revenue:

\[
(10,000\times500)+(2,000\times450)
\]

\[
=5M+900,000
\]

\[
=₱5,900,000
\]

Instead of ₱6 million, revenue is ₱5.9 million.

This demonstrates that demand volume and selling price must be evaluated together.

## 6.2 Effects of Production Volume and Capacity Utilization

Suppose the company has a monthly practical production capacity of 15,000 units.

Actual production:

- Original: 12,000 units.
- Increased: 15,000 units.

Capacity utilization:

\[
Capacity\ Utilization =
\frac{Actual\ Production}{Available\ Capacity}\times100
\]

Original:

\[
\frac{12,000}{15,000}\times100=80\%
\]

Increased:

\[
\frac{15,000}{15,000}\times100=100\%
\]

Higher utilization may improve fixed cost absorption.

However, running at maximum capacity may also create:

- More overtime.
- Increased machine wear.
- Higher maintenance requirements.
- Reduced flexibility for urgent orders.
- Greater risk of breakdowns.

Therefore, maximum utilization does not automatically mean optimal financial performance.

## 6.3 Effects of Excess Inventory

Suppose the company produces 15,000 units but only sells 10,000.

Assuming the inventory is saleable and its cost is ₱250 per unit:

\[
Excess\ Production=15,000-10,000
\]

\[
=5,000\ units
\]

Financial inventory accumulation:

\[
5,000\times250
\]

\[
\boxed{₱1,250,000}
\]

The company has ₱1.25 million in additional inventory cost tied up in unsold products.

Possible consequences:

- Higher working capital requirement.
- Higher storage and handling costs.
- Higher risk of obsolescence.
- Increased potential inventory write-downs.
- Lower cash availability.

Important: The ₱1.25 million is not automatically an expense or loss. It is the inventory's assumed cost value while the products remain recognized as inventory, subject to valuation rules.

## 6.4 Effects of Insufficient Inventory

Now consider the opposite situation.

Customer demand is 12,000 units, but the company can supply only 10,000.

Potential unfulfilled demand:

\[
12,000-10,000=2,000\ units
\]

At ₱500 per unit:

\[
2,000\times500
\]

\[
\boxed{₱1,000,000}
\]

Potential revenue at risk is ₱1 million.

But be careful:

This is potential revenue, not necessarily an actual financial loss.

The actual impact depends on whether customers:

- Cancel their orders.
- Accept delayed delivery.
- Switch to competitors.
- Place backorders.
- Purchase alternative products.

This is why customer service performance and revenue performance must be analyzed together.

## 6.5 Procurement Costs and Expedited Shipments

Suppose a supplier delay threatens production.

The company decides to use expedited air freight instead of standard sea freight.

| Description | Standard shipping | Expedited shipping |
|---|---:|---:|
| Freight cost | ₱100,000 | ₱350,000 |
| Additional cost | | ₱250,000 |

The expedited shipment costs an additional ₱250,000.

But suppose it prevents the loss of 1,000 units of customer sales.

Potential revenue protected:

\[
1,000\times500=₱500,000
\]

Assume the normal COGS for these units is ₱320,000.

Contribution toward gross profit:

\[
500,000-320,000=₱180,000
\]

Compare:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Gross profit protected | ₱180,000 |
| Additional expedited freight | ₱250,000 |
| Net incremental impact | -₱70,000 |

Under these simplified assumptions, expediting costs ₱70,000 more than the gross profit protected.

However, the decision could still be justified if avoiding the shortage prevents additional penalties, customer loss, contractual consequences, or future lost business.

**S&OP lesson:** Evaluate the total financial impact, not just the additional cost or additional revenue.

# 7. Financial KPI Interpretation

## 7.1 Actual vs. Budget vs. Forecast

Let's put the three measurements together.

| Financial KPI | Budget | Forecast | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱5.5M | ₱5.2M | ₱5.0M |
| COGS | ₱3.4M | ₱3.3M | ₱3.2M |
| Gross Profit | ₱2.1M | ₱1.9M | ₱1.8M |
| Operating Expenses | ₱1.1M | ₱1.05M | ₱1.0M |
| Operating Profit | ₱1.0M | ₱850K | ₱800K |

Observations:

1. Revenue is below budget and forecast.
2. COGS is lower than both budget and forecast.
3. Gross profit is below budget and forecast.
4. Operating expenses are lower than budget.
5. Operating profit is still below target.

An inexperienced analyst might say:

"Good news, our COGS and operating expenses are lower than budget!"

But a professional analyst asks:

- Did costs decrease because of genuine efficiency?
- Did production decrease?
- Were fewer units sold?
- Did the product mix change?
- Did lower procurement costs contribute?
- Were maintenance activities postponed?
- Did the company delay necessary spending?

This is the difference between reporting a variance and analyzing a variance.

## 7.2 Why Improved Operational KPIs May Not Improve Financial Results

Consider these situations:

| Operational improvement | Possible financial consequence |
|---|---|
| Higher production attainment | Excess finished goods if demand is lower |
| Higher machine utilization | Increased maintenance and overtime costs |
| Higher inventory availability | Higher carrying cost |
| Better forecast accuracy | Does not guarantee profitable demand |
| Lower production cost per unit | May be caused by overproduction and fixed-cost absorption |
| Higher customer service level | May require costly expedited shipments |

A good analyst always asks:

**What is the financial consequence of this operational improvement?**

ABC Electronics Manufacturing produces electronic components for industrial customers.

The company is reviewing its monthly S&OP plan.

\boxed{50\%}

\[
2,500,000-1,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱1,500,000}

\frac{1,500,000}{5,000,000}\times100

\[
\boxed{30\%}

4,000\times250

\[
\boxed{₱1,000,000}

# 9. Business Perspective and S&OP Decision-Making

Ito ang pinaka-importanteng part ng topic: paano ginagamit ng S&OP analyst ang financial KPIs para makapag-recommend ng business decisions?

## 9.1 Connecting Operational KPIs to Financial Results

Let's connect the previous topics to financial performance.

| Operational situation | Operational KPI | Financial impact |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Forecasted demand | Potential revenue growth |
| Forecast is inaccurate | Forecast accuracy | Revenue and inventory risk |
| Production capacity is insufficient | Capacity utilization | Lost sales and potential overtime |
| Production cost increases | Production cost per unit | Lower gross margin |
| Supplier delivery is delayed | Supplier OTIF / lead time | Expediting costs and lost sales |
| Inventory increases | DOS / Inventory turnover | Higher working capital and carrying costs |
| Customer orders are delayed | OTIF / Service level | Potential lost revenue and customer penalties |

An S&OP analyst must understand these relationships.

For example:

A material shortage is not simply an inventory problem.

It can create a chain of financial consequences:

**Raw Material Shortage**  
↓  
**Production Disruption**  
↓  
**Insufficient Finished Goods**  
↓  
**Delayed Customer Deliveries**  
↓  
**Financial Consequences: Lost sales, expediting costs, penalties, reduced profitability**

The purpose of financial KPI analysis is to quantify the business consequences of operational problems.

## 9.2 How to Communicate Operational Issues Using Financial Impact

Compare these two statements.

**Operational-only communication:**

"Our production is below target because of insufficient raw materials."

This explains the problem but does not communicate its financial significance.

**Financially supported communication:**

"Raw material shortages are restricting production capacity by approximately 2,000 units. Based on the current selling price of ₱500 per unit, the potential revenue at risk is ₱1 million. We recommend prioritizing the supplier recovery plan and evaluating alternative material sources to protect customer deliveries."

The second statement communicates:

1. Operational issue.
2. Quantified impact.
3. Financial consequence.
4. Recommended action.

This is a valuable professional skill in S&OP.

## 9.3 The Four Financial Questions an S&OP Analyst Should Ask

When reviewing a proposed demand or supply plan, ask:

**1. Revenue: Are we generating enough sales?**

- Is forecasted demand increasing or decreasing?
- Can the company fulfill customer requirements?
- Are we losing sales because of supply constraints?

**2. Profitability: Are we earning enough from those sales?**

- Are material costs increasing?
- Is production cost per unit improving?
- Are discounts affecting gross margin?
- Are additional sales actually profitable?

**3. Inventory and Working Capital: How much money is tied up in inventory?**

- Are inventory levels aligned with demand?
- Are we accumulating excess stock?
- Is the inventory value increasing?
- Are we maintaining enough cash for operations?

**4. Financial Targets: Are we achieving the business plan?**

- Are actual results meeting budget?
- Is the latest forecast realistic?
- What are the major financial variances?
- What actions are necessary to recover performance?

## 9.4 Management's Overall Perspective

Management does not normally evaluate revenue, profit, inventory, and cash flow independently.

They want to understand the overall financial condition of the business.

For example:

A proposed production increase may:

- Improve customer service.
- Increase revenue.
- Improve fixed-cost absorption.
- Increase raw material requirements.
- Increase inventory value.
- Increase working capital requirements.

The final decision depends on whether the benefits outweigh the costs and risks.

**This is the essence of financial integration in S&OP: balancing growth, profitability, operational capability, and financial resources.**

# 10. Key Takeaways

Here are the concepts you should master before proceeding.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Value earned from selling products |
| COGS | Cost assigned to products sold |
| Gross Profit | Revenue minus COGS |
| Gross Margin | Gross profit as a percentage of revenue |
| Operating Profit | Gross profit minus operating expenses |
| Operating Margin | Operating profit as a percentage of revenue |
| Production Cost per Unit | Average cost to manufacture one unit |
| Carrying Cost | Cost of maintaining inventory over time |
| Working Capital | Current assets minus current liabilities |
| Inventory Value | Monetary value of inventory on hand |
| Budget Variance | Difference between actual and budget |
| Forecast vs. Actual | Difference between expected and realized results |
| Cash Flow | Actual cash movement into and out of the business |

### Five principles to remember

1. **Revenue is not profit.** Higher sales do not automatically mean higher profitability.

2. **Profit is not cash flow.** A company can report profits while experiencing cash shortages.

3. **Production volume is not the same as sales volume.** Producing more products does not guarantee that customers will buy them.

4. **Inventory is both an operational resource and a financial investment.** Physical quantities and monetary values must be analyzed together.

5. **Favorable variance does not always mean good performance.** Lower costs could result from efficiency, but they could also result from underproduction or deferred maintenance.
