# Module 9 – S&OP Performance Measurement and KPIs
## Topic 9.1: Introduction to Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

**Main learning objective:** Understand how organizations measure whether their S&OP plans are working, whether business objectives are being achieved, and whether management decisions are producing the desired results.

---

# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What are we going to discuss?

Throughout the previous modules, we learned how organizations develop and manage their S&OP plans.

We discussed how companies:

- Forecast customer demand.
- Evaluate production and supply capabilities.
- Manage inventory and material availability.
- Reconcile demand and supply.
- Evaluate financial implications.
- Conduct Pre-S&OP and Executive S&OP meetings.
- Translate approved plans into operational actions.

But there is one important question:

**How do we know if the S&OP process is actually working?**

For example:

Imagine that your company prepared an S&OP plan for November.

Management approved the following:

| Business area | November plan |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 10,000 units |
| Production | 10,000 units |
| Ending inventory | 2,000 units |
| Customer service level | 98% |
| Manufacturing cost | ₱5,000,000 |

At the end of November, the actual results were:

| Business area | Plan | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Customer demand | 10,000 | 12,000 |
| Production | 10,000 | 10,500 |
| Ending inventory | 2,000 | 500 |
| Customer service level | 98% | 91% |
| Manufacturing cost | ₱5,000,000 | ₱5,800,000 |

Question:

Did the company achieve its objectives?

Production exceeded its original plan, but inventory decreased significantly, customer service deteriorated, and manufacturing costs increased.

If management only looks at production output, the company might appear successful.

However, when we examine the other business results, we discover that the overall performance was not satisfactory.

This is where KPIs become important.

## 1.2 Why is this topic important?

KPIs allow organizations to:

1. Measure business performance objectively.
2. Identify gaps between plans and actual results.
3. Detect problems before they become bigger.
4. Evaluate whether management decisions are effective.
5. Improve future demand, supply, inventory, and financial plans.
6. Align different departments toward common business objectives.

## 1.3 Connection to Module 8

In Module 8, we discussed the S&OP process, including its meetings, outputs, implementation, and execution.

Think of the relationship this way:

| Module 8 | Module 9 |
|---|---|
| Develop the demand plan | Measure demand planning performance |
| Develop the supply plan | Measure supply performance |
| Reconcile demand and supply | Measure planning effectiveness |
| Approve the S&OP plan | Establish performance expectations |
| Execute approved decisions | Monitor actual results |
| Resolve operational issues | Identify performance gaps |

**Module 8 teaches us how to develop and execute the plan. Module 9 teaches us how to measure whether the plan is delivering results.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 What exactly is a KPI?

Let's use a simple example.

Imagine you are managing a manufacturing production line.

Your production objective is to manufacture 1,000 units every day.

At the end of the shift, you check the production report:

- Target production: 1,000 units
- Actual production: 900 units

You immediately know that production is 100 units below target.

But what does that tell you?

It tells you that your production performance did not meet expectations.

Now imagine that you also monitor:

- Number of defective products.
- Machine downtime.
- Production efficiency.
- Material consumption.
- Manufacturing cost.

These indicators help you understand not only whether production achieved its target, but also WHY it achieved or failed to achieve it.

That's the basic idea behind KPIs.

**A KPI is a measurement used to determine how effectively an organization, department, or process is achieving an important business objective.**

## 2.2 Everyday analogy: Your personal fitness tracker

Imagine your goal is to improve your physical fitness.

You monitor:

| Measurement | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Body weight | Your current weight |
| Daily steps | How much you move |
| Running distance | How far you run |
| Resting heart rate | An indication of cardiovascular fitness |
| Weekly running frequency | Consistency of training |

Not every measurement is necessarily a KPI.

For example:

If your main objective is improving cardiovascular endurance, weekly running distance and running performance may be important KPIs.

Your body weight might still be useful, but it may not be the most relevant indicator of that particular objective.

**The important lesson: Not everything that can be measured deserves to become a KPI.**

A KPI should tell you something important about the achievement of a specific objective.

## 2.3 Applying this to S&OP

Let's say a manufacturing company wants to improve customer satisfaction.

Its management identifies three important indicators:

1. Forecast accuracy: Are we predicting customer demand correctly?
2. Production schedule adherence: Are we producing according to the approved schedule?
3. On-Time Delivery (OTD): Are customers receiving their orders on time?

These indicators provide different perspectives on the same business objective.

Forecasting accurately does not automatically guarantee on-time delivery.

Production schedule adherence does not guarantee that materials are available.

And producing enough products does not guarantee that the correct products will be delivered to customers.

This is why organizations need multiple interconnected KPIs.

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 What is a Key Performance Indicator?

A **Key Performance Indicator (KPI)** is a quantifiable measure used to evaluate the performance of an organization, business unit, process, or individual against defined strategic or operational objectives.

Let's break down the terminology:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Key | Important to achieving a business objective |
| Performance | How effectively something is operating |
| Indicator | A measurable signal that provides information about performance |

For example:

A manufacturing company wants to improve inventory efficiency.

It selects Inventory Turnover as one of its KPIs.

Why?

Because inventory turnover helps management evaluate how efficiently the company converts inventory into sales or consumption.

The KPI provides information that supports inventory-related decisions.

## 3.2 Difference between Measurement, Metric, KPI, Target, Actual, and Variance

This is one of the most important foundations of this lesson.

These terms are related, but they are NOT interchangeable.

Let's use one manufacturing example throughout.

A company wants to improve production output.

Its expected production is 1,000 units per day.

Actual production is 900 units.

### Understanding the six related concepts

**1. Measurement**

The act or result of determining a value.

Example: Counting how many units were produced during the shift.

It is the process of obtaining information.

**2. Metric**

A quantifiable measure used to describe or monitor something.

Example: Daily production output = 900 units.

It tells us what happened or what condition exists.

**3. KPI**

A metric selected because it is important to a business objective.

Example: Daily production output is selected as a KPI because the company wants to improve production capacity utilization and output.

It tells us whether an important objective is being achieved.

**4. Target**

The expected or desired performance level.

Example: 1,000 units/day.

It tells us what we want to achieve.

**5. Actual**

The performance level actually achieved.

Example: 900 units/day.

It tells us what really happened.

**6. Variance**

The difference between actual performance and target performance.

Example: 900 - 1,000 = -100 units.

It tells us how far actual performance is from our target.

| Concept | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Measurement | How did we obtain the information? |
| Metric | What are we measuring? |
| KPI | What important performance are we monitoring? |
| Target | What should we achieve? |
| Actual | What did we achieve? |
| Variance | How far are we from our target? |

**Important distinction:** A KPI is not automatically a target. A KPI measures performance, while a target establishes the desired performance level.

## 3.3 Leading vs. Lagging Indicators

Another important KPI classification is based on WHEN the indicator provides information.

There are two major types:

### A. Leading Indicators

Leading indicators provide information about activities, conditions, or factors that may influence future performance.

In simple terms:

They help us anticipate what might happen.

Examples:

| Leading indicator | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| Supplier delivery reliability | Potential material availability |
| Production capacity utilization | Remaining production flexibility |
| Open purchase orders | Future incoming materials |
| Forecast bias | Potential systematic forecasting problems |
| Preventive maintenance compliance | Potential equipment reliability |

Example:

A company depends on a supplier for an important raw material.

The supplier's delivery performance begins deteriorating.

Even if production is still running normally today, the declining supplier performance may indicate future material shortages.

This is a leading indicator because it provides an early warning.

### B. Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators measure results that have already happened.

In simple terms:

They tell us what already happened.

Examples:

| Lagging indicator | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| Actual sales | Sales already generated |
| Customer complaints | Problems already experienced |
| Actual production output | Products already manufactured |
| Inventory write-offs | Inventory losses already incurred |
| On-Time Delivery | Delivery performance already achieved |

Example:

The supplier failed to deliver raw materials on time.

Production stopped, and customer orders were delayed.

The resulting customer delivery performance is a lagging indicator of the problem.

### Leading vs. Lagging Comparison

| Leading | Lagging |
|---|---|
| Forward-looking | Backward-looking |
| Helps anticipate problems | Confirms actual results |
| Supports preventive action | Supports corrective action |
| Examples: supplier risk, capacity utilization | Examples: actual production, OTD |

**Professional insight:** Effective performance management requires BOTH.

If a company only monitors lagging indicators, it may discover problems after the damage has already occurred.

If it only monitors leading indicators, it may fail to determine whether its actions are actually producing results.

# 4. Characteristics of Effective KPIs

Not all KPIs are useful.

A company can have 100 indicators and still have poor performance management if those indicators do not support meaningful decisions.

Let's discuss how organizations select effective KPIs.

## 4.1 SMART Objectives

Before selecting KPIs, organizations should establish clear objectives.

One commonly used framework is SMART.

| Element | Meaning | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| S | Specific | Clearly defines what must be achieved |
| M | Measurable | Can be evaluated using measurable information |
| A | Achievable | Realistic considering available resources |
| R | Relevant | Connected to business priorities |
| T | Time-bound | Has a defined period for achievement |

Example:

**Poor objective**

"Improve inventory performance."

**SMART objective**

"Reduce average finished goods inventory from ₱20 million to ₱17 million within six months while maintaining customer service level at or above 97%."

Why is the second objective better?

Because it establishes:

- What needs improvement: Finished goods inventory.
- Current condition: ₱20 million.
- Desired condition: ₱17 million.
- Time frame: Six months.
- Business constraint: Maintain customer service at 97% or higher.

This prevents management from reducing inventory at the expense of customer satisfaction.

## 4.2 Characteristics of an Effective KPI

Aside from SMART objectives, a good KPI should possess the following characteristics.

| Characteristic | Meaning | Manufacturing example |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Supports business objectives | OTD for customer satisfaction |
| Accuracy | Reflects actual performance | Correct shipment records |
| Consistency | Uses the same calculation method | Same forecast accuracy formula every month |
| Timeliness | Available when decisions are needed | Weekly production reports |
| Clarity | Easily understood by stakeholders | Percentage instead of ambiguous labels |
| Actionability | Helps identify possible actions | Production schedule adherence |

Let's examine some of these more closely.

**Relevance**

Suppose management wants to improve customer service.

It monitors employee attendance as its primary KPI.

Although employee attendance may affect operations, it does not directly establish whether customers are receiving their orders on time.

OTD would be a more directly relevant KPI.

**Accuracy**

Imagine two departments calculating forecast accuracy differently.

- Department A uses absolute percentage error.
- Department B uses a different calculation and does not account for demand volume in the same way.

Their reported results cannot be fairly compared without understanding the calculation differences.

**Consistency**

If a company changes its KPI calculation every month, it becomes difficult to determine whether performance is genuinely improving.

**Timeliness**

A monthly report may be sufficient for strategic inventory analysis, but it may be too slow for daily production monitoring.

The reporting frequency must match the decision being supported.

## 4.3 Common Mistakes When Selecting KPIs

| Mistake | Why it is problematic |
|---|---|
| Measuring everything | Creates unnecessary reporting workload |
| Selecting irrelevant KPIs | Does not support business objectives |
| Too many KPIs | Management loses focus |
| Unclear formulas | Different departments produce inconsistent results |
| Unrealistic targets | Encourages frustration or manipulation |
| Ignoring data quality | Decisions are based on unreliable information |
| Focusing on one KPI only | Can create negative consequences elsewhere |

One important concept here is **KPI conflict**.

For example:

Production management is evaluated primarily on production volume.

To achieve its target, the department manufactures more products than customer demand requires.

Production output increases, but excess inventory also increases.

The production KPI looks good, but the overall business performance becomes worse.

This brings us to an important S&OP principle:

**KPIs must encourage decisions that optimize the overall business, not merely the performance of individual departments.**

# 5. KPI Categories in S&OP

S&OP involves several interconnected business functions.

Therefore, performance measurement must cover more than production or sales.

Let's examine the six major KPI categories.

## 5.1 Demand Planning KPIs

These measure how effectively the organization predicts and understands customer demand.

| KPI | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Forecast Accuracy | Measures how close forecasts are to actual demand |
| Forecast Bias | Identifies systematic overforecasting or underforecasting |
| Forecast Value Added (FVA) | Evaluates whether forecasting adjustments improve the forecast |
| Demand Plan Attainment | Evaluates actual demand against the demand plan |

Example:

The demand planning team forecasts 10,000 units.

Actual demand is 12,000 units.

The company needs to understand whether this difference is caused by:

- Unexpected customer orders.
- Market growth.
- Poor forecasting assumptions.
- Promotional activities.
- Incorrect historical data.

These KPIs will be discussed in greater detail in Topic 9.2.

## 5.2 Supply and Production KPIs

These measure the ability of the organization to manufacture and supply the required products.

| KPI | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Production Plan Attainment | Measures actual production against planned production |
| Schedule Adherence | Measures compliance with the production schedule |
| Capacity Utilization | Measures how much available capacity is being used |
| Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) | Evaluates equipment availability, performance, and quality |
| Supplier On-Time Delivery | Measures supplier delivery reliability |

Example:

Demand requires 10,000 units.

The approved supply plan calls for 10,000 units.

Actual production is only 8,500 units.

Management needs to investigate:

- Equipment downtime.
- Material shortages.
- Labor availability.
- Production yield.
- Capacity constraints.

## 5.3 Inventory KPIs

These evaluate how efficiently the company manages its inventory.

| KPI | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Inventory Turnover | Measures how frequently inventory is sold or consumed |
| Days of Supply (DOS) | Estimates how long available inventory can support demand |
| Inventory Accuracy | Measures agreement between recorded and actual inventory |
| Excess and Obsolete Inventory | Identifies inventory that is excessive or no longer useful |
| Safety Stock Compliance | Monitors whether inventory buffers meet defined requirements |

Example:

The company has ₱20 million worth of inventory.

However, only ₱12 million is required to support expected demand.

The remaining ₱8 million may represent excess inventory, depending on the company's inventory policies and future requirements.

This creates opportunities to improve working capital and reduce holding costs.

## 5.4 Customer Service KPIs

These evaluate how effectively the company fulfills customer requirements.

| KPI | Purpose |
|---|---|
| On-Time Delivery (OTD) | Measures whether deliveries are made on time |
| On-Time In-Full (OTIF) | Measures whether orders arrive on time and complete |
| Fill Rate | Measures the proportion of demand fulfilled from available supply |
| Backorder Rate | Measures demand that cannot be fulfilled immediately |
| Order Cycle Time | Measures the time required to fulfill customer orders |

Example:

A customer orders 1,000 units.

The company delivers 900 units on the requested date.

The delivery was on time, but the order was incomplete.

Depending on the company's OTD definition, it may count as on-time, but it would not qualify as On-Time In-Full.

This is why KPI definitions matter.

## 5.5 Financial KPIs

These connect operational performance to financial results.

| KPI | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Revenue Attainment | Measures actual revenue against the revenue plan |
| Gross Margin | Evaluates profitability after cost of goods sold |
| Manufacturing Cost | Measures production-related expenditure |
| Inventory Carrying Cost | Measures the cost of holding inventory |
| Working Capital | Evaluates funds tied up in business operations |

Example:

The company increases production to satisfy demand.

However, overtime, expedited freight, and additional material purchases increase operating costs.

Sales may improve, but profitability may deteriorate.

Financial KPIs help management understand the economic consequences of operational decisions.

## 5.6 Process and Organizational KPIs

These evaluate how effectively the S&OP process itself operates.

| KPI | Purpose |
|---|---|
| S&OP Plan Adherence | Measures compliance with the approved S&OP plan |
| Meeting Attendance | Monitors participation of required stakeholders |
| Action Closure Rate | Measures completion of assigned actions |
| Decision Lead Time | Measures how quickly important decisions are made |
| Data Availability | Monitors availability of required planning information |

Example:

During the Pre-S&OP meeting, management identifies a raw material shortage.

The procurement manager is assigned to secure additional supply within two weeks.

The action closure rate helps determine whether assigned responsibilities are being completed.

However, remember that meeting attendance alone does not establish whether an S&OP process is effective.

The quality of decisions and business outcomes matters more.

# 6. Understanding KPI Components

Now that we understand the different categories, let's examine how an actual KPI is documented and reported.

A properly defined KPI should contain enough information for different departments to interpret and calculate it consistently.

## 6.1 The Anatomy of a KPI

Let's use Production Plan Attainment.

### Production Plan Attainment

**1. Definition**

Measures the percentage of planned production volume that was actually achieved.

**2. Business purpose**

Evaluates whether production can deliver the quantity committed in the approved production plan.

**3. Formula**

\[
\text{Plan Attainment}=
\frac{\text{Actual Production}}{\text{Planned Production}}
\times100
\]

**4. Unit of measurement**

Percentage (%)

**5. Performance information**

| Component | Value |
|---|---:|
| Target | 100% |
| Actual | 90% |
| Variance | -10 percentage points |

**6. Reporting frequency**

Daily operational reporting and monthly S&OP review.

**7. Data source**

MES, production reports, and approved production schedules.

**8. KPI owner**

Production Planning or Manufacturing, depending on the company's accountability structure.

## 6.2 KPI Target, Actual, and Variance

Let's calculate the example.

Given:

- Planned production = 1,000 units
- Actual production = 900 units

**Step 1: Calculate actual performance.**

\[
\frac{900}{1,000}\times100=90\%
\]

**Step 2: Compare actual performance with the target.**

\[
Variance=Actual-Target
\]

\[
Variance=90\%-100\%=-10\text{ pp}
\]

pp = percentage points, which is different from percent change.

**Performance result**

| Component | Result |
|---|---:|
| Actual Production Plan Attainment | 90% |
| Target | 100% |
| Variance | -10 pp |
| Status | Below target |

Interpretation:

The manufacturing team produced only 90% of the planned quantity.

It failed to achieve the production commitment by 100 units.

But the KPI itself does not explain WHY the shortfall happened.

We still need to investigate the underlying causes.

## 6.3 KPI Ownership and Accountability

Every KPI should have an assigned owner.

Why?

Because if nobody is responsible for monitoring a KPI, identifying its problems, and initiating corrective actions, the indicator becomes nothing more than a number in a report.

Consider this structure:

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| KPI owner | Accountable for overall KPI performance |
| Data owner | Ensures data availability and quality |
| KPI analyst | Calculates, validates, and analyzes results |
| Management | Reviews performance and makes decisions |
| Action owner | Implements corrective or improvement actions |

One person may hold multiple responsibilities depending on the organization.

For example, an S&OP analyst may calculate forecast accuracy, while the demand planning manager owns the overall demand planning performance.

**Important:** KPI ownership does not necessarily mean the owner personally controls every factor affecting the KPI.

For example, customer service may depend on demand planning, production, procurement, logistics, and inventory management.

Accountability must therefore be supported by cross-functional collaboration.

# 7. KPI Targets and Performance Evaluation

A KPI becomes useful when the organization establishes expectations and evaluates actual performance against them.

## 7.1 How Organizations Establish KPI Targets

Targets should not simply be selected because a number looks good.

Organizations generally consider several factors:

**A. Historical Performance**

Suppose production plan attainment during the last six months was:

| Month | Attainment |
|---|---:|
| May | 91% |
| June | 92% |
| July | 93% |
| August | 94% |
| September | 92% |
| October | 93% |

The average performance is:

\[
\frac{91+92+93+94+92+93}{6}=92.5\%
\]

Management might use this historical performance as a reference when establishing a future target.

**B. Business Objectives**

If the company wants to improve customer service, it may need to establish higher production reliability and delivery performance targets.

**C. Operational Capabilities**

Targets must consider:

- Available production capacity.
- Labor availability.
- Equipment reliability.
- Supplier capabilities.
- Material lead times.

**D. Industry Benchmarks**

Organizations may compare their performance with industry peers or established standards.

However, benchmark values should be relevant to the company's product, process, industry, and operating environment.

There is no universal KPI target suitable for every manufacturing company.

**E. Financial Requirements**

Targets should also support financial objectives such as profitability, working capital, and cost control.

## 7.2 Understanding Variance Analysis

Variance analysis is the process of evaluating the difference between actual performance and planned or expected performance.

For a simple higher-is-better KPI:

\[
Variance=Actual-Target
\]

Example:

| KPI | Target | Actual | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Production attainment | 100% | 95% | -5 pp |
| Forecast accuracy | 90% | 85% | -5 pp |
| OTD | 98% | 94% | -4 pp |
| Inventory accuracy | 99% | 99.5% | +0.5 pp |

However, be careful.

Not every KPI follows the same favorable direction.

For example:

- Higher production attainment is generally favorable.
- Higher OTD is generally favorable.
- Lower manufacturing cost is generally favorable.
- Lower excess inventory is generally favorable.

Therefore, the sign of the variance alone does not automatically tell us whether performance is good or bad.

For a lower-is-better KPI, a positive variance may actually indicate unfavorable performance.

## 7.3 Performance Thresholds and Status

Organizations often use thresholds to classify performance.

An illustrative example:

| Performance | Status |
|---|---|
| At or above 100% of target | On target |
| 95% to below 100% | Warning |
| Below 95% | Critical |

These are only sample thresholds, not universal industry standards.

For KPIs where lower is better, the threshold logic must be reversed or otherwise specifically defined.

For example:

| Manufacturing cost variance | Status |
|---|---|
| At or below budget | On target |
| Above budget by up to 5% | Warning |
| Above budget by more than 5% | Critical |

Thresholds help management quickly identify which performance areas need attention.

## 7.4 Why Achieving One KPI Can Damage Another

This is one of the most important concepts in S&OP performance management.

Let's examine a manufacturing example.

The production department wants to achieve 100% production plan attainment.

However, customer demand suddenly decreases.

Instead of adjusting the production plan, the department continues producing at its original volume.

Result:

| KPI | Result |
|---|---|
| Production plan attainment | Improved |
| Inventory level | Increased |
| Inventory carrying cost | Increased |
| Working capital | Increased |
| Inventory obsolescence risk | Increased |

The production KPI improved, but the overall business became less efficient.

This is called **suboptimization**.

Suboptimization happens when an individual department optimizes its own performance at the expense of the overall organization.

Another example:

Procurement negotiates lower material prices by purchasing large quantities.

Result:

- Purchase price variance improves.
- Raw material inventory increases.
- Storage costs increase.
- Working capital increases.
- Risk of material obsolescence increases.

This is why S&OP requires cross-functional performance measurement.

**The goal is not to maximize every individual KPI. The goal is to achieve the best overall business outcome while managing trade-offs.**

# 8. Practical Manufacturing Example

Let's put everything together.

Imagine a fictional manufacturing company called ABC Manufacturing.

It produces industrial electronic components and supplies different customers.

Management wants to achieve five major business objectives for November.

## 8.1 Business Objectives

| Business area | Objective |
|---|---|
| Demand | Improve demand forecasting |
| Production | Meet planned production requirements |
| Inventory | Reduce excess inventory |
| Customer service | Improve delivery reliability |
| Finance | Control manufacturing costs |

Management selects KPIs that directly support these objectives.

## 8.2 Selected KPI Scorecard

The following figures are illustrative assumptions.

| Category | KPI | Target | Actual |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Demand | Forecast Accuracy | 90% | 84% |
| Supply | Production Plan Attainment | 100% | 96% |
| Inventory | Inventory Turnover | 6 times | 4.5 times |
| Customer | OTD | 98% | 93% |
| Financial | Manufacturing Cost | ₱5M | ₱5.6M |

Let's examine what each result tells management.

**Demand: Forecast Accuracy = 84%**

The demand forecast is less accurate than expected.

Potential implications:

- Incorrect production requirements.
- Poor material planning.
- Excess inventory or shortages.
- Unnecessary capacity adjustments.

**Supply: Production Plan Attainment = 96%**

Production achieved most, but not all, of the approved production plan.

Potential causes:

- Machine downtime.
- Material shortages.
- Unplanned production changes.
- Labor constraints.

**Inventory: Inventory Turnover = 4.5 times**

The company is turning over inventory less frequently than its target of six times.

Potential implications:

- Excess inventory.
- Slow-moving products.
- Higher inventory carrying costs.
- Reduced working capital efficiency.

**Customer Service: OTD = 93%**

Only 93% of the relevant deliveries were completed on time, based on the company's defined OTD methodology.

Potential causes:

- Production delays.
- Insufficient inventory.
- Incorrect demand forecasts.
- Supplier delivery issues.
- Logistics constraints.

**Financial: Manufacturing Cost = ₱5.6 million**

Actual manufacturing cost exceeded the ₱5 million target.

Possible causes:

- Higher material consumption.
- Overtime.
- Lower production efficiency.
- Equipment downtime.
- Unplanned manufacturing expenses.

## 8.3 Why Multiple KPIs Are Necessary

Let's imagine management only monitors production plan attainment.

**Single-KPI perspective**

96% production plan attainment.

"Production is performing relatively well."

But when management examines the complete scorecard:

**Overall business perspective**

| KPI | Actual | Performance |
|---|---:|---|
| Forecast Accuracy | 84% | Below target |
| Production Attainment | 96% | Below target |
| Inventory Turnover | 4.5x | Below target |
| On-Time Delivery | 93% | Below target |
| Manufacturing Cost | ₱5.6M | Above budget |

Multiple business objectives are not being achieved.

Now management can see that the business has interconnected problems.

For example:

1. Demand forecasting is inaccurate.
2. The supply plan may not reflect actual customer requirements.
3. Inventory may not be positioned correctly.
4. Production may be unable to satisfy customer requirements.
5. Delivery performance deteriorates.
6. Manufacturing costs increase.

This is precisely why KPIs are essential to S&OP.

They connect different departments through a common performance measurement framework.

## 8.4 KPI Relationship and Business Impact

The following is a simplified illustration, not a strictly linear relationship. In actual S&OP, these areas influence one another continuously.

**Demand Planning**
- Forecast Accuracy and Forecast Bias

↓

**Supply and Production Planning**
- Production Attainment and Schedule Adherence

↓

**Inventory Management**
- Inventory Turnover and Days of Supply

↓

**Customer Service**
- OTD and OTIF

↓

**Financial Performance**
- Revenue, Gross Margin, and Working Capital

For example, financial constraints can affect procurement, while inventory shortages can affect production and customer service.

# 9. Business Perspective and Decision-Making

Now let's approach this from the perspective of an S&OP Analyst.

Your responsibility is not simply to calculate KPIs and prepare monthly reports.

You must understand what the numbers mean and help management determine what actions are necessary.

## 9.1 How an S&OP Analyst Uses KPIs

A typical KPI analysis follows this sequence:

1. **Collect performance data.** Gather actual demand, production, inventory, customer service, and financial data.
2. **Calculate KPIs.** Apply approved KPI definitions and formulas.
3. **Compare actual against target.** Identify which indicators are performing above or below expectations.
4. **Analyze variances.** Determine the magnitude, trend, and potential reasons behind performance gaps.
5. **Identify root causes.** Investigate whether problems originate from demand, supply, materials, capacity, or other factors.
6. **Develop recommendations.** Propose corrective actions or alternative business scenarios.
7. **Monitor action effectiveness.** Evaluate whether implemented actions improve future KPI results.

## 9.2 Example: Identifying a Business Problem

Suppose your monthly S&OP dashboard shows:

| KPI | Target | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Forecast Accuracy | 90% | 82% |
| Production Attainment | 100% | 97% |
| Inventory Turnover | 6x | 4x |
| OTD | 98% | 90% |

As the S&OP analyst, you should not immediately recommend increasing production.

Why?

Because production attainment is already relatively high, but customer delivery performance is poor.

You need to investigate further.

Possible questions:

- Is the forecast inaccurate because of unexpected demand changes?
- Are we producing the wrong product mix?
- Are raw materials available when needed?
- Is inventory concentrated in products that customers are not ordering?
- Are production constraints affecting high-demand products?
- Are customer orders being delayed during logistics execution?

Notice that the problem might not be insufficient total inventory.

It might be an inventory mix problem.

For example:

| Product | Inventory | Actual demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 | 1,000 |
| Product B | 500 | 3,000 |

The company has 5,500 units of inventory, but Product B has a shortage relative to demand.

Increasing total inventory without correcting the product mix could make the situation worse.

This is where KPI analysis becomes valuable.

It helps you move from simply reporting numbers to understanding business problems.

## 9.3 KPIs and Continuous Improvement

KPIs are also connected to continuous improvement methodologies such as the PDCA cycle.

PDCA means:

| Stage | Meaning | KPI application |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Establish objectives and actions | Set OTD target at 98% |
| Do | Implement the action | Improve production scheduling |
| Check | Evaluate results | Actual OTD reaches 94% |
| Act | Standardize or adjust | Investigate remaining delivery gaps |

The cycle continues until the desired performance is achieved and sustained.

**Remember:** A KPI is not the final output of performance management. It is an input to better decisions and continuous improvement.

# 10. Key Takeaways

Before proceeding, make sure you understand these fundamental concepts.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| KPI | Measures performance against an important objective |
| Metric | A quantifiable measure |
| Measurement | The process or result of obtaining a value |
| Target | Desired performance |
| Actual | Achieved performance |
| Variance | Difference between actual and target |
| Leading indicator | Helps anticipate future performance |
| Lagging indicator | Measures results that already happened |
| SMART | Framework for establishing clear objectives |
| KPI owner | Person or function accountable for monitoring performance |
| Suboptimization | Improving one department's results at the expense of the overall business |

### Five fundamental principles

1. Not every metric is a KPI.
2. Every KPI should support a meaningful business objective.
3. KPI definitions and formulas must be consistent.
4. Multiple KPIs are necessary to understand overall business performance.
5. KPIs should lead to investigation, decisions, and improvement, not merely reporting.
