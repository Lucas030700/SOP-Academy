# Module 3 – Demand Planning
## Topic 3.6: Consensus Demand Planning

**Main learning objective:** Understand how different departments work together to develop one agreed, realistic, and evidence-based demand plan that the entire organization can use for planning and decision-making.

---

# 1. Topic Overview

### 1.1 What is Consensus Demand Planning?

Imagine that a manufacturing company produces electronic components.

The Demand Planning team analyzes historical sales and generates a statistical forecast of **10,000 units for next month**.

However, other departments have additional information:

- Sales knows that a major customer might increase its orders.
- Marketing plans to launch a promotional campaign.
- Customer Service knows that one customer has postponed its orders.
- Product Management knows that a new product variant will be introduced.
- Finance questions whether the expected revenue is realistic.
- Supply Planning needs to know how much production capacity and raw materials will be required.

Now, here is the problem:

Should the company simply use the statistical forecast of 10,000 units?

Should it follow Sales' higher forecast?

Should Marketing's promotional estimate automatically be added?

Or should the company consider all available information before deciding?

This is where **Consensus Demand Planning** comes in.

**Consensus Demand Planning** is the process of combining statistical forecasts, business intelligence, customer information, and cross-functional inputs to develop one agreed demand plan.

In simpler terms:

> Instead of allowing one department to decide how much the company expects to sell, all relevant departments contribute their information and agree on one realistic demand outlook.

### 1.2 Why is this important?

Because demand planning affects almost every major business activity.

| Business function | Why the demand plan matters |
|---|---|
| Sales | Establishes expected customer demand |
| Production | Determines how much to manufacture |
| Procurement | Determines raw material requirements |
| Inventory | Helps prevent excess or insufficient stocks |
| Finance | Supports revenue and financial planning |
| Management | Provides a basis for business decisions |

For example:

If the company expects 15,000 units but actual demand is only 9,000 units, it might experience:

- Excess finished goods inventory.
- Unnecessary raw material purchases.
- Underutilized production capacity.
- Higher inventory carrying costs.
- Potential obsolescence.

On the other hand, if the company plans for only 9,000 units when actual demand reaches 15,000 units, it might experience:

- Stockouts.
- Delayed customer deliveries.
- Lost sales.
- Expedited procurement costs.
- Lower customer satisfaction.

The objective is not necessarily to produce the highest forecast or the lowest forecast.

**The objective is to develop the most reasonable expectation of future demand based on available evidence.**

### 1.3 Connection to previous topics

In the previous topics of Module 3, we discussed how demand patterns are understood and how quantitative forecasting methods generate forecasts.

We can now connect those concepts:

| Previous learning | Contribution to Topic 3.6 |
|---|---|
| Historical demand analysis | Provides the foundation for forecasting |
| Demand patterns | Helps explain changes in demand |
| Quantitative forecasting | Generates the statistical baseline |
| Demand influencing factors | Helps identify reasons for future demand changes |
| Consensus demand planning | Combines these insights into one agreed demand plan |

Think of the statistical forecast as the starting point. Consensus demand planning adds the business context that historical data alone cannot fully capture.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

### 2.1 Imagine planning a family vacation

Suppose your family wants to plan a vacation next month.

You initially estimate that the trip will cost ₱20,000 based on previous vacations.

But other family members have additional information:

- Your father knows that transportation costs have increased.
- Your mother knows that accommodation prices are higher during holidays.
- Your sibling wants to include another destination.
- You know that one family member might not be available on the planned dates.

Would it make sense to finalize the budget using only the original ₱20,000 estimate?

Of course not.

You need to collect everyone's information, evaluate the changes, and agree on a realistic budget.

That is essentially how consensus demand planning works.

The statistical forecast is your original ₱20,000 estimate.

The additional information from family members represents the inputs from different departments.

The final agreed budget represents the consensus demand plan.

### 2.2 Three important concepts you must distinguish

This is probably the most important foundation of today's lesson.

#### 1. Statistical Forecast

**What does the data predict?**

Ito ang forecast generated using historical demand and statistical forecasting methods.

Example: 10,000 units.

#### 2. Judgmental Adjustments

**What additional information should change the forecast?**

Ito ang adjustments based on new business information, customer developments, market intelligence, and professional judgment.

Example: +2,000 units from a confirmed customer demand increase.

#### 3. Consensus Demand Plan

**What demand figure has the organization agreed to plan for?**

Ito ang resulting demand plan after evaluating the statistical forecast and justified adjustments.

Example: 11,500 units.

These three concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

**Remember this distinction:**

- Statistical forecast = Data-based starting point.
- Judgmental adjustments = Evidence-based changes to the starting point.
- Consensus demand plan = Agreed final demand outlook.

### 2.3 Why can't Sales decide everything?

Sales is close to customers, so it has valuable information about customer requirements.

However, Sales also has sales targets and revenue commitments.

For example:

A Sales Manager might want the company to achieve 15,000 units next month because that would help meet the department's sales target.

But the actual customer demand might only support 11,000 units.

If the company automatically accepts the 15,000-unit forecast, it could produce 4,000 unnecessary units.

Likewise, Demand Planning cannot independently decide everything because statistical models cannot automatically understand every upcoming promotion, customer contract, market disruption, or product launch.

**Consensus planning exists because no single department has complete visibility of future demand.**

# 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the professional concepts used in actual S&OP environments.

### 3.1 Formal definition

**Consensus Demand Planning** is a cross-functional planning process in which an organization integrates statistical forecasts, customer demand information, market intelligence, commercial assumptions, and relevant business inputs to establish a unified and agreed demand plan.

There are three important principles:

1. **Data-driven:** The statistical forecast provides an objective starting point.
2. **Cross-functional:** Different departments contribute information based on their areas of expertise.
3. **Evidence-based:** Changes to the forecast should have identifiable business reasons and supporting information.

### 3.2 Important terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Baseline Forecast | Initial statistical forecast before business adjustments |
| Statistical Forecast | Forecast generated through quantitative methods |
| Judgmental Forecast | Forecast based on human expertise and business knowledge |
| Forecast Adjustment | Addition or reduction applied to the baseline |
| Demand Assumption | Expected business condition that influences demand |
| Demand Consensus | Agreement among relevant stakeholders on expected demand |
| Demand Review | Meeting where demand assumptions, changes, and risks are evaluated |
| Forecast Override | Replacing or modifying the statistical forecast using judgment |
| Forecast Bias | Consistent tendency to overforecast or underforecast |
| Demand Signal | Information indicating a possible change in future demand |
| Exception Management | Focusing attention on significant forecast changes, risks, and unusual situations |

One distinction worth remembering:

**A forecast override is not automatically wrong.** Sometimes the statistical forecast is outdated because new information has become available.

However, an override without sufficient evidence can introduce bias and reduce forecast quality.

### 3.3 Statistical forecast versus consensus demand plan

Let's compare them more technically.

| Dimension | Statistical Forecast | Consensus Demand Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Primary source | Historical demand data | Statistical forecast plus business inputs |
| Method | Quantitative models | Quantitative and qualitative |
| Main owner | Demand Planning | Cross-functional planning team |
| Customer information | May not fully capture future changes | Incorporates available customer intelligence |
| Promotions | May be absent from historical patterns | Can incorporate planned promotions |
| New products | Limited historical data | Uses launch assumptions and market inputs |
| Business assumptions | Limited | Explicitly evaluated |
| Purpose | Establish baseline expectation | Establish agreed demand outlook |

**Important:** Consensus demand planning should not mean changing a statistically generated forecast just to satisfy everyone.

The goal is not departmental compromise for the sake of agreement. The goal is a better demand estimate.

### 3.4 Consensus does not necessarily mean unanimous agreement

In actual business environments, departments may disagree.

For example:

- Sales expects 14,000 units.
- Demand Planning estimates 11,000 units.
- Finance supports 12,000 units.

The organization still needs one demand plan.

The team evaluates the evidence, discusses the differences, and follows its agreed decision-making and escalation process.

A final plan can be approved even when some participants still disagree with particular assumptions.

This is an important distinction:

**Consensus means an agreed planning outcome, not necessarily that everybody has identical opinions.**

# 4. Detailed Discussion

## 4.1 Departments and Their Contributions

Let's examine the responsibilities of each department.

### A. Demand Planning

**Forecast integration and coordination**

Demand Planning is usually responsible for developing and maintaining the demand forecast.

Its responsibilities include:

- Generating the statistical baseline forecast.
- Analyzing historical demand.
- Monitoring forecast accuracy and bias.
- Consolidating inputs from different departments.
- Evaluating proposed forecast adjustments.
- Preparing demand review reports.
- Maintaining the agreed demand plan.

**Example:**

Historical demand suggests that next month's demand should be 10,000 units.

Demand Planning identifies this as the baseline forecast and asks Sales and Marketing whether any known business events could change the expected demand.

Its role is not simply to accept departmental estimates. It must evaluate whether the information justifies changing the forecast.

### B. Sales

**Customer intelligence**

Sales provides information directly related to customer requirements and commercial activities.

Typical contributions:

- Customer forecasts.
- Expected purchase orders.
- Contract changes.
- New customer opportunities.
- Customer order cancellations.
- Changes in customer purchasing behavior.
- Major account developments.

**Example:**

A major customer informs Sales that it expects to increase its monthly purchases from 2,000 to 3,000 units.

Sales communicates this information to Demand Planning.

However, Demand Planning should still determine whether the additional 1,000 units are:

- Confirmed orders.
- An updated customer forecast.
- A preliminary verbal estimate.
- An opportunity that has not yet materialized.

These are not equivalent levels of certainty.

### C. Marketing

**Market activities and promotions**

Marketing contributes information about activities that could increase or decrease demand.

Typical inputs:

- Promotional campaigns.
- Advertising activities.
- Discounts.
- Seasonal campaigns.
- Product launches.
- Market expansion.
- Competitor activities.

**Example:**

Marketing plans a promotional campaign that could generate an additional 800 units.

However, the company should not automatically add 800 units.

It should evaluate:

- How many customers will be reached?
- What is the expected conversion rate?
- How many incremental purchases are expected?
- Is the promotion expected to generate genuinely additional demand?
- Will customers simply purchase earlier than usual?

The last point is particularly important because a promotion can shift demand between periods rather than create entirely new demand.

### D. Product Management

**Product lifecycle and product strategy**

Product Management provides information about product-related changes.

Typical inputs:

- New product introductions.
- Product discontinuations.
- Product substitutions.
- Product upgrades.
- Changes in product specifications.
- Product lifecycle information.
- Expected product cannibalization.

**Product cannibalization** occurs when a new product takes demand away from an existing product within the same company's portfolio.

Example:

A company launches a new electronic component.

It expects 1,000 units of new-product demand, but 300 units are expected to replace purchases of an existing product.

The actual incremental demand across the entire portfolio is only 700 units, assuming those are the only affected products.

### E. Customer Service

**Customer order and service information**

Customer Service provides information about actual customer transactions and service-related changes.

Typical contributions:

- Open customer orders.
- Order cancellations.
- Delivery rescheduling.
- Backorders.
- Customer complaints.
- Changes in requested delivery dates.
- Customer communication and service issues.

**Example:**

A customer has an existing order of 1,500 units but requests delivery to be moved from November to December.

Customer Service identifies the rescheduling.

Demand Planning must consider the impact on the timing of demand.

The order should not be counted as an additional 1,500 units if it is already included in the existing demand plan.

### F. Finance

**Financial validation**

Finance evaluates the financial implications of the demand plan.

Typical contributions:

- Revenue expectations.
- Selling price assumptions.
- Revenue targets.
- Product mix.
- Gross margin implications.
- Budget alignment.
- Financial risks.

**Example:**

Sales forecasts 12,000 units at ₱100 per unit.

Expected revenue:

\[
12,000 \times ₱100 = ₱1,200,000
\]

Finance identifies that a planned discount will reduce the average selling price to ₱95.

Revised expected revenue:

\[
12,000 \times ₱95 = ₱1,140,000
\]

The volume forecast remains unchanged, but the financial outlook changes.

This illustrates an important concept:

**Demand volume and financial value are related, but they are not the same planning measure.**

Finance should challenge financial assumptions, but revenue targets should not be used to artificially inflate expected customer demand.

### G. Supply Planning

**Supply feasibility and constraints**

Supply Planning evaluates whether the company can fulfill the expected demand.

Typical contributions:

- Production capacity.
- Available inventory.
- Raw material availability.
- Supplier lead times.
- Manufacturing constraints.
- Planned maintenance.
- Production bottlenecks.

**Example:**

Consensus demand is 12,000 units.

However, Supply Planning identifies that available capacity is only 10,500 units.

This creates a supply-demand gap of:

\[
12,000-10,500=1,500\text{ units}
\]

Supply Planning should identify possible solutions, such as overtime, additional shifts, subcontracting, or inventory utilization.

**Important distinction:** Supply constraints do not automatically reduce the demand forecast.

The demand plan represents what customers are expected to need. The supply plan determines how the company intends to meet that demand.

This distinction is fundamental to S&OP.

### Summary of Departmental Contributions

| Department | Main contribution |
|---|---|
| Demand Planning | Statistical forecast and forecast integration |
| Sales | Customer demand and commercial intelligence |
| Marketing | Promotions and market activities |
| Product Management | Product lifecycle and launch information |
| Customer Service | Order changes and customer service information |
| Finance | Revenue, price, and financial assumptions |
| Supply Planning | Capacity, materials, and supply feasibility |

## 4.2 Developing a Consensus Demand Plan

Let's walk through the complete process.

1. **Generate the statistical baseline forecast.** Demand Planning analyzes historical demand and generates the initial forecast.
2. **Gather customer and market information.** Collect customer forecasts, purchase orders, promotions, market developments, and other demand signals.
3. **Collect departmental inputs.** Each department submits its relevant information and assumptions.
4. **Identify forecast changes and exceptions.** Compare departmental estimates against the baseline. Identify significant increases, decreases, and unusual changes.
5. **Evaluate proposed adjustments.** Assess evidence, timing, probability, historical performance, and potential overlap.
6. **Resolve conflicting assumptions.** Discuss disagreements and determine which assumptions are supported by the strongest evidence.
7. **Agree on the consensus demand plan.** Establish the agreed demand outlook for the planning horizon.
8. **Document decisions and communicate the final plan.** Record assumptions, adjustments, owners, risks, and communicate the agreed figures to relevant stakeholders.

Let's examine some important details.

### Step 1: Generate the Statistical Baseline Forecast

Historical demand is analyzed using an appropriate forecasting method.

For example:

| Month | Actual demand |
|---|---:|
| July | 9,500 |
| August | 10,200 |
| September | 9,800 |
| October | 10,100 |

Based on the selected forecasting methodology, the baseline forecast for November is 10,000 units.

This is the starting point.

### Step 2: Gather Customer and Market Information

The company collects new information that might not yet be reflected in historical demand.

Examples:

- Customer A expects higher purchases.
- Customer B delays an order.
- Marketing schedules a promotion.
- A competitor experiences a supply disruption.
- A new product is scheduled for launch.

The objective is to understand what has changed and why.

### Step 3: Collect Departmental Inputs

Each department submits its information using an agreed planning format.

A useful submission should contain:

| Required information | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Proposed adjustment | What change is being requested? |
| Quantity | How many additional or fewer units? |
| Period | When will the change occur? |
| Reason | Why is the change necessary? |
| Supporting evidence | What information supports it? |
| Owner | Who is accountable? |
| Confidence | How reliable is the assumption? |

### Step 4: Identify Forecast Changes and Exceptions

Not every adjustment requires a lengthy discussion.

Demand Planning can focus on significant changes.

For example:

- Baseline forecast: 10,000 units.
- Sales proposal: +2,000 units.
- Marketing proposal: +1,500 units.

These proposed changes create a large deviation from the baseline.

The team should investigate the reasons rather than automatically accepting them.

### Step 5: Evaluate Proposed Adjustments

This is where analytical thinking becomes important.

Ask:

1. Is the information new?
2. Is it supported by evidence?
3. Is the demand incremental?
4. Is the timing correct?
5. Is the demand already included in the baseline?
6. Does another department's adjustment cover the same demand?

This prevents unnecessary forecast inflation.

### Step 6: Resolve Conflicting Assumptions

Consider this situation:

| Department | Expected demand |
|---|---:|
| Demand Planning | 10,000 |
| Sales | 13,000 |
| Marketing | 12,500 |
| Finance | 11,500 |

The company should not simply calculate the average.

Why?

Because each estimate may have a different basis.

Sales might be using an unconfirmed customer opportunity, while Finance might be using a conservative revenue assumption.

The correct approach is to investigate the differences, not blindly average the figures.

### Step 7: Agree on the Consensus Demand Plan

After evaluating the information, the team agrees on the final expected demand.

This figure becomes the common demand planning reference for downstream activities.

### Step 8: Document and Communicate

The final plan should include:

- Agreed forecast by product and period.
- Approved adjustments.
- Supporting assumptions.
- Risks and opportunities.
- Adjustment owners.
- Unresolved issues and escalation requirements.
- Version and approval status.

A consensus plan without documentation is difficult to audit, explain, and improve.

## 4.3 Forecast Adjustments and Collaboration

A forecast adjustment modifies the statistical baseline to reflect relevant business information.

There are two basic directions.

- **Positive adjustment:** Expected demand increases. Example: +1,000 units from additional confirmed customer demand.
- **Negative adjustment:** Expected demand decreases. Example: -800 units from a customer cancellation or delayed purchase.

### Types of adjustments

| Adjustment type | Example |
|---|---|
| Customer-specific | Major customer increases its forecast by 1,000 units |
| Promotional | Campaign expected to generate 500 incremental units |
| New product | New product launch expected to generate 800 units |
| Discontinuation | Existing product demand expected to decline by 400 units |
| Market-driven | Market demand expected to decline by 5% |
| Order-related | Customer postpones 600 units to the following month |

### Statistical versus judgmental forecasting

Statistical forecasting uses mathematical relationships in historical data.

Judgmental forecasting uses information that may not be represented in historical data.

Neither should automatically replace the other.

The statistical model gives consistency and objectivity.

Business judgment adds information about future events.

The strongest demand plan uses both appropriately.

### The danger of optimistic assumptions

Suppose Sales repeatedly requests additional forecast quantities because it wants to meet its annual sales target.

If these increases are not supported by actual customer demand, the organization may develop a systematic upward forecast bias.

This can result in:

- Excess inventory.
- Unnecessary procurement.
- Increased storage requirements.
- Higher working capital.
- Poor forecast accuracy.

### Avoiding double-counting

This is one of the most important technical controls.

Suppose:

- Baseline forecast already includes 1,000 units of expected demand from Customer A.
- Sales receives an updated customer forecast of 1,300 units.
- Sales proposes adding 1,300 units to the baseline.

Incorrect:

\[
1,000+1,300=2,300
\]

Correct:

\[
1,300-1,000=300
\]

The adjustment should be only +300 units, assuming the revised customer forecast fully replaces the original 1,000-unit expectation.

**Key principle:** Add the incremental demand, not the entire updated demand figure.

Also, customer orders and customer forecasts must be reconciled carefully. An order that replaces an existing forecast should not be counted as additional demand.

## 4.4 Demand Review Meeting

### What is a Demand Review?

A Demand Review is a structured cross-functional meeting where stakeholders evaluate the demand forecast, discuss changes, challenge assumptions, and agree on the demand plan.

It is commonly part of the broader S&OP cycle.

The Demand Review is not simply a meeting where departments present their individual numbers.

It is a decision-making session.

### Typical participants

| Participant | Meeting responsibility |
|---|---|
| Demand Planning | Presents baseline and proposed demand plan |
| Sales | Explains customer demand changes |
| Marketing | Explains promotional assumptions |
| Product Management | Explains launches and product lifecycle |
| Customer Service | Explains order changes |
| Finance | Validates financial assumptions |
| Supply Planning | Identifies supply implications |
| S&OP Manager / Facilitator | Guides discussion and resolves escalations |

Depending on company structure, some participants may be combined or additional stakeholders may attend.

### Information required before the meeting

A well-prepared Demand Review normally includes:

1. Historical actual demand.
2. Statistical baseline forecast.
3. Current consensus forecast.
4. Forecast accuracy and bias.
5. Significant forecast adjustments.
6. Customer forecast changes.
7. Promotion and product launch information.
8. Risks and opportunities.
9. Demand versus financial outlook.
10. Unresolved assumptions and decisions required.

### Important questions during the meeting

**Regarding the statistical forecast:**

- What is the baseline forecast?
- Is the forecast behaving consistently with historical demand?
- Are there unusual changes or outliers?

**Regarding Sales:**

- Which customer is driving the increase?
- Is the customer forecast confirmed?
- Is there an actual purchase order?
- What is the expected timing?

**Regarding Marketing:**

- Is the promotion expected to create incremental demand?
- What is the estimated conversion rate?
- Are there historical campaign results?

**Regarding Finance:**

- Are the selling price and revenue assumptions realistic?
- Is the volume consistent with the financial outlook?
- What are the financial risks?

**Regarding Supply Planning:**

- Can the expected demand be fulfilled?
- Are there material or capacity constraints?
- What happens if demand is higher than expected?

### How disagreements are resolved

| Situation | Recommended approach |
|---|---|
| Sales estimate is higher than baseline | Request customer evidence and assess probability |
| Marketing estimates large promotion impact | Review campaign assumptions and historical performance |
| Finance challenges revenue | Validate selling price, product mix, and volume |
| Customer postpones orders | Reconcile timing and existing demand |
| Supply capacity is insufficient | Escalate the supply-demand gap without hiding demand |
| Departments cannot agree | Document alternatives and escalate to the designated decision-maker |

### Expected meeting outputs

A Demand Review should produce tangible results.

- Agreed demand forecast.
- Approved forecast adjustments.
- Documented assumptions.
- Identified demand risks and opportunities.
- Assigned action items.
- Escalation items for management.
- Updated demand plan for the next S&OP stage.

The resulting demand plan becomes an input to Supply Review and the broader S&OP process.

# 5. Practical Examples and Applications

## 5.1 Manufacturing Business Scenario

Let's use an illustrative electronic components manufacturer.

The company manufactures electronic components supplied to different industrial customers.

The planning period is November.

The initial statistical forecast is:

**November statistical baseline: 10,000 units**

Different departments provide the following information.

### Step 1: Sales input

Sales identifies a major customer that expects to increase its purchases.

- Existing expected demand: 2,000 units.
- Revised customer forecast: 3,000 units.

Incremental demand:

\[
3,000-2,000=+1,000
\]

Sales proposes a +1,000-unit adjustment.

Demand Planning verifies that the updated customer forecast is valid and is not already incorporated into the baseline.

**Accepted: +1,000 units**

### Step 2: Marketing input

Marketing plans a promotional campaign.

Its initial estimate is an additional 1,000 units.

However, the team reviews the historical performance of similar campaigns and determines that only 600 incremental units are reasonably supported.

The remaining 400 units are considered too optimistic.

**Accepted: +600 units**

### Step 3: Customer Service input

Customer Service identifies a customer that postponed 500 units from November to December.

The 500 units were already included in the November demand plan.

Therefore:

\[
500-500=-500
\]

The November demand plan decreases by 500 units.

These units are shifted to December, assuming the customer still intends to purchase them.

**Accepted: -500 units**

### Step 4: Product Management input

Product Management reports that a new component variant will generate 400 units of demand.

However, 200 units will replace demand for an existing variant.

For the total product family:

\[
400-200=+200
\]

The net incremental demand is +200 units.

**Accepted: +200 units**

### Step 5: Finance review

Finance evaluates the expected revenue.

The initial financial assumption was based on a selling price of ₱100 per unit.

However, the company expects a promotional discount that reduces the average selling price to ₱96.

Finance does not recommend reducing the demand volume simply because the selling price is lower.

Instead, it updates the revenue assumption.

### Step 6: Final reconciliation

Now we consolidate the accepted adjustments.

| Input | Adjustment |
|---|---:|
| Statistical baseline | 10,000 |
| Sales: customer increase | +1,000 |
| Marketing: promotion | +600 |
| Customer Service: postponement | -500 |
| Product Management: net new product demand | +200 |
| Finance: price adjustment | 0 units |
| **Consensus demand plan** | **11,300** |

Calculation:

\[
10,000+1,000+600-500+200
\]

\[
\boxed{11,300\text{ units}}
\]

**Final agreed November demand: 11,300 units**

- Baseline forecast: 10,000
- Net adjustments: +1,300
- Demand increase: 13%

### Step 7: Financial interpretation

Revised expected revenue:

\[
11,300\times ₱96=₱1,084,800
\]

Compare this against the original baseline revenue:

\[
10,000\times ₱100=₱1,000,000
\]

The demand volume increased by 13%, while expected revenue increased by 8.48%.

Why?

Because the average selling price decreased from ₱100 to ₱96.

This demonstrates why volume planning and financial planning must be connected.

### Step 8: Supply Planning implications

Supply Planning now receives the agreed demand of 11,300 units.

Suppose available production capacity is only 10,500 units.

The supply gap is:

\[
11,300-10,500=800\text{ units}
\]

The company must evaluate possible actions:

- Add overtime.
- Use available finished goods inventory.
- Increase production shifts.
- Reallocate capacity from another product.
- Negotiate delivery schedules with customers.

Notice that the demand plan remains 11,300 units.

The 10,500-unit capacity does not change the customer's expected demand.

It creates a separate supply problem that must be resolved.

### What did we accomplish?

We started with 10,000 units.

Different departments provided new information.

Some adjustments increased demand, some decreased demand, and some changed only the financial assumptions.

After evaluation, the organization agreed on 11,300 units.

That is consensus demand planning in action.

## 5.2 Correct versus Incorrect Approaches

| Situation | Incorrect approach | Correct approach |
|---|---|---|
| Sales expects more demand | Accept the entire increase | Validate customer evidence |
| Marketing launches promotion | Automatically add projected sales | Estimate incremental demand |
| Customer revises forecast | Add the full revised quantity | Calculate the difference from existing demand |
| New product launch | Add all new-product volume | Consider cannibalization |
| Finance lowers price | Reduce demand volume automatically | Recalculate financial value separately |
| Supply capacity is insufficient | Reduce demand forecast to available capacity | Maintain demand and resolve supply gap |

The common principle is that every adjustment must have a clear business explanation.

# 6. Formulas and Calculations

Consensus demand planning does not have one universal mathematical formula. It is primarily a structured decision-making process.

However, several calculations are useful for evaluating and consolidating adjustments.

## 6.1 Consensus Demand Calculation

A simplified formula is:

\[
CDP=BF+\sum A_i
\]

Where:

- \(CDP\) = Consensus Demand Plan (units).
- \(BF\) = Baseline Forecast (units).
- \(A_i\) = Individual accepted forecast adjustments (units).

Using our example:

\[
CDP=10,000+1,000+600-500+200
\]

\[
CDP=11,300\text{ units}
\]

Remember: Only accepted, non-duplicated adjustments should be included.

## 6.2 Forecast Adjustment Percentage

This measures how much the consensus demand plan differs from the baseline.

\[
Adjustment\%=
\frac{CDP-BF}{BF}\times100
\]

Substitute:

\[
=\frac{11,300-10,000}{10,000}\times100
\]

\[
=\boxed{13\%}
\]

Interpretation:

The consensus demand plan is 13% higher than the statistical baseline.

This does not automatically mean the forecast is better. Actual demand must eventually be used to evaluate forecast performance.

## 6.3 Revenue Calculation

\[
Revenue=Demand\ Volume\times Average\ Selling\ Price
\]

\[
Revenue=11,300\times ₱96
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱1,084,800}
\]

Use this to understand the financial implications of the demand plan.

## 6.4 Weighted Demand Estimation

Sometimes departments provide estimates with different confidence levels.

A weighted estimate can be used as an analytical illustration, although it is not a universal consensus-planning rule.

Suppose the team evaluates three independent estimates:

| Source | Estimate | Illustrative weight |
|---|---:|---:|
| Statistical model | 10,000 | 50% |
| Validated customer outlook | 12,000 | 30% |
| Market intelligence | 11,000 | 20% |

Formula:

\[
Weighted\ Estimate=\sum(F_i\times W_i)
\]

Calculation:

\[
(10,000\times0.50)
+(12,000\times0.30)
+(11,000\times0.20)
\]

\[
=5,000+3,600+2,200
\]

\[
=\boxed{10,800\text{ units}}
\]

However, be careful.

You should only use weighted estimates when the inputs are genuinely comparable and sufficiently independent.

If the customer outlook and market intelligence already influence the statistical forecast, combining them again can double-count the same demand signal.

In many S&OP environments, a transparent adjustment-based reconciliation is more practical than simply averaging departmental forecasts.

## 6.5 Forecast Accuracy and Bias

Once actual demand becomes available, the organization can evaluate the quality of its consensus demand plan.

For example:

- Consensus demand forecast: 11,300 units.
- Actual demand: 11,000 units.

Forecast Error:

\[
Forecast\ Error=Forecast-Actual
\]

\[
=11,300-11,000=300
\]

The forecast was 300 units higher than actual demand.

Absolute Percentage Error:

\[
APE=\frac{|Forecast-Actual|}{Actual}\times100
\]

\[
=\frac{300}{11,000}\times100
\]

\[
=\boxed{2.73\%}
\]

This is useful for evaluating forecast performance.

A commonly used bias calculation is:

\[
Bias\%=\frac{\sum(Forecast-Actual)}{\sum Actual}\times100
\]

Positive bias indicates overforecasting under this sign convention, while negative bias indicates underforecasting.

Note that companies may use different sign conventions and other forecast accuracy metrics.

**Important:** A good consensus demand plan is not necessarily the one that matches the statistical forecast. It is the one that best represents expected demand based on information available at the time of planning.

# 7. Business Perspective and Decision-Making

Now let's move from understanding the process to understanding how an S&OP Analyst actually uses it.

This is particularly important because your role is not necessarily to make every commercial decision. Your responsibility is to help the organization make better decisions using reliable information.

## 7.1 Role of an S&OP Analyst

An S&OP Analyst acts as an information integrator, analytical support, and planning facilitator.

Let's examine the responsibilities.

### A. Facilitate cross-functional discussions

You help ensure that departments provide their inputs on time and that discussions focus on relevant demand changes.

For example:

Sales proposes increasing demand by 2,000 units.

Instead of immediately accepting or rejecting the proposal, you ask:

- Which customer will generate the additional demand?
- Is there an updated customer forecast?
- What is the expected delivery period?
- Is this already reflected in the baseline?

Your job is to make sure the adjustment is properly understood.

### B. Challenge unsupported assumptions

You should not automatically accept numbers simply because they come from department managers.

Consider:

| Department | Proposed adjustment | Supporting evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Sales | +2,000 | Verbal customer discussion |
| Marketing | +1,000 | Approved promotional campaign |
| Customer Service | -500 | Confirmed order postponement |

The adjustments have different levels of supporting evidence.

You should distinguish confirmed information from preliminary assumptions.

A useful principle:

**Challenge the assumption, not the person presenting it.**

### C. Consolidate different demand inputs

You organize the information into one consolidated view.

For example:

| Product | Baseline | Adjustments | Consensus |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 | +800 | 5,800 |
| Product B | 3,000 | -200 | 2,800 |
| Product C | 2,000 | +700 | 2,700 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **+1,300** | **11,300** |

This allows management to understand not only the final demand but also where the changes originated.

### D. Identify risks and opportunities

An S&OP Analyst should look beyond the final number.

For example:

| Observation | Potential implication |
|---|---|
| Customer demand increased significantly | Potential capacity shortage |
| Promotion has uncertain conversion | Forecast overestimation risk |
| Customer postponed orders | Inventory accumulation |
| New product launch | Uncertain demand profile |
| Supplier lead time increased | Potential inability to fulfill demand |

You should communicate these observations before they become operational problems.

### E. Document decisions

Maintain an adjustment log.

Here is an example:

| Adjustment | Quantity | Reason | Owner | Status |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Customer A increase | +1,000 | Revised customer forecast | Sales | Approved |
| Promotion | +600 | Historical campaign performance | Marketing | Approved |
| Order postponement | -500 | Customer delivery rescheduling | Customer Service | Approved |
| New product | +200 | Net of cannibalization | Product Management | Approved |

This creates accountability.

If the forecast later becomes inaccurate, the organization can investigate which assumptions were incorrect.

### F. Present the demand plan to management

Management generally does not need to see every individual data point.

They need to understand:

1. What is the expected demand?
2. How does it compare with the previous forecast?
3. What caused the changes?
4. What are the major risks?
5. What decisions are required?
6. What are the potential business consequences?

For example:

> November consensus demand increased from 10,000 to 11,300 units, representing a 13% increase against the statistical baseline. The increase is primarily driven by revised customer demand and an approved promotional campaign, partially offset by customer order postponement. Current production capacity is 10,500 units, creating an 800-unit supply gap requiring management review.

That is the type of communication an S&OP Analyst should develop.

## 7.2 Relationship Between Consensus Demand and Other S&OP Functions

Consensus Demand Planning is not the final destination of the S&OP process.

It provides the demand-side expectation that other functions must evaluate.

**Consensus Demand Plan: 11,300 units** (expected customer demand)

This plan informs:

- **Supply Planning:** Available capacity of 10,500 units, creating a potential supply gap.
- **Finance:** Expected revenue of ₱1.085 million.
- **Management / Executive S&OP:** Reconciliation of demand, supply, inventory, and financial objectives to establish the business plan.

This is why consensus demand planning is an essential bridge between forecasting and operational decision-making.

## 7.3 Three Learning Levels: What You Should Be Able to Do

| Learning level | Expected capability |
|---|---|
| Level 1: Understanding | Explain consensus demand planning and identify departmental responsibilities |
| Level 2: Technical Application | Calculate adjustments, consolidate forecasts, detect double-counting, and interpret changes |
| Level 3: Professional Analysis | Challenge assumptions, resolve conflicting inputs, identify business risks, and recommend actions |

For your development as an S&OP Analyst, the transition from Level 2 to Level 3 is particularly important.

Being able to calculate 11,300 units is useful.

Being able to explain why 11,300 is more reasonable than 13,000 is a much more valuable professional skill.

# 8. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate the most important lessons.

### 8.1 Three concepts you must never confuse

| Concept | Remember |
|---|---|
| Statistical Forecast | What historical data and the forecasting model indicate |
| Judgmental Adjustments | What additional business information justifies changing the forecast |
| Consensus Demand Plan | The final agreed demand outlook |

### 8.2 Important principles

1. No single department has complete visibility of future demand.
2. Statistical forecasts provide the objective starting point.
3. Sales, Marketing, Product Management, Customer Service, and Finance contribute different information.
4. Supply Planning evaluates the feasibility of fulfilling demand.
5. Forecast adjustments must have clear reasons and supporting evidence.
6. Customer forecasts and purchase orders must be reconciled to prevent double-counting.
7. Sales targets should not automatically become demand forecasts.
8. Demand Review meetings should produce decisions, not just presentations.
9. Consensus does not require unanimous agreement.
10. Demand should remain distinct from supply capacity.

### 8.3 The most important formula

\[
\boxed{CDP=Baseline+\text{Accepted Adjustments}}
\]

But remember: the quality of the consensus plan depends more on the quality of its assumptions than on the arithmetic itself.

**My recommended mental model:**

- Statistical forecasting tells you where demand is likely to go based on data.
- Business intelligence tells you what might change.
- Cross-functional collaboration validates those changes.
- Consensus demand planning converts the information into one agreed business outlook.
