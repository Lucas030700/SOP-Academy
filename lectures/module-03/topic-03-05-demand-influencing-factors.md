**Learning Progression:** Level 1 – Understanding → Level 2 – Technical Application → Level 3 – Professional Analysis

**Main Learning Objective:** Understand kung bakit nagbabago ang customer demand, anong business and market factors ang nakakaapekto rito, at paano ginagamit ng S&OP Analyst ang information na ito para makagawa ng mas realistic at reliable na demand forecast.

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

## 1.1 What are Demand Influencing Factors?

Imagine na gumagawa ang isang electronic components manufacturer ng MLCC (Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors).

For the past six months, consistent ang monthly customer demand:

| Month | Customer Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 100,000 units |
| February | 102,000 units |
| March | 101,000 units |
| April | 103,000 units |
| May | 102,000 units |
| June | 104,000 units |

Based on historical data, mukhang relatively stable ang demand.

Pero pagdating ng July, biglang naging 150,000 units ang customer requirement.

**Question: Bakit biglang tumaas ang demand?**

Possible reasons:

- Nag-increase ng production ang major customer.
- Nagkaroon ng bagong customer project.
- Nag-launch ang company ng promotional activity.
- Nagkaroon ng shortage ang competitor.
- Nagkaroon ng unexpected market opportunity.

Ang mga pangyayaring ito ang tinatawag nating **Demand Influencing Factors**.

**Technical Definition:**

Demand Influencing Factors are internal or external conditions, events, decisions, and market developments that affect the quantity, timing, composition, or direction of customer demand for products or services.

Sa mas simpleng salita:

Ito ang mga dahilan kung bakit bumibili ang customers ng mas marami, mas kaunti, mas maaga, o mas huling panahon kaysa sa normal nilang purchasing behavior.

## 1.2 Why is this topic important?

Sa Demand Planning, hindi sapat na malaman lang natin kung ano ang binili ng customer historically.

Kailangan din nating malaman kung ano ang posibleng mangyari sa future.

For example:

- Historical demand: 100,000 units/month.
- New customer project: Additional 30,000 units/month.
- Expected future demand: 130,000 units/month.

Kung historical data lang ang gagamitin natin, baka 100,000 units pa rin ang forecast natin.

Resulta? Potential shortage, delayed deliveries, insufficient raw materials, at lost sales.

On the other hand, kung mag-assume tayo ng additional 30,000 units without validating the customer project, baka mag-overproduce naman tayo.

Therefore, demand influencing factors help companies understand the **business reasons behind the numbers**.

## 1.3 Connection to previous topics

Sa previous topics ng Module 3, napag-aralan natin ang historical demand, demand patterns, at quantitative forecasting methods.

The connection:

| Previous Learning | Connection to Topic 3.5 |
|---|---|
| Historical Demand | Provides the actual demand data. |
| Demand Patterns | Helps identify trends, seasonality, and variations. |
| Quantitative Forecasting | Calculates expected future demand using historical data. |
| Demand Influencing Factors | Explains business events that may change the expected future demand. |

Think of it this way:

**Statistical forecasting tells us what may happen based on historical patterns. Demand influencing factors help explain what else may happen because of changing business conditions.**

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

## 2.1 Why does customer demand change over time?

Let's use a simple example.

Imagine may sari-sari store ka.

Normally, nakakabenta ka ng 50 bottles of soft drinks every day.

Pero may iba't ibang circumstances na puwedeng magpabago sa sales mo:

| Situation | Possible Effect |
|---|---|
| Mainit ang panahon | Increased sales |
| May basketball tournament nearby | Increased sales |
| Nagtaas ng presyo | Potentially decreased sales |
| May bagong competitor | Potentially decreased sales |
| May promotional discount | Increased sales |
| Umuulan nang malakas | Potentially decreased sales |

Notice something?

Hindi nagbabago ang demand dahil lang sa random numbers. May mga underlying reasons kung bakit nagbabago ang customer behavior.

Ganoon din sa manufacturing.

Ang demand ng electronic components ay maaaring maapektuhan ng production requirements ng customers, product launches, competitor activities, economic conditions, at maraming iba pang factors.

## 2.2 Normal Demand Variation vs. Event-Driven Demand Change

Very important ang distinction na ito.

Hindi lahat ng pagbabago sa demand ay nangangahulugan na may bagong business event.

### A. Normal Demand Variation

Ito ang ordinary fluctuations na nangyayari kahit walang major change sa business environment.

Example:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 100,000 |
| February | 102,000 |
| March | 99,000 |
| April | 101,000 |
| May | 103,000 |

May fluctuations, pero relatively stable pa rin ang demand.

Possible causes:
- Normal customer ordering variations.
- Small changes in purchasing schedules.
- Minor operational fluctuations.
- Random variations.

### B. Event-Driven Demand Change

Ito naman ang pagbabago na may identifiable business reason.

Example:

| Month | Demand | Explanation |
|---|---:|---|
| January | 100,000 | Normal |
| February | 102,000 | Normal |
| March | 101,000 | Normal |
| April | 150,000 | New customer project |
| May | 152,000 | Project continues |
| June | 151,000 | Project continues |

Hindi na ordinary fluctuation ang nangyari.

May specific event na nagbago sa demand level.

## 2.3 Why historical data alone is insufficient

Imagine na may bagong customer na mag-o-open ng manufacturing facility next month.

Historically, zero ang demand natin from that customer.

Kung historical data lang ang gagamitin natin, zero pa rin ang forecast.

Pero alam nating may upcoming business opportunity.

This is why demand planning must consider both:

1. **Historical Information:** What happened in the past?
2. **Forward-Looking Information:** What do we know or expect to happen in the future?

**The central idea of Topic 3.5:**

Past Data + Future Business Information = More Informed Demand Forecast.

This is an illustrative conceptual relationship, not a mathematical forecasting formula.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

Now that we understand the basic concept, let's introduce the professional terminology used in Demand Planning.

## 3.1 Classification of Demand Influencing Factors

Generally, we can classify demand influencing factors into three major groups:

1. Internal Business Factors
2. External Market Factors
3. Product and Customer-Related Factors

### 1. Internal Business Factors

Factors controlled or influenced by the company.

Examples: Pricing, promotions, sales strategy, product launches.

### 2. External Market Factors

Factors originating outside the company's direct control.

Examples: Inflation, competition, regulations, economic conditions.

### 3. Product and Customer-Related Factors

Factors associated with the product's lifecycle and customer behavior.

Examples: New customer projects, product phase-outs, substitutions.

Important: May overlap ang classifications. For example, a customer product launch is customer-related, but it can also be considered an external commercial event from the component supplier's perspective.

## 3.2 Internal vs. External Factors

| Comparison | Internal Factors | External Factors |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Within the company | Outside the company |
| Control | Generally more controllable | Generally less controllable |
| Examples | Pricing, promotions | Inflation, competition |
| Monitoring | Sales and Marketing reports | Market intelligence and economic reports |
| Response | Adjust commercial activities | Adapt business and forecasting assumptions |

A useful distinction:

**Controllable does not mean fully predictable.**

For example, a company controls when it launches a promotion, but it cannot perfectly predict how customers will respond.

## 3.3 Demand Impact Categories

Demand influencing factors can affect demand in several ways.

| Impact | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Demand Increase | Higher demand volume | New customer project |
| Demand Decrease | Lower demand volume | Economic slowdown |
| Demand Spike | Sudden temporary increase | Promotional campaign |
| Demand Shift | Demand moves between periods | Customer orders earlier |
| Product Mix Change | Demand changes across products | Customer switches to premium model |
| Long-Term Trend | Sustained demand direction | Growing electric vehicle market |
| One-Time Demand | Non-recurring requirement | Special engineering project |

These categories are important because **the same increase in demand does not always require the same forecasting treatment**.

For example:

- A permanent 20% increase may require a new baseline forecast.
- A one-month 20% increase may only require a temporary adjustment.
- An increase caused by an order pulled forward may require reducing the following month's forecast.

We will examine these differences in detail in the next sections.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Internal Business Factors

Internal Business Factors are conditions, strategies, and decisions within the company that can influence customer purchasing behavior.

Although the company may control these activities, their actual effect on demand still needs to be validated.

### A. Product Pricing and Price Changes

Price is one of the most direct factors affecting purchasing decisions.

Generally:

- Price decrease may encourage customers to purchase more.
- Price increase may discourage purchases.
- Expected future price increases may cause customers to purchase earlier.
- Competitor price changes may affect the attractiveness of our products.

**Example:**

An electronic component manufacturer sells a capacitor for ₱5.00 per unit.

The company reduces the price to ₱4.50 to become more competitive.

| Description | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unit Price | ₱5.00 | ₱4.50 |
| Monthly Demand | 100,000 | 120,000 |
| Monthly Revenue | ₱500,000 | ₱540,000 |

Demand increased by 20%, but revenue increased by only 8%.

This demonstrates why demand planners should consider both volume and commercial implications.

**S&OP Application:**

The Demand Planner should obtain:
- Approved pricing changes.
- Effective dates.
- Expected customer response.
- Historical response to similar price changes.
- Sales estimates supported by customer information.

Do not automatically assume that a 10% price reduction will generate a 10% increase in demand.

The relationship depends on the product, customer, market, and competition.

### B. Promotions and Marketing Campaigns

Promotions are activities intended to encourage customers to purchase products.

Examples:
- Discounts.
- Bundled offers.
- Limited-time offers.
- Seasonal campaigns.
- Trade promotions.
- Marketing campaigns.

**Manufacturing example:**

A company offers a special discount to distributors during September.

Normal demand: 100,000 units/month.

Expected promotional demand: 130,000 units.

Expected increase: 30%.

However, an important question is:

Does the promotion create NEW demand, or does it simply encourage customers to purchase earlier?

For example:

| Month | Normal Forecast | Promotional Scenario |
|---|---:|---:|
| August | 100,000 | 90,000 |
| September | 100,000 | 130,000 |
| October | 100,000 | 80,000 |
| **Total** | **300,000** | **300,000** |

In this example, the promotion did not create additional total demand.

It shifted purchasing from August and October into September.

This is called **Demand Pull-Forward**.

Why is this important?

Kung hindi natin ito ma-identify, baka mag-produce tayo ng additional 30,000 units in September without recognizing that some of those units would otherwise have been purchased in other months.

Potential consequences:
- Excess inventory after the promotion.
- Unnecessary production capacity allocation.
- Increased procurement requirements.
- Higher inventory holding costs.

### C. Sales Strategies and Activities

Sales teams directly interact with customers. They often receive information about upcoming orders, business opportunities, and changes in customer requirements.

Examples:
- Acquiring new customers.
- Increasing sales coverage.
- Entering new markets.
- Negotiating long-term contracts.
- Cross-selling additional products.
- Securing customer projects.

**Example:**

The sales team secures a new customer requiring 50,000 units per month starting January.

This information should be incorporated into the forecast even if there is no historical demand for that customer.

However, the planner must distinguish between:

| Sales Information | Forecast Treatment |
|---|---|
| Signed purchase agreement | Stronger basis for adjustment |
| Confirmed customer forecast | Incorporate based on agreed assumptions |
| Verbal customer interest | Treat as an opportunity, not guaranteed demand |
| Unqualified sales lead | Usually exclude from committed baseline |

An important professional concept here is **Pipeline vs. Committed Demand**.

A sales opportunity is not automatically an actual demand requirement.

### D. Product Launches and Discontinuations

A product launch introduces a new product into the market.

A discontinuation means that the company will stop selling or manufacturing an existing product.

Both can significantly affect demand.

**New Product Introduction (NPI)**

Example:

The company introduces a new capacitor model designed for electric vehicle applications.

Expected demand:

| Month | Forecast |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 20,000 |
| March | 35,000 |
| April | 50,000 |

Since the product is new, there may be little or no historical demand.

Therefore, planners need:
- Customer launch schedules.
- Engineering qualification status.
- Sample approval results.
- Expected production ramp-up.
- Customer purchase commitments.

**Product Discontinuation / Phase-Out**

Suppose an existing product has a monthly demand of 100,000 units.

The customer plans to replace it with a newer model.

| Month | Old Product | New Product |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 100,000 | 0 |
| February | 80,000 | 20,000 |
| March | 50,000 | 50,000 |
| April | 20,000 | 80,000 |
| May | 0 | 100,000 |

Notice that demand is transferring from the old product to the new product.

The planner should monitor the combined product-family demand, not just the individual products.

Otherwise, there is a risk of:
- Excess inventory of the old product.
- Insufficient inventory of the new product.
- Duplicate forecasting of replacement demand.

### E. Product Quality and Availability

Customer demand is not always equal to actual sales.

Imagine that customers want 100,000 units, but the manufacturer can only supply 80,000 units because of production defects.

The recorded sales might only be 80,000 units.

If the planner interprets the historical sales as actual customer demand, the future forecast may be underestimated.

This is an important distinction:

- **Unconstrained Demand:** What customers actually want or require.
- **Constrained Supply:** What the company can physically supply.

Product quality can also influence future demand.

For example:
- Repeated defects may cause customers to reduce orders.
- Product recalls may cause customers to switch suppliers.
- Improved quality may help recover lost customers.

### F. Customer Service Performance

Customer service performance affects customer confidence and purchasing behavior.

Examples of relevant factors:

- On-Time Delivery (OTD).
- Order Fulfillment Rate.
- Lead Time.
- Complaint Resolution.
- Delivery Reliability.

Suppose a supplier repeatedly delivers late.

The customer may:
- Reduce future orders.
- Increase orders from another supplier.
- Maintain higher safety stock.
- Transfer some business to competitors.

Therefore, poor service performance can negatively affect future demand even if the product itself remains competitive.

### G. Sales Targets and Commercial Decisions

Sales targets are goals established by management.

Example:

The company targets a 15% increase in sales next year.

Important distinction:

**A sales target is not automatically a demand forecast.**

A target describes what the business wants to achieve.

A forecast describes what the business realistically expects to happen based on available evidence.

| Sales Target | Demand Forecast |
|---|---|
| Desired result | Expected result |
| May be aspirational | Evidence-based |
| Driven by business objectives | Driven by demand information and assumptions |
| Used for performance management | Used for planning operations |

If the company wants to increase sales by 15%, planners should identify the actual drivers supporting that growth.

For example:
- New customers.
- Additional market share.
- New products.
- Increased customer consumption.
- Improved commercial competitiveness.

Without these supporting factors, increasing the forecast by 15% merely to match the target creates an unrealistic planning signal.

## 4.2 External Market Factors

External Market Factors originate outside the company's direct control.

The company cannot necessarily prevent these events, but it can monitor them and prepare appropriate responses.

### A. Economic Conditions and Inflation

Economic conditions affect customer purchasing power, production activity, and investment decisions.

Examples:

- Economic growth.
- Recession.
- Inflation.
- Interest rates.
- Exchange rates.
- Unemployment.

**Manufacturing example:**

A company manufactures electronic components used in consumer electronics.

During an economic slowdown:
1. Consumers reduce spending.
2. Demand for electronic devices decreases.
3. Device manufacturers reduce production.
4. Their component requirements decrease.
5. Component supplier forecasts decline.

This is an example of derived demand.

**Derived Demand:** Demand for a product that depends on the demand for another product or activity.

For example, demand for capacitors may depend on the production volume of televisions, smartphones, automobiles, and industrial equipment.

This is especially important in B2B manufacturing.

### B. Competition and Competitor Pricing

Competitor actions can affect market share and customer purchasing decisions.

Examples:
- Competitor launches a lower-priced product.
- Competitor improves product quality.
- Competitor offers shorter lead times.
- Competitor introduces new technology.
- Competitor experiences supply shortages.

**Example:**

Your company supplies 100,000 units monthly to a customer.

A competitor offers a comparable product at a lower price.

The customer decides to transfer 20% of its purchases to the competitor.

| Description | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Your Company's Volume | 100,000 | 80,000 |
| Competitor's Volume | 0 | 20,000 |
| Total Customer Requirement | 100,000 | 100,000 |

The market demand did not decrease.

What changed was your company's share of the customer's demand.

This is an important distinction between:

- **Market Demand:** Total demand in the market.
- **Company Demand:** Portion of market demand captured by your company.

### C. Market Trends

Market trends refer to sustained changes in market behavior, technology, or industry preferences.

Examples:
- Increasing demand for electric vehicles.
- Growth in renewable energy.
- Increasing demand for high-performance electronics.
- Shift toward energy-efficient products.
- Growth in automation.

A market trend can create a long-term demand increase.

However, planners must determine whether the trend is relevant to their actual products and customers.

For example, growth in the EV market does not automatically mean that every capacitor manufacturer will experience increased demand.

The company must understand its market position, product applications, customer relationships, and competitive capabilities.

### D. Customer Preferences and Purchasing Behavior

Customers can change their preferences even when their overall consumption remains stable.

Examples:
- Switching to premium products.
- Preferring smaller package sizes.
- Demanding higher-quality components.
- Choosing environmentally friendly products.
- Purchasing from suppliers with shorter lead times.

This can create changes in product mix.

Example:

| Product | Previous Demand | New Demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standard Capacitor | 80,000 | 50,000 |
| Premium Capacitor | 20,000 | 50,000 |
| **Total** | **100,000** | **100,000** |

Total demand remains unchanged, but the product mix has changed.

This can still create substantial operational implications because the products may require different materials, production processes, and capacities.

### E. Seasonality and Weather

Seasonality refers to recurring demand patterns associated with particular periods.

Examples:
- Higher demand for cooling equipment during hot months.
- Higher demand for consumer electronics during holiday seasons.
- Increased automotive production before certain model-year launches.
- Higher agricultural equipment demand during planting seasons.

Weather can also affect demand and supply chains.

For example:
- Extreme heat may increase cooling-related product demand.
- Typhoons may disrupt customer factories.
- Flooding may delay deliveries.
- Severe weather may interrupt supplier operations.

**Important distinction:**

Seasonality is recurring.

A one-time weather disruption is not necessarily seasonality.

### F. Industry Developments

Industry developments refer to significant changes within a specific industry.

Examples:
- New automotive manufacturing plants.
- Semiconductor industry capacity expansion.
- New electronics manufacturing standards.
- Major investments in renewable energy.
- Changes in industrial automation requirements.

These developments can create additional demand or reduce demand for certain products.

### G. Government Regulations

Government policies can influence which products customers are allowed or encouraged to purchase.

Examples:
- Environmental requirements.
- Import restrictions.
- Tariffs.
- Product certification requirements.
- Emission standards.
- Local manufacturing incentives.

For example, a new environmental regulation may encourage automotive manufacturers to transition toward electric vehicles.

This could influence demand for certain electronic components.

However, regulatory changes may also create delays if products need to undergo additional certification or qualification.

### H. Technological Changes

Technology can create new demand while making existing products obsolete.

Examples:
- New battery technology.
- Advanced semiconductor designs.
- Miniaturization of electronic components.
- New automotive electronic systems.
- Changes in device architecture.

This may create:
- New product opportunities.
- Reduced demand for older products.
- Product substitution.
- Changes in customer specifications.

### I. Geopolitical Events and Market Disruptions

These include events involving countries, trade relationships, and global supply networks.

Examples:
- International conflicts.
- Trade restrictions.
- Sanctions.
- Shipping route disruptions.
- Export controls.
- Global logistics interruptions.

These events can affect customer demand indirectly.

For example:

A trade restriction increases the cost of importing electronic components. A customer may delay its production expansion, resulting in lower component requirements.

However, some disruptions may also increase demand for alternative suppliers if customers shift their sourcing arrangements.

### How do companies monitor external factors?

| Information Source | What It Helps Monitor |
|---|---|
| Economic reports | Inflation, GDP, economic growth |
| Customer forecasts | Expected purchasing requirements |
| Sales reports | Customer behavior and market opportunities |
| Competitor intelligence | Pricing and market position |
| Industry reports | Industry growth and technological changes |
| Government announcements | Regulations and trade restrictions |
| Market research | Customer preferences and market trends |
| Supplier/customer discussions | Upcoming operational changes |

An S&OP Analyst does not need to personally investigate every external factor.

The important responsibility is to identify which developments could materially affect the company's demand and coordinate with the appropriate business stakeholders.

## 4.3 Product and Customer-Related Factors

These factors focus on the relationship between the product's lifecycle and the customer's purchasing requirements.

### A. Product Lifecycle

Products generally pass through different stages:

1. Introduction
2. Growth
3. Maturity
4. Decline

| Lifecycle Stage | Demand Behavior | Planning Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Low initial demand | Customer qualification and ramp-up |
| Growth | Increasing demand | Capacity and material expansion |
| Maturity | Relatively stable demand | Efficient production and inventory |
| Decline | Decreasing demand | Inventory reduction and phase-out |

Why does this matter?

A product that is growing should not necessarily be forecast using its historical average alone.

Similarly, a declining product should not automatically continue receiving the same production allocation.

### B. New Product Introductions (NPI)

A new product introduction occurs when a company introduces a new product or a new product version.

Important information:
- Expected launch date.
- Customer approval.
- Qualification status.
- Initial order quantity.
- Production ramp-up schedule.
- Expected monthly demand.

One common mistake is forecasting full production demand before the customer has completed qualification.

For example, a customer may initially request 5,000 sample units but eventually require 100,000 units per month after mass production approval.

These are different demand stages and should be planned separately.

### C. Product Phase-Out

Product phase-out is the planned discontinuation of a product.

Important considerations:
- Last production date.
- Last order date.
- Final purchase requirement.
- Remaining customer inventory.
- Replacement product.
- Remaining raw material and finished goods.

The main risk is producing too much of a product that will soon become obsolete.

### D. Product Substitutions

Product substitution happens when a customer replaces one product with another.

Example:

A customer previously purchases Model A but decides to use Model B instead.

| Month | Model A | Model B | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 100,000 | 0 | 100,000 |
| February | 75,000 | 25,000 | 100,000 |
| March | 50,000 | 50,000 | 100,000 |
| April | 25,000 | 75,000 | 100,000 |
| May | 0 | 100,000 | 100,000 |

Total demand remains the same.

But the demand allocation between the two products changes.

This is particularly important when different products have different:
- Raw materials.
- Manufacturing processes.
- Production cycle times.
- Supplier lead times.
- Production capacities.

### E. Customer Growth or Decline

A customer's business performance can directly influence the demand for components.

Examples:

**Customer Growth**
- Increased production volume.
- Additional manufacturing facilities.
- New product programs.
- Increased market share.

**Customer Decline**
- Reduced production.
- Plant closures.
- Financial difficulties.
- Lost market share.
- Business restructuring.

The S&OP team should distinguish between temporary production fluctuations and sustained changes in customer business volume.

### F. Customer Purchasing Patterns

Customers may have different ordering behaviors even when their actual consumption remains relatively stable.

Examples:
- Monthly orders.
- Weekly orders.
- Large quarterly orders.
- Irregular purchasing.
- Just-in-Time ordering.
- Bulk purchases before holidays.

This introduces an important distinction:

**Consumption vs. Ordering Pattern**

A customer may consume 100,000 units monthly but order 300,000 units once every three months.

If we use the order quantity without understanding the ordering pattern, the forecast may show artificial demand spikes.

### G. Customer-Specific Projects

Some customers purchase components for specific projects.

Examples:
- New vehicle model.
- New smartphone production.
- New industrial equipment.
- New electronics manufacturing line.

Project-related demand can have a defined beginning and ending.

A planner must understand:
- Project start date.
- Expected production volume.
- Project duration.
- Customer commitment.
- Expected project completion.
- Potential delays or cancellations.

### H. Changes in Customer Requirements

Customers may change:
- Product specifications.
- Required quantities.
- Delivery schedules.
- Quality standards.
- Preferred suppliers.
- Production schedules.

These changes may affect demand volume, product mix, and timing.

For example, a customer may move its planned production from December to November.

The total requirement may remain unchanged, but the timing of component demand changes.

## 4.4 Understanding Temporary vs. Permanent Demand Changes

This is one of the most important analytical concepts in Topic 3.5.

Not all demand changes should be incorporated into the forecast in the same way.

### Temporary Demand Change

A temporary demand change occurs when demand increases or decreases for a limited period before returning to its previous level or expected pattern.

Examples:
- Promotional campaigns.
- One-time customer orders.
- Temporary competitor supply shortage.
- Short-term production recovery.
- Seasonal demand.

### Permanent or Structural Demand Change

A permanent or structural change occurs when the underlying demand level or pattern changes for an extended period.

Examples:
- A customer permanently increases production.
- A major customer permanently transfers business to your company.
- A product is replaced by a new product.
- A customer closes a manufacturing facility.
- A market experiences sustained growth or decline.

Compare the following:

| Month | Normal Baseline | Temporary Scenario | Structural Scenario |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 100,000 | 100,000 | 100,000 |
| February | 100,000 | 130,000 | 130,000 |
| March | 100,000 | 100,000 | 130,000 |
| April | 100,000 | 100,000 | 130,000 |
| May | 100,000 | 100,000 | 130,000 |

In the temporary scenario, the increase occurs only in February.

In the structural scenario, the increase continues.

**Important analytical rule:**

Before changing the long-term forecast baseline, determine whether the influencing factor represents a temporary event or a sustained change in underlying demand.

Do not automatically treat one unusually high month as evidence of permanent growth.

# 5. PRACTICAL EXAMPLES AND APPLICATIONS

Now let's combine everything using an illustrative electronic components manufacturer.

Assume the company manufactures MLCC components and supplies automotive and consumer electronics customers.

Its normal monthly demand is:

**500,000 units**

This is an illustrative baseline forecast.

We will examine six business scenarios.

## Scenario 1: Major Customer Increases Production Requirements

**Situation:**

A major automotive customer announces that it will increase vehicle production by 20% beginning January.

Your company currently supplies 200,000 MLCC units monthly to this customer.

The customer expects component requirements to increase proportionally.

| Description | Current | Expected |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly Demand | 200,000 | 240,000 |
| Increase | | 40,000 |
| Percentage Increase | | 20% |

**Influencing Factor:** Customer production expansion.

**Expected Demand Impact:** Sustained demand increase, assuming the production expansion is confirmed and the component usage per vehicle remains unchanged.

**Information Needed:**
- Customer production schedule.
- Updated customer forecast.
- Vehicle production plan.
- Component usage per unit.
- Customer order commitments.

**Forecast Adjustment:**

Add 40,000 units to the monthly forecast beginning January, subject to the customer's confirmed ramp-up schedule.

**Operational Implications:**

| Area | Potential Impact |
|---|---|
| Production | Additional production requirements |
| Procurement | Higher raw material requirements |
| Inventory | Possible need for additional safety stock |
| Capacity | Increased machine utilization |
| Customer Service | Risk of shortages if capacity is insufficient |

Professional consideration:

Do not immediately assume a 20% increase in component demand just because vehicle production increases by 20%.

First validate whether the customer is using the same component quantity per vehicle.

## Scenario 2: Competitor Launches a Lower-Priced Product

**Situation:**

A competitor introduces a comparable MLCC product at a price 10% lower than yours.

A customer plans to transfer 15% of its monthly orders to the competitor.

Current customer demand from your company is 100,000 units.

| Description | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Current Demand | 100,000 |
| Expected Business Transfer | 15,000 |
| Revised Demand | 85,000 |

**Influencing Factor:** Competitor pricing and increased competition.

**Expected Demand Impact:** Demand decrease for your company.

**Information Needed:**
- Competitor price.
- Customer sourcing decision.
- Customer allocation agreement.
- Actual order reductions.
- Competitor qualification status.

**Forecast Adjustment:**

Reduce the affected customer's forecast by 15,000 units, beginning when the customer is expected to transfer its business.

**Operational Implications:**

- Reduce production allocation.
- Reassess procurement requirements.
- Review inventory exposure.
- Reallocate capacity to other customers if possible.

Important:

The customer's total market demand may remain unchanged. What decreases is your company's share.

## Scenario 3: Promotional Activity Increases Demand

**Situation:**

The company launches a special promotional program for one of its electronic component distributors.

Normal monthly demand is 80,000 units.

Expected promotional demand is 104,000 units.

| Description | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Normal Demand | 80,000 |
| Promotional Demand | 104,000 |
| Incremental Demand | 24,000 |
| Increase | 30% |

**Influencing Factor:** Promotional activity.

**Expected Demand Impact:** Temporary increase or demand pull-forward.

**Information Needed:**
- Promotion duration.
- Distributor inventory.
- Customer purchase commitments.
- Historical promotional performance.
- Expected post-promotion demand.

**Forecast Adjustment:**

Add 24,000 units to the promotional period only if the increase represents additional demand.

If the promotion only shifts purchases from the next month, the following month's forecast should also be adjusted.

**Operational Implications:**

- Temporary increase in production.
- Additional raw material requirements.
- Possible inventory buildup at the distributor.
- Potential reduction in subsequent orders.

## Scenario 4: New Product Launch

**Situation:**

The company launches a new high-performance MLCC model for an automotive customer.

Expected demand:

| Month | New Product Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 20,000 |
| March | 40,000 |
| April | 60,000 |

**Influencing Factor:** New product introduction and customer project launch.

**Expected Demand Impact:** New demand that gradually increases during production ramp-up.

**Information Needed:**
- Product approval.
- Qualification status.
- Customer launch schedule.
- Expected production volume.
- Material availability.
- Production readiness.

**Forecast Adjustment:**

Introduce the new product forecast based on the validated customer launch and ramp-up schedule.

Do not use the historical demand of an unrelated existing product as the new product's baseline.

**Operational Implications:**

- New raw material requirements.
- Potential supplier qualification.
- Production line preparation.
- Capacity allocation.
- Initial inventory buildup.

## Scenario 5: Existing Product Phase-Out

**Situation:**

A customer plans to discontinue Model A and transition to Model B.

The original demand for Model A is 100,000 units monthly.

The customer expects a five-month transition.

| Month | Model A | Model B | Combined |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 100,000 | 0 | 100,000 |
| February | 80,000 | 20,000 | 100,000 |
| March | 60,000 | 40,000 | 100,000 |
| April | 30,000 | 70,000 | 100,000 |
| May | 0 | 100,000 | 100,000 |

**Influencing Factor:** Product phase-out and substitution.

**Expected Demand Impact:** Demand transfers from the old product to the new product.

**Information Needed:**
- Last order date.
- Last production date.
- Replacement product approval.
- Customer transition schedule.
- Remaining customer inventory.

**Forecast Adjustment:**

Gradually reduce Model A and increase Model B according to the transition plan.

**Operational Implications:**

- Avoid excess inventory of Model A.
- Ensure Model B materials are available.
- Adjust production schedules.
- Review obsolete raw materials.
- Coordinate final purchases and phase-out quantities.

|---|---:|

**Expected Demand Impact:** Potential sustained demand decrease.

- Updated customer forecasts.
- Customer production schedules.
- Industry demand indicators.
- Actual order cancellations.
- Customer inventory levels.

**Forecast Adjustment:**

Reduce the affected customer or market-segment forecast by 45,000 units if the decrease is supported by customer and market information.

**Operational Implications:**

- Review raw material purchasing.
- Avoid unnecessary finished goods inventory.
- Reassess capacity utilization.
- Evaluate inventory exposure.

# 6. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

Demand influencing factors do not always require mathematical calculations.

However, S&OP Analysts need quantitative methods to translate business information into measurable forecast adjustments.

Let's discuss the most useful calculations.

## 6.1 Demand Percentage Change

This calculates how much demand increased or decreased compared with the original demand.

**Formula:**

Demand Change % = [(New Demand − Original Demand) / Original Demand] × 100

Where:

- New Demand = Revised demand quantity.
- Original Demand = Previous or baseline demand.

**Example:**

Original demand = 100,000 units.

New demand = 120,000 units.

Step 1: Calculate the difference.

120,000 − 100,000 = 20,000

Step 2: Divide by original demand.

20,000 / 100,000 = 0.20

Step 3: Convert to percentage.

0.20 × 100 = 20%

**Result: 20% demand increase.**

This is useful when evaluating customer growth, pricing changes, and market developments.

## 6.2 Forecast Adjustment

This calculates the revised forecast after considering an influencing factor.

**Formula:**

Adjusted Forecast = Baseline Forecast + Adjustment

Example:

| Component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Baseline Forecast | 500,000 |
| Additional Customer Demand | +40,000 |
| Competitor Impact | -15,000 |
| Revised Forecast | 525,000 |

Calculation:

500,000 + 40,000 − 15,000 = **525,000 units**

This assumes the two adjustments affect separate, non-overlapping demand quantities.

## 6.3 Demand Adjustment Percentage

Sometimes business stakeholders provide a percentage adjustment rather than an actual quantity.

**Formula:**

Adjustment Quantity = Baseline Forecast × Adjustment %

Example:

Baseline forecast = 200,000 units.

Expected increase = 15%.

200,000 × 0.15 = 30,000

Adjusted forecast:

200,000 + 30,000 = **230,000 units**

## 6.4 Incremental Demand vs. Demand Shift

This is particularly important when evaluating promotions, customer projects, and purchasing behavior.

**Incremental Demand** means additional demand that would not have existed without the influencing factor.

**Demand Shift** means demand transferred from one period to another.

Example:

| Month | Baseline | Revised | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| August | 100,000 | 90,000 | -10,000 |
| September | 100,000 | 130,000 | +30,000 |
| October | 100,000 | 80,000 | -20,000 |
| **Total** | **300,000** | **300,000** | **0** |

Total incremental demand:

Total Revised Demand − Total Baseline Demand

300,000 − 300,000 = **0**

Therefore, there is no additional total demand in this illustrative three-month period.

The promotion simply changed the timing of purchases.

This is important because production and procurement may need to shift their timing without increasing the total planned quantity.

## 6.5 Product Mix Percentage

This calculates the proportion of each product within total demand.

**Formula:**

Product Mix % = (Product Demand / Total Demand) × 100

Example:

| Product | Demand | Product Mix |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standard | 60,000 | 60% |
| Premium | 40,000 | 40% |
| Total | 100,000 | 100% |

After a customer preference change:

| Product | Demand | Product Mix |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standard | 40,000 | 40% |
| Premium | 60,000 | 60% |
| Total | 100,000 | 100% |

The total demand is unchanged, but the product mix changes significantly.

This matters because different products may have different production and material requirements.

## 6.6 Avoiding Double-Counting Adjustments

This is a professional-level consideration.

Suppose:

- Baseline forecast = 100,000 units.
- Sales team reports a new customer project = +20,000.
- Customer forecast already includes that project.
- Planner adds another +20,000 to the existing forecast.

Result:

100,000 + 20,000 + 20,000 = 140,000

But the correct demand may only be:

100,000 + 20,000 = **120,000**

The additional 20,000 was counted twice.

This is called **Double-Counting**.

To prevent it, the planner must establish:

1. What demand is already included in the baseline?
2. What demand is included in the customer's forecast?
3. Which adjustments represent genuinely new demand?
4. Which adjustments are already reflected in another input?

A demand adjustment should have a clear source, owner, period, and reason.

# 7. BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE AND DECISION-MAKING

Now let's move from understanding the concept to thinking like an S&OP Analyst.

Your responsibility is not simply to receive numbers from Sales, Marketing, or customers and place them into a spreadsheet.

You must evaluate whether the information is credible, relevant, measurable, and useful for business planning.

## 7.1 Identifying Demand Changes

The first responsibility is identifying whether demand has changed and understanding why.

For example:

Your statistical forecast is 500,000 units.

Sales submits an updated forecast of 580,000 units.

The difference is:

580,000 − 500,000 = 80,000

Your next question should not simply be:

"Should I accept the 580,000?"

Instead, ask:

- What caused the additional 80,000 units?
- Which customers are responsible?
- Is the increase temporary or permanent?
- Is it supported by customer information?
- Is it already included in the baseline?
- When will the increase occur?

## 7.2 Monitoring Internal and External Factors

An analyst should establish a regular process for collecting demand-related information.

| Stakeholder | Information Provided |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer opportunities, orders, expected demand |
| Marketing | Promotions and market campaigns |
| Product Management | Launches and phase-outs |
| Customer Service | Customer complaints and service issues |
| Finance | Revenue assumptions and economic outlook |
| Supply Chain | Customer delivery requirements and supply constraints |
| Management | Commercial strategies and business priorities |

The goal is to build a consolidated understanding of what may change future demand.

## 7.3 Challenging Unsupported Assumptions

Let's say Sales submits the following statement:

"Next quarter, demand will increase by 25% because the market is growing."

As an analyst, this is insufficient information.

You should challenge the assumption professionally.

Possible questions:

1. Which market segment is growing?
2. What evidence supports the 25% growth?
3. Which customers will generate the increase?
4. When will the increase begin?
5. Is the growth already reflected in customer forecasts?
6. Is this an opportunity or an actual customer requirement?

The purpose is not to reject Sales' inputs.

It is to improve the reliability of the demand plan.

## 7.4 Quantifying Opportunities and Risks

An S&OP Analyst should translate qualitative business information into measurable demand impacts.

Example:

| Business Event | Potential Demand Impact | Confidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Confirmed customer expansion | +40,000 | High |
| Potential new customer | +30,000 | Low |
| Competitor business transfer | -15,000 | Medium |
| Planned promotion | +20,000 | Medium |

The confidence levels are illustrative assessments, not universal industry standards.

You can use these inputs to communicate:

- Potential upside.
- Potential downside.
- Expected demand.
- Uncertainty.
- Operational exposure.

An important concept here is **Demand Risk and Opportunity Assessment**.

A demand opportunity is a possible increase in business.

A demand risk is a possible decrease or disruption in expected demand.

Both should be visible during Demand Review.

## 7.5 Incorporating Information into Demand Review

Demand Review is where relevant stakeholders evaluate the demand outlook and discuss important changes, assumptions, risks, and opportunities.

A simplified workflow:

1. **Identify the influencing factor.** Example: A major customer plans to increase production.
2. **Collect supporting information.** Obtain customer forecasts, production schedules, and sales inputs.
3. **Validate the information.** Confirm the timing, quantity, reliability, and scope.
4. **Quantify the demand impact.** Calculate the incremental or reduced demand.
5. **Adjust the forecast.** Apply the validated adjustment to the appropriate product, customer, and period.
6. **Evaluate risks and assumptions.** Identify uncertainty, possible double-counting, and alternative scenarios.
7. **Present during Demand Review.** Communicate the revised outlook and business implications.

## 7.6 Demand Adjustment Documentation

One of the most useful professional practices is maintaining a Demand Adjustment Log.

Example:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Customer | Customer A |
| Product | MLCC Model X |
| Influencing Factor | Customer production expansion |
| Baseline Forecast | 100,000 |
| Adjustment | +20,000 |
| Revised Forecast | 120,000 |
| Effective Period | January |
| Source | Customer forecast |
| Owner | Sales |
| Duration | January–June |
| Confidence | High |
| Status | Validated |

Why is documentation important?

Because the forecast should be explainable.

If management asks why January's demand increased by 20%, the analyst should be able to identify the exact business event that caused the adjustment.

**Professional principle: Every material demand adjustment should have a traceable business reason and supporting evidence.**

# 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Let's consolidate the most important concepts from Topic 3.5.

## 8.1 Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What You Should Remember |
|---|---|
| Demand Influencing Factors | Events and conditions that affect customer demand. |
| Internal Factors | Business decisions such as pricing, promotions, and sales strategies. |
| External Factors | Market conditions such as inflation, competition, and regulations. |
| Product-Related Factors | Product launches, phase-outs, lifecycle, and substitutions. |
| Customer-Related Factors | Customer growth, projects, purchasing behavior, and requirements. |
| Derived Demand | Demand for a product that depends on demand for another product or activity. |
| Demand Pull-Forward | Customers purchase earlier than originally planned. |
| Incremental Demand | Additional demand created by a particular event. |
| Product Mix | Distribution of demand across different products. |
| Double-Counting | Counting the same demand more than once. |

## 8.2 Five Important Distinctions

These distinctions are particularly important in actual S&OP work.

| Distinction | Difference |
|---|---|
| Normal Variation vs. Event-Driven Change | Ordinary fluctuations versus changes caused by identifiable events. |
| Internal vs. External Factors | Company-originated versus market-originated factors. |
| Temporary vs. Structural Change | Short-term changes versus sustained changes in demand. |
| Incremental Demand vs. Demand Shift | Additional demand versus demand transferred between periods. |
| Sales Target vs. Demand Forecast | Desired business result versus realistic expected demand. |

## 8.3 The Five Questions an S&OP Analyst Should Always Ask

Whenever someone proposes a demand adjustment, ask:

1. **WHAT happened?** Identify the influencing factor.
2. **WHY will it affect demand?** Understand the business relationship.
3. **HOW MUCH will demand change?** Quantify the impact.
4. **WHEN will it happen?** Determine the affected periods.
5. **HOW DO WE KNOW?** Validate the information using supporting evidence.

These five questions form a practical framework for evaluating demand assumptions.

## 8.4 What You Should Be Able to Do

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Identify internal and external demand influencing factors.
- Explain how pricing, promotions, and competition affect demand.
- Distinguish market demand from company-specific demand.
- Understand product lifecycle and customer-related changes.
- Differentiate temporary and structural demand changes.
- Identify demand increases, decreases, shifts, and product mix changes.
- Calculate demand adjustments and revised forecasts.
- Recognize demand pull-forward and double-counting.
- Evaluate the reliability of Sales and customer inputs.
- Explain demand changes during Demand Review.
