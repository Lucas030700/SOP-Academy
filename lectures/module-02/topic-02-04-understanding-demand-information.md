# Module 2 – Supply Chain Management
## Topic 2.4: Understanding Demand Information

**Main learning objective:** Understand how companies collect, organize, analyze, and interpret information about customer demand to make better business decisions.

For this lesson, imagine that you're an S&OP Analyst in an electronic components manufacturing company. Every month, Sales provides customer requirements, the ERP system contains customer orders, historical reports show previous sales, and Demand Planning has a forecast.

Your responsibility is to understand how all these pieces of information fit together.

Here's an important question that we'll keep returning to throughout this lesson:

**If a customer is expected to purchase 10,000 units, but the company has only received confirmed orders for 6,000 units, how should the company understand and plan for the remaining 4,000 units?**

This is where understanding demand information becomes essential.

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

## 1.1 What will we discuss?

In the previous topics, we established the basic structure of the supply chain:

- **Topic 2.1:** Understanding what a supply chain is.
- **Topic 2.2:** Understanding the different components of a supply chain.
- **Topic 2.3:** Understanding how products and materials physically flow through the supply chain.
- **Topic 2.4:** Understanding the information that tells a company what customers need, how much they need, and when they need it.

Notice the transition.

Topic 2.3 focuses on the physical movement of products.

Topic 2.4 focuses on the information that helps companies anticipate and respond to the movement of those products.

A company cannot effectively manage its production, purchasing, and inventory if it doesn't understand what customers are likely to require.

## 1.2 Why is demand information important?

Imagine a manufacturer that produces electronic components.

Its customers are electronics manufacturers that purchase components regularly.

The manufacturer needs to answer several questions:

1. What products do customers need?
2. How many units will they require?
3. When will they need them?
4. Which customers are increasing or reducing their orders?
5. Are upcoming requirements temporary or part of a long-term trend?
6. Are the requirements already confirmed, or are they only expected?

These questions influence several business functions.

| Business function | How demand information helps |
|---|---|
| Demand Planning | Determines expected customer requirements |
| Supply Planning | Determines how much supply is needed |
| Production | Determines what and how much to manufacture |
| Procurement | Determines required raw materials |
| Inventory Management | Helps maintain appropriate stock levels |
| Finance | Supports revenue and financial planning |
| S&OP | Aligns expected demand with business capabilities |

**Central idea:** Demand information connects customer requirements to internal business decisions.

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

## 2.1 What is demand information?

Let's start with a simple example.

Imagine you own a small bakery.

You sell three products:

- Pandesal
- Loaf bread
- Spanish bread

Yesterday, you sold:

| Product | Quantity sold |
|---|---:|
| Pandesal | 500 |
| Loaf bread | 100 |
| Spanish bread | 150 |
| **Total** | **750** |

Today, you receive the following information:

- A customer ordered 200 loaf breads for tomorrow.
- A nearby school will hold an event next week.
- Your sales staff expects pandesal demand to increase.
- Last December, your bread sales increased significantly.

All these pieces of information help you understand customer demand.

They are examples of **demand information**.

In simple terms:

> Demand information is the collection of data, records, observations, and business insights that help a company understand what customers want, how much they want, and when they want it.

Hindi lang ito basta sales records.

It includes information about:

- What customers already purchased.
- What customers have ordered.
- What customers are expected to order.
- What customers might need in the future.
- What market conditions could affect their purchasing behavior.

## 2.2 Demand versus demand information

Magkaiba ang demand mismo at ang information na ginagamit natin para maintindihan ito.

Think of it this way:

- **Demand** is the actual customer requirement or desire for a product.
- **Demand information** is the evidence and data that help us understand that requirement.

Example:

A customer needs 5,000 electronic components next month.

That requirement represents demand.

However, how do we know about it?

Perhaps:

- The customer submitted an order.
- The customer shared a monthly forecast.
- The Sales team received a purchasing commitment.
- Historical records indicate that the customer normally purchases 5,000 units monthly.

These are different sources of demand information.

Importante ito because companies do not always have direct and complete visibility of what customers will actually need.

## 2.3 Why can't companies simply use sales data?

Let's return to the bakery.

Suppose you normally sell 1,000 pandesal every day.

One day, you sell only 600.

Does this automatically mean customers only wanted 600?

Not necessarily!

Perhaps:

- You only prepared 600 pieces.
- You ran out of ingredients.
- Your bakery closed early.
- Your delivery was delayed.

The 600 units represent actual sales, but they may not represent the full customer demand.

This is one of the most important ideas in Demand Planning:

**Actual sales and actual demand are not always equal.**

We will explore this distinction in detail later.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

## 3.1 Formal definition of demand information

In a supply chain and S&OP environment, demand information refers to the structured collection of historical, current, and forward-looking information used to understand, quantify, and evaluate customer requirements for products or services over a specified period.

It typically includes:

- Historical sales and shipment records.
- Customer orders and order backlog.
- Customer forecasts.
- Internal demand forecasts.
- Sales and market intelligence.
- Promotional and commercial activities.
- New product and end-of-life requirements.
- Other factors affecting future customer demand.

The purpose is to establish a reliable understanding of demand that can support operational and strategic planning.

## 3.2 Demand information versus demand planning

These two terms are related, but they are not interchangeable.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand Information | The data and business inputs used to understand customer requirements |
| Demand Analysis | The process of examining and interpreting demand information |
| Demand Forecasting | Estimating future demand using available information and appropriate methods |
| Demand Planning | Developing and maintaining an agreed view of expected demand |
| S&OP Demand Review | Reviewing demand assumptions, changes, risks, opportunities, and the demand plan |

Think of these as connected activities.

First, the company collects information.

Next, it analyzes the information.

Then, it develops its demand forecast and demand plan.

Finally, the organization reviews and aligns the plan through the S&OP process.

A deeper discussion of forecasting methodologies will come in Module 3.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Sources of Demand Information

Companies obtain demand information from multiple sources.

Hindi puwedeng umasa sa iisang source lang because each source provides a different perspective.

Let's examine the major sources.

### A. Customer Orders

Customer orders are requests submitted by customers to purchase specific products in specified quantities and delivery periods.

Example:

A customer sends a purchase order:

| Information | Details |
|---|---|
| Customer | Customer A |
| Product | MLCC Model X |
| Order quantity | 10,000 units |
| Requested delivery | December 15 |
| Order status | Confirmed |

This provides relatively strong evidence of customer purchasing requirements.

However, remember that a customer order does not guarantee that the customer will receive or consume the entire quantity.

Orders may still be revised, delayed, or cancelled.

**Important customer order terminology**

**1. Confirmed Customer Orders**

Orders accepted and recorded by the supplier according to its order-processing rules.

These represent committed demand for planning purposes, subject to subsequent changes.

**2. Open Orders**

Orders that have been received but are not yet fully fulfilled or closed.

Example:

A customer orders 10,000 units.

The company delivers 6,000 units.

The remaining 4,000 units are still open.

**3. Backlog**

The portion of outstanding customer demand that remains unfulfilled.

In the example:

\[
Backlog = 10,000-6,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Backlog=4,000\ units}
\]

Backlog is important because it represents existing requirements that the company still needs to fulfill.

**4. Order Changes and Cancellations**

Customers may:

- Increase order quantities.
- Reduce order quantities.
- Change delivery dates.
- Cancel orders.

These changes must be reflected in the demand information to avoid planning based on outdated requirements.

**Business application:** Customer orders help companies understand near-term requirements and determine what needs to be supplied.

### B. Historical Sales Data

Historical sales data refers to records of products sold during previous periods.

Example:

| Month | Product X sales |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 9,000 |
| March | 10,000 |
| April | 11,000 |
| May | 12,000 |

This information allows the company to identify changes in purchasing activity.

For example, sales increased every month.

This might indicate an upward demand trend.

**What can companies learn from historical sales?**

- Previous sales quantities.
- Customer purchasing behavior.
- Seasonal demand patterns.
- Product performance.
- Changes in market activity.
- Differences between actual results and previous forecasts.

However, historical sales have limitations.

Consider this situation:

Customers wanted 12,000 units, but the company could only deliver 9,000 because of insufficient inventory.

The recorded sales or shipments might show only 9,000 units.

If the company uses 9,000 as its historical demand without investigating the shortage, it may underestimate future requirements.

Therefore:

**Historical sales are useful evidence of past demand, but they do not automatically represent all customer demand.**

Also, distinguish the following:

- Sales: Commercial transactions recorded by the business.
- Shipments: Products physically dispatched to customers.
- Consumption: Products actually used by customers.

These may occur at different times and quantities.

### C. Demand Forecasts

A demand forecast is an estimate of how much customers are expected to require in the future.

Example:

| Month | Forecast quantity |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 11,000 |
| March | 12,000 |
| April | 12,500 |

Unlike customer orders, these quantities may not yet be supported by confirmed purchase orders.

For example:

A company forecasts that Customer A will purchase 12,000 units in January.

However, it has received confirmed orders for only 8,000 units.

The difference is 4,000 units.

That difference represents forecast demand not yet covered by the recorded customer orders, assuming both figures use the same product, customer, period, and quantity basis.

**Important forecast terminology**

- Forecast period: The time period covered by the forecast.
- Planning bucket: The time interval used for planning, such as weekly or monthly.
- Forecast quantity: The expected number of units.
- Forecast assumptions: Conditions or expectations used to develop the forecast.

One important consideration:

Customer forecasts and internal forecasts may overlap with confirmed orders.

Hindi natin dapat basta pagsamahin ang lahat ng numbers.

We will demonstrate this in the manufacturing scenario.

### D. Sales and Market Information

Not all demand information comes from transaction systems.

Some information comes from people who interact directly with customers and markets.

Examples:

| Source | Example information |
|---|---|
| Sales team | Customer expects higher purchasing volume |
| Customer account manager | Customer plans to increase production |
| Marketing | Upcoming promotional campaign |
| Customer communication | New project requiring additional components |
| Market intelligence | Industry demand is expected to grow |
| Competitive information | Competitor discontinued a product |

This information is valuable because it may reveal demand changes before they appear in actual orders.

However, its reliability varies.

For example:

A Sales representative says that Customer A may increase its orders by 30%.

That is useful information, but the analyst should ask:

- Is this based on an actual customer discussion?
- Is there a formal customer forecast?
- Is it a confirmed commitment?
- When will the increase happen?
- How long will it last?

This is called validating demand assumptions.

### E. Other Demand Signals

Other events can influence future demand.

| Demand signal | Possible effect |
|---|---|
| New product launch | Creates new demand |
| Product phase-out | Reduces or eliminates future demand |
| Customer project | Creates additional requirements |
| Economic downturn | May reduce purchasing activity |
| Seasonal events | Create predictable demand changes |
| Market disruption | Can increase or decrease requirements |
| Product substitution | Shifts demand between products |

For example:

An electronics manufacturer launches a new product.

Its customer requires 50,000 additional electronic components for the launch.

Even if historical sales show no previous demand for that specific product, the new project creates a legitimate demand signal.

This is why historical data alone is insufficient.

## 4.2 Types of Demand Information

Now that we understand the sources, let's distinguish the different types of demand information.

This is particularly important because these terms are sometimes used interchangeably in business reports, even though they have different meanings.

### A. Actual Demand

Actual demand refers to the real quantity of products that customers require during a particular period.

Example:

Customer A needed 10,000 units in January.

However, the manufacturer delivered only 8,000 units because of a production shortage.

The customer's requirement was 10,000 units, even though the company delivered only 8,000.

Actual demand attempts to represent what customers required, including requirements that may not have been fulfilled.

**Important:** Actual demand can be difficult to measure perfectly, especially when lost sales or unfulfilled customer requirements are not recorded.

### B. Actual Sales

Actual sales refer to the quantity of products recorded as sold during a specific period.

Example:

| Description | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer requirement | 10,000 |
| Actual sales recorded | 8,000 |
| Unfulfilled requirement | 2,000 |

In this situation, actual sales are lower than actual demand.

However, the exact relationship depends on how the company records sales. Revenue recognition, invoicing, shipment, and order booking may use different dates.

### C. Customer Orders

These are quantities formally requested by customers through purchase orders or other accepted ordering channels.

Example:

Customer A submits an order for 10,000 units with a requested delivery date of January 20.

This is a recorded customer requirement, but it is not necessarily the same as actual sales or shipment.

### D. Open Orders

Open orders are customer orders that remain uncompleted in the order management system.

Example:

| Description | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Original order | 10,000 |
| Delivered | 6,000 |
| Remaining open quantity | 4,000 |

Open orders may include orders that are not yet due for delivery.

Therefore, an open order is not automatically overdue.

### E. Backlog

Backlog represents outstanding customer requirements that have not yet been fulfilled.

For this lesson, we will use the operational definition:

\[
Backlog = Outstanding\ Unfulfilled\ Orders
\]

Example:

| Customer | Outstanding quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer A | 4,000 |
| Customer B | 3,000 |
| Customer C | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **9,000** |

The manufacturer has 9,000 units of outstanding customer demand.

A company may further distinguish between:

- Total open order backlog.
- Past-due backlog.
- Future-due open orders.

These distinctions are important for delivery performance and supply planning.

### F. Historical Demand

Historical demand refers to demand information recorded for previous periods.

It may include:

- Historical customer orders.
- Actual sales.
- Shipments.
- Customer consumption.
- Adjusted demand estimates.

The exact definition depends on the company's reporting methodology.

For example:

| Month | Historical demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 11,000 |
| March | 12,000 |

This information helps the company understand previous demand behavior.

Remember: Historical demand and historical sales are not necessarily equal.

### G. Forecasted Demand

Forecasted demand is the estimated quantity customers are expected to require during a future period.

Example:

The company expects Customer A to require 15,000 units in February.

However, only 9,000 units have been ordered so far.

The forecast provides visibility of expected demand beyond the confirmed orders currently recorded.

### H. Independent Demand

This is a more technical concept.

Independent demand refers to demand for products that is determined by external customer requirements or market needs, rather than being directly calculated from the demand for another component.

Example:

A customer purchases 10,000 finished electronic devices.

The demand for those finished devices is independent demand.

The manufacturer must understand this customer requirement before determining its production and material requirements.

### I. Dependent Demand

Dependent demand refers to demand for components, materials, or subassemblies that is derived from the requirement for another product.

Example:

A finished product requires:

- 2 electronic components.
- 1 housing.
- 4 screws.

If the production plan requires 10,000 finished products:

| Component | Quantity per finished product | Total requirement |
|---|---:|---:|
| Electronic component | 2 | 20,000 |
| Housing | 1 | 10,000 |
| Screws | 4 | 40,000 |

The demand for these materials depends on the production requirement of the finished product.

This is the fundamental idea behind dependent demand.

We will discuss its relationship with production and material planning in later learning topics.

### J. Unconstrained Demand

Unconstrained demand represents the customer demand expected without limiting it based on the company's available supply, production capacity, inventory, or other internal constraints.

Example:

Customers are expected to require 50,000 units next month.

However, the manufacturer can currently produce only 40,000 units.

The unconstrained demand remains 50,000 units.

Hindi natin dapat babaan ang demand forecast to 40,000 just because iyon lang ang kaya nating i-produce.

Otherwise, the company may hide the actual supply problem.

### K. Constrained Demand

Constrained demand is a demand plan or demand quantity adjusted to reflect an agreed constraint, business limitation, or allocation decision.

Terminology varies between organizations, so it is important to understand the company's planning definitions.

For example:

- Unconstrained demand: 50,000 units.
- Available supply: 40,000 units.
- Agreed customer allocation: 40,000 units.

The company may establish an executable allocation of 40,000 units.

But the original customer requirement remains 50,000 units.

**Important distinction:** Constrained demand should not be confused with actual customer demand. The company must retain visibility of unmet requirements.

### Complete comparison of demand information types

| Type | Main question it answers |
|---|---|
| Actual demand | What did customers actually require? |
| Actual sales | What quantity was recorded as sold? |
| Customer orders | What quantities did customers formally order? |
| Open orders | What ordered quantities remain unfulfilled? |
| Backlog | What customer requirements remain outstanding? |
| Historical demand | What was the demand in previous periods? |
| Forecasted demand | What are customers expected to require? |
| Independent demand | What is externally driven by customers or the market? |
| Dependent demand | What is derived from another product's requirements? |
| Unconstrained demand | What would customers require without supply limitations? |
| Constrained demand | What demand quantity or plan reflects an agreed constraint? |

**A critical distinction to remember:**

These are not eleven separate quantities that you should add together.

They are different classifications, views, and measurements of demand.

For example, a customer order can also be part of historical demand, open order backlog, and independent demand.

The same underlying requirement can appear in several reports.

This is why understanding the data definitions is essential.

## 4.3 Understanding Demand Data at Different Levels

Demand information can be classified according to different dimensions.

In professional planning, these dimensions help analysts organize data and determine what level of detail is appropriate for a particular analysis.

Let's explore four major dimensions.

### A. Time Dimension

Demand information can be organized by different time periods.

| Time level | Example | Common application |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | 500 units/day | Short-term operational monitoring |
| Weekly | 3,500 units/week | Production and delivery planning |
| Monthly | 15,000 units/month | Demand planning and S&OP |
| Quarterly | 45,000 units/quarter | Business performance and capacity planning |
| Annual | 180,000 units/year | Strategic planning |

Why does this matter?

Imagine the following monthly demand:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 12,000 |
| February | 12,000 |
| March | 12,000 |

At the monthly level, demand appears stable.

But when you examine daily data, you might discover that customer demand is concentrated in the last five working days of every month.

That difference matters for production scheduling and delivery planning.

**Lesson:** Different planning horizons require different levels of time detail.

### B. Product Dimension

Demand information can be classified according to product hierarchy.

Consider an electronic components manufacturer.

**Product hierarchy example**

1. **Product Category:** Electronic Components
2. **Product Family:** MLCC
3. **Product Model:** Model X / Model Y
4. **Individual SKU:** Model X - 100nF - 16V - Specific Packaging

The different levels serve different purposes.

| Product level | Why it is useful |
|---|---|
| Individual SKU | Detailed inventory and order monitoring |
| Product model | Understanding demand for specific models |
| Product family | Identifying broader demand trends |
| Product category | Evaluating overall business demand |

For example:

The company may experience decreasing demand for Model X but increasing demand for Model Y.

At the MLCC family level, total demand might remain stable.

If the analyst only examines the family total, the changing demand of individual products may be hidden.

This is an example of information loss caused by excessive aggregation.

### C. Customer Dimension

Demand can also be analyzed based on customer classifications.

| Customer level | Example |
|---|---|
| Individual customer | Customer A |
| Customer group | Automotive customers |
| Market segment | Automotive electronics |
| Geographic region | Southeast Asia |

Why is this useful?

Suppose total demand increases by 15%.

At first glance, this appears positive.

But after examining customer-level information, you discover:

- Customer A increased demand by 40%.
- Customer B decreased demand by 20%.
- Customer C remained stable.

This helps identify where the demand change actually originated.

It also helps Sales and Demand Planning understand customer concentration and demand risks.

### D. Quantity and Value Dimension

Demand can be measured using different units.

**1. Units or volume**

Example: 50,000 pieces of MLCC.

**2. Revenue or sales value**

Example: ₱500,000 worth of products.

**3. Order quantity**

Example: Customer ordered 20,000 pieces.

**4. Forecast quantity**

Example: Company expects demand of 25,000 pieces.

These measurements are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Consider:

| Product | Demand volume | Unit selling price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Product A | 10,000 | ₱2 | ₱20,000 |
| Product B | 5,000 | ₱10 | ₱50,000 |
| **Total** | **15,000** | | **₱70,000** |

Product A has higher volume.

Product B generates higher revenue.

Therefore, an analyst must understand whether the business is evaluating demand in terms of physical volume, commercial value, or both.

**Practical S&OP application:** A production team may be more interested in units, while Finance may focus on revenue and profitability.

The demand plan should be capable of supporting both perspectives.

## 4.4 Understanding Demand Patterns

Historical demand is not always constant.

It changes over time because of customer behavior, market conditions, product life cycles, and other factors.

These changes are called demand patterns or demand characteristics.

We will introduce five important patterns.

### A. Trend

A trend is a long-term direction of demand.

It may be:

- Increasing.
- Decreasing.
- Relatively stable.

Example:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 9,000 |
| March | 10,000 |
| April | 11,000 |
| May | 12,000 |

The overall direction is increasing.

Possible reasons:

- More customers are purchasing the product.
- Existing customers are expanding production.
- The product is gaining market acceptance.
- A competitor is losing market share.

**Key point:** A trend describes the general direction of demand over time.

### B. Seasonality

Seasonality refers to recurring demand changes that occur at relatively predictable intervals.

Example:

A manufacturer supplies electronic components used in consumer electronics.

Demand may increase before major holiday shopping seasons because customers prepare for higher product sales.

| Period | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 8,500 |
| March | 9,000 |
| April | 8,500 |
| May | 8,000 |
| June | 8,500 |
| July | 9,000 |
| August | 10,000 |
| September | 12,000 |
| October | 15,000 |
| November | 18,000 |
| December | 14,000 |

This illustrates a possible recurring seasonal pattern.

### Trend versus Seasonality

This distinction is important.

| Trend | Seasonality |
|---|---|
| Long-term direction | Recurring fluctuations |
| Can develop over several years | Repeats at predictable intervals |
| Example: demand grows annually | Example: demand increases every November |
| Does not necessarily repeat | Has a recurring pattern |

A company can experience both at the same time.

For example, demand may increase by 10% every year while also experiencing a seasonal peak every November.

### C. Cyclical Variation

Cyclical variation refers to demand changes associated with broader economic or business cycles.

Examples:

- Economic expansion.
- Economic recession.
- Industry investment cycles.
- Construction industry cycles.
- Semiconductor industry cycles.

Unlike seasonality, cyclical movements do not necessarily follow a fixed calendar schedule.

For example:

During an economic expansion, electronics manufacturers may increase their production and purchasing.

During an economic downturn, they may reduce orders.

The cycle may last several years.

### D. Random Variation

Random variation refers to irregular demand fluctuations that cannot be easily explained by a recurring pattern or known business factor.

Example:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 10,300 |
| March | 9,800 |
| April | 10,100 |
| May | 9,900 |

Small fluctuations occur naturally.

These may result from ordinary changes in customer ordering behavior.

Not every increase or decrease represents a meaningful business trend.

An analyst must avoid overreacting to every movement in the data.

### E. Intermittent Demand

Intermittent demand refers to demand that occurs irregularly, with periods of zero or very low demand between orders.

Example:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 0 |
| February | 0 |
| March | 500 |
| April | 0 |
| May | 0 |
| June | 800 |
| July | 0 |
| August | 0 |
| September | 600 |

This pattern is common in:

- Spare parts.
- Maintenance components.
- Specialized industrial equipment.
- Low-volume replacement products.

Why is this important?

A company may have an average monthly demand of approximately 211 units.

But that doesn't mean customers require 211 units every month.

The actual purchasing pattern is irregular.

This matters when deciding how to maintain inventory and when to expect customer orders.

### Demand pattern summary

| Pattern | Main characteristic |
|---|---|
| Trend | Long-term increase or decrease |
| Seasonality | Repeating demand fluctuations |
| Cyclical | Longer-term economic or industry cycles |
| Random | Irregular, unpredictable variations |
| Intermittent | Demand occurs with periods of zero demand |

For this module, our objective is simply to recognize and interpret these patterns. Forecasting techniques for analyzing them will be covered in Module 3.

## 4.5 Demand Information Quality

Having a large amount of demand data does not automatically mean that the company has good demand information.

Imagine receiving a spreadsheet with 100,000 customer transactions.

However:

- Some orders are duplicated.
- Several product codes are incorrect.
- Some quantities use boxes instead of pieces.
- Delivery dates are outdated.
- Some customer forecasts are missing.

Can you confidently use this information to prepare an S&OP report?

Not yet.

The company needs reliable data.

### Six characteristics of useful demand information

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Information correctly represents the underlying event | Correct order quantity |
| Completeness | Required information is present | No missing customer orders |
| Timeliness | Information is available when needed | Updated orders before the planning cutoff |
| Consistency | Data follows common definitions and formats | All reports use the same unit of measure |
| Relevance | Information is useful for the decision being made | Correct product and planning period |
| Visibility | Relevant stakeholders can access the information | Sales and Planning see the same demand updates |

Let's understand each one more deeply.

**1. Accuracy**

Suppose the customer ordered 10,000 units, but the system recorded 1,000.

The information exists, but it is incorrect.

This may cause the company to underestimate demand by 9,000 units.

**2. Completeness**

Suppose a customer submitted three purchase orders, but only two were included in the demand report.

The report is incomplete.

The company may fail to recognize an additional requirement.

**3. Timeliness**

Suppose the customer increases its order on November 10.

However, the demand report was last updated on November 1.

The analyst may be using outdated information.

**4. Consistency**

Suppose one report records demand in pieces while another records demand in boxes.

If one box contains 1,000 pieces, combining the numbers without conversion will produce incorrect results.

**5. Relevance**

Suppose the analyst is preparing a January demand review.

A five-year-old customer forecast may not be relevant to the current planning decision unless it provides useful historical context.

**6. Visibility**

Suppose Sales knows that Customer A cancelled an order, but Demand Planning still sees the old order quantity.

The departments are working with different versions of reality.

This can create unnecessary inventory or incorrect production decisions.

### Common demand data problems

| Data problem | Possible consequence |
|---|---|
| Missing customer orders | Underestimated demand |
| Duplicate transactions | Overestimated demand |
| Incorrect product codes | Demand assigned to the wrong product |
| Wrong units of measurement | Incorrect demand quantities |
| Outdated forecasts | Poor future demand visibility |
| Incorrect delivery dates | Incorrect time-phased requirements |
| Inconsistent reporting periods | Misleading comparisons |
| Sales data affected by shortages | Demand may be underestimated |

### Important concept: Garbage In, Garbage Out

In data analysis, this is commonly called GIGO.

It means that incorrect or poor-quality input data can produce incorrect analytical results.

For example:

If an analyst calculates a forecast using duplicated sales transactions, the mathematical calculation may be correct.

However, the result is still unreliable because the input data is wrong.

**Technical lesson:** Data validation is part of demand analysis, not merely an administrative activity.

## 4.6 Understanding Demand Adjustments and Exceptions

Demand information changes over time.

A customer may initially forecast 10,000 units but later increase the requirement to 15,000 units.

Another customer may cancel an existing order.

A promotional campaign may create temporary demand.

These are examples of demand adjustments.

### Common reasons for demand adjustments

| Reason | Example | Potential effect |
|---|---|---|
| Customer revision | Order increases from 10,000 to 15,000 | Higher demand |
| Cancellation | Customer cancels 5,000 units | Lower demand |
| New customer requirement | New project needs 8,000 units | Additional demand |
| Promotion | Marketing campaign generates extra orders | Temporary increase |
| Product launch | New product requires components | New demand |
| Market disruption | Customer reduces production | Lower demand |
| Supply shortage | Customer shifts requested delivery dates | Timing changes |

### What is a demand exception?

A demand exception is a significant deviation, unusual change, or unexpected condition in demand information that requires investigation.

Example:

A customer normally orders 10,000 units per month.

Suddenly, the forecast increases to 30,000 units.

Should the analyst immediately accept the 30,000-unit forecast?

No.

The analyst should investigate.

Possible questions:

1. Is this a confirmed customer requirement?
2. Is there a new customer project?
3. Is the increase temporary or permanent?
4. Is this forecast already reflected in customer orders?
5. Is the increase based on actual market information?
6. Could the increase be a data-entry error?

The objective is not to reject unusual demand.

The objective is to understand why it changed.

### Demand adjustment versus demand exception

These concepts are related but different.

- A demand adjustment is a change made to demand information or the demand plan.
- A demand exception is a condition that requires investigation or management attention.

For example:

A customer increases an order by 5,000 units.

That is an order adjustment.

If the increase is 200% above its normal purchasing level, it may also trigger a demand exception.

# 5. PRACTICAL EXAMPLES AND APPLICATIONS

Now let's apply the concepts in a manufacturing scenario.

This is the most important part of the lesson because we will combine the different demand information sources and practice interpreting them as an S&OP analyst.

## 5.1 Manufacturing Business Scenario

Imagine that you work as a Demand and Supply Planning Analyst for an electronic components manufacturer.

The company manufactures MLCC products and supplies different electronics manufacturers.

You are preparing the November S&OP demand review.

The company has three months of historical information and a forecast for the coming months.

For simplicity, we will focus on one product, MLCC Model X, and one customer, Customer A.

All figures below are illustrative.

## 5.2 Historical Demand and Sales Information

The company's historical records show:

| Month | Actual sales | Recorded unfulfilled demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| August | 9,000 | 1,000 |
| September | 10,000 | 2,000 |
| October | 11,000 | 3,000 |

Let's examine October.

Customers purchased 11,000 units, but the company also recorded 3,000 units of unmet requirements.

Therefore, if these are verified quantities for the same period and demand definition:

\[
Actual\ Demand = Actual\ Sales + Unfulfilled\ Demand
\]

\[
=11,000+3,000
\]

\[
\boxed{14,000\ units}
\]

Notice that actual sales alone would suggest 11,000 units.

But the company had evidence of another 3,000 units of demand that it could not fulfill.

This is why demand analysts must investigate the circumstances behind historical sales.

*Assumption:* The recorded unmet demand represents valid lost or unfulfilled requirements and does not duplicate another recorded order.

## 5.3 Customer Orders and Backlog

The company now reviews the current customer order information for November.

| Customer order information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Total accepted November orders | 12,000 |
| Already delivered | 5,000 |
| Remaining open orders | 7,000 |

Calculation:

\[
Open\ Orders=12,000-5,000
\]

\[
\boxed{7,000\ units}
\]

The company still needs to fulfill 7,000 units from those November orders.

However, remember that open orders can include both orders due now and orders scheduled for future delivery.

The delivery schedule must be checked before preparing the time-phased demand plan.

## 5.4 Customer Forecast Information

The customer also submitted its forecast for the next three months.

| Month | Customer forecast |
|---|---:|
| November | 14,000 |
| December | 16,000 |
| January | 18,000 |

The company compares the forecast with the orders already received.

Suppose the November forecast of 14,000 units includes the 12,000 units already ordered.

Then:

\[
Remaining\ Forecast\ Exposure
=
Customer\ Forecast-Confirmed\ Orders
\]

\[
=14,000-12,000
\]

\[
\boxed{2,000\ units}
\]

This means that the customer expects a total of 14,000 units, of which 12,000 are already covered by recorded orders.

The additional 2,000 units represent forecast demand not yet covered by orders.

**Critical warning:** This calculation is valid only if the forecast and orders refer to the same customer, product, delivery period, and unit of measure, and if the forecast includes the orders.

If the customer forecast represents only additional demand on top of existing orders, the calculation would be different.

## 5.5 New Customer Demand and Cancelled Orders

Now the Sales team provides additional information.

- A new customer project is expected to require 5,000 units in December.
- Customer A cancels 2,000 units from its original December requirement.
- A promotional activity is expected to create another 1,000 units of demand in January.

The analyst must determine how these changes should be reflected.

Let's create an adjustment register.

| Month | Demand event | Adjustment |
|---|---|---:|
| December | New customer project | +5,000 |
| December | Customer cancellation | -2,000 |
| January | Promotional activity | +1,000 |

Important: We must verify whether these events are already included in the submitted forecasts or orders.

Otherwise, we risk double-counting.

## 5.6 Consolidating the Demand Information

Let's assume the following:

1. The customer's forecast already includes its existing orders.
2. The December new project is not yet included in the customer forecast.
3. The December cancellation is already reflected in the adjusted forecast.
4. The January promotional demand is not yet included in the forecast.
5. There are no other adjustments.

We can prepare the following demand summary.

| Month | Base forecast | Additional demand | Adjusted demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| November | 14,000 | 0 | 14,000 |
| December | 16,000 | +5,000 | 21,000 |
| January | 18,000 | +1,000 | 19,000 |
| **Total** | **48,000** | **+6,000** | **54,000** |

The adjusted demand for December is:

\[
16,000+5,000=21,000
\]

The adjusted demand for January is:

\[
18,000+1,000=19,000
\]

Total expected demand:

\[
14,000+21,000+19,000
\]

\[
\boxed{54,000\ units}
\]

This represents the adjusted demand view under our stated assumptions.

It is not automatically the final approved demand plan.

It still needs validation and business review.

## 5.7 Why Customer Orders and Forecasts Must Not Be Double-Counted

Let's examine a common mistake.

Suppose:

- Customer forecast = 14,000 units.
- Confirmed customer orders = 12,000 units.

Incorrect calculation:

\[
14,000+12,000=26,000
\]

Why is this wrong?

Because the forecast already includes the 12,000 units of confirmed orders.

The company would count the same demand twice.

Correct calculation:

\[
Total\ Demand=14,000
\]

The composition is:

| Demand component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Existing confirmed orders | 12,000 |
| Forecast demand not yet ordered | 2,000 |
| **Total demand** | **14,000** |

This is one of the most important practical skills for a Demand Planning Analyst.

You must understand the relationship between different data sources before consolidating them.

### Two possible forecast definitions

| Forecast definition | How to interpret it |
|---|---|
| Inclusive forecast | Already includes existing customer orders |
| Incremental forecast | Represents demand additional to existing customer orders |

Never assume which definition applies.

Confirm it with the customer, Sales team, or internal planning process.

## 5.8 Identifying Unusual Demand Changes

Now compare the adjusted demand with the previous demand level.

| Month | Previous reference demand | Adjusted demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| November | 14,000 | 14,000 |
| December | 16,000 | 21,000 |
| January | 18,000 | 19,000 |

December has a significant increase.

Let's calculate its percentage increase relative to the base forecast.

\[
\% Increase =
\frac{Adjusted\ Demand-Base\ Forecast}{Base\ Forecast}
\times100
\]

\[
=
\frac{21,000-16,000}{16,000}
\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{31.25\%}
\]

The December demand increased by 31.25%.

Should this automatically be accepted?

No.

The analyst should verify:

- Is the new customer project confirmed?
- Is the customer project scheduled for December?
- Is the additional quantity realistic?
- Is the customer already ordering from another supplier?
- Is there a risk that the project will be delayed?

The increase should be documented as a demand exception.

## 5.9 Preparing a Demand Information Summary for S&OP

An S&OP analyst should not simply present a large spreadsheet full of numbers.

The analyst should translate the information into something that management can understand and use.

Here is an illustrative summary.

### Demand Information Summary

**Illustrative planning data – MLCC Model X**

**Three-month adjusted demand: 54,000 units**

**December demand increase: 31.25% versus base forecast**

**Monthly demand**

| Month | Adjusted demand |
|---|---:|
| November | 14,000 |
| December | 21,000 |
| January | 19,000 |

**Key observations**

1. November demand is aligned with the existing customer forecast.
2. December demand increases due to a new customer project.
3. January includes an additional promotional requirement.
4. Customer cancellations and existing orders must be reconciled against the latest forecast.
5. December requires further validation because of the significant demand increase.

**Recommended analyst actions**

- Confirm the December project with Sales.
- Verify that customer orders and forecasts do not overlap incorrectly.
- Confirm promotional demand assumptions.
- Communicate the revised demand profile to Supply Planning.
- Track changes before the next planning cycle.

This is the difference between merely collecting data and performing demand analysis.

A good analyst doesn't just report numbers.

A good analyst explains what the numbers mean, why they changed, and what decisions may be necessary.

# 6. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

Although this topic is primarily about understanding and interpreting demand information, there are several basic calculations that an S&OP analyst should be comfortable performing.

These calculations help validate demand data and identify changes that require attention.

## 6.1 Open Order Quantity

**Purpose:** Determine how much of an accepted customer order remains unfulfilled.

\[
Open\ Order=Ordered\ Quantity-Delivered\ Quantity
\]

Example:

- Ordered quantity = 20,000 units.
- Delivered quantity = 12,000 units.

\[
20,000-12,000=\boxed{8,000}
\]

Interpretation: The company still has 8,000 units of open orders.

## 6.2 Backlog Quantity

**Purpose:** Determine the total outstanding unfulfilled customer requirements.

\[
Total\ Backlog=\sum Outstanding\ Order\ Quantities
\]

Example:

| Customer | Backlog |
|---|---:|
| A | 5,000 |
| B | 3,000 |
| C | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** |

Interpretation: The company has 10,000 units of outstanding customer requirements.

Make sure the backlog report follows the company's definition and does not include cancelled or already fulfilled quantities.

## 6.3 Net Demand Adjustments

**Purpose:** Determine the adjusted demand after accounting for validated increases and decreases.

\[
Adjusted\ Demand=Base\ Demand+Increases-Decreases
\]

Example:

| Component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Base demand | 20,000 |
| New project | +5,000 |
| Promotion | +2,000 |
| Customer cancellation | -3,000 |
| **Adjusted demand** | **24,000** |

Calculation:

\[
20,000+5,000+2,000-3,000
\]

\[
\boxed{24,000\ units}
\]

## 6.4 Demand Growth Percentage

**Purpose:** Measure how much demand has increased or decreased relative to a previous period.

\[
Demand\ Growth(\%)=
\frac{Current\ Demand-Previous\ Demand}
{Previous\ Demand}\times100
\]

Example:

Previous demand = 10,000 units.

Current demand = 12,000 units.

\[
\frac{12,000-10,000}{10,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{20\%}
\]

Interpretation: Demand increased by 20%.

A negative result means demand decreased.

## 6.5 Demand Mix Percentage

**Purpose:** Determine how much each product contributes to total demand.

\[
Demand\ Mix(\%)=
\frac{Product\ Demand}{Total\ Demand}\times100
\]

Example:

| Product | Demand |
|---|---:|
| Model A | 30,000 |
| Model B | 20,000 |
| Model C | 50,000 |
| Total | 100,000 |

For Model A:

\[
\frac{30,000}{100,000}\times100
=\boxed{30\%}
\]

Interpretation: Model A contributes 30% of the company's total demand volume.

This is useful when determining which products contribute most to overall demand.

## 6.6 Forecast Coverage by Customer Orders

**Purpose:** Determine what proportion of the forecast is already covered by recorded customer orders.

\[
Order\ Coverage(\%)=
\frac{Confirmed\ Orders}{Forecast}\times100
\]

Example:

- Forecast = 20,000 units.
- Confirmed orders = 15,000 units.

\[
\frac{15,000}{20,000}\times100
=\boxed{75\%}
\]

Interpretation:

- 75% of forecast demand is covered by recorded orders.
- 25% remains forecast demand not yet covered by orders.

This calculation assumes that the forecast includes those confirmed orders and that the quantities are comparable.

**Important:** This is an order-coverage indicator, not a guarantee that the customer will actually purchase the remaining forecast quantity.

## 6.7 Average Historical Demand

**Purpose:** Understand the average demand over a selected historical period.

\[
Average\ Demand=
\frac{\sum Historical\ Demand}{Number\ of\ Periods}
\]

Example:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 12,000 |
| March | 11,000 |

\[
Average=\frac{10,000+12,000+11,000}{3}
\]

\[
\boxed{11,000\ units/month}
\]

Interpretation: Average historical demand was 11,000 units per month.

However, be careful.

An average can hide significant changes, seasonality, and intermittent demand.

It should not automatically be treated as the forecast for the next month.

# 7. BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE AND DECISION-MAKING

Now let's look at this topic from the perspective of an actual S&OP professional.

Why does management care about demand information?

Because demand information influences nearly every major supply chain decision.

## 7.1 Impact on Demand Planning

Demand Planning needs to establish a reliable understanding of expected customer requirements.

For example:

The customer forecast indicates 20,000 units next month.

However, Sales reports that a customer project has been postponed.

The analyst needs to investigate whether the forecast should be adjusted.

Without accurate information, the company may develop an unrealistic demand plan.

**Analyst's responsibility:** Consolidate demand information, validate assumptions, investigate unusual changes, and communicate the expected demand profile.

## 7.2 Impact on Supply Planning

Supply Planning uses the demand plan to evaluate whether the company can meet expected requirements.

Example:

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Expected demand | 50,000 |
| Available inventory | 10,000 |
| Planned production | 25,000 |
| Expected available supply | 35,000 |
| Potential supply gap | 15,000 |

Assuming the inventory and planned production are available within the required delivery periods:

\[
Supply\ Gap=50,000-35,000
\]

\[
\boxed{15,000\ units}
\]

The company now has a potential supply shortage.

But notice where the calculation begins.

It begins with demand information.

If the demand information is incorrect, the supply gap calculation will also be unreliable.

## 7.3 Impact on Inventory Management

Demand information helps determine how much inventory a company should maintain.

Consider two situations.

**Situation A: Demand is increasing.**

- Previous monthly demand: 10,000 units.
- Current expected demand: 15,000 units.

The company may need to evaluate whether its inventory position is sufficient.

**Situation B: Demand is declining.**

- Previous monthly demand: 15,000 units.
- Current expected demand: 8,000 units.

If production and purchasing continue based on the old demand level, excess inventory may accumulate.

Therefore, demand information supports both shortage prevention and excess inventory prevention.

## 7.4 Impact on Procurement

Procurement uses demand information to understand future material requirements.

Example:

The manufacturer expects to produce 20,000 electronic components.

Each finished product requires a certain quantity of raw materials.

Procurement needs visibility of:

- Expected production requirements.
- Existing material inventory.
- Open purchase orders.
- Supplier lead times.
- Upcoming demand changes.

If the demand plan increases significantly, Procurement may need to coordinate with suppliers earlier.

## 7.5 Impact on Production Planning

Production needs to understand what products must be manufactured and when they are required.

Example:

| Month | Product A | Product B |
|---|---:|---:|
| November | 10,000 | 15,000 |
| December | 18,000 | 12,000 |
| January | 20,000 | 10,000 |

Even if total demand remains relatively similar, the product mix changes.

This may affect:

- Machine allocation.
- Production scheduling.
- Changeover requirements.
- Labor requirements.
- Production capacity.
- Material availability.

This is why product-level demand visibility is important.

## 7.6 Impact on Customer Service

Demand information also affects the company's ability to meet customer expectations.

If customer requirements are not visible or updated on time, the company may:

- Miss requested delivery dates.
- Allocate products incorrectly.
- Prioritize the wrong customers.
- Fail to prepare for upcoming orders.

Good demand visibility helps the company anticipate customer requirements rather than constantly reacting to problems.

## 7.7 Demand Information in the S&OP Process

Now let's connect everything to S&OP.

The Demand Review is where the organization evaluates its demand picture.

The objective is to establish an informed view of expected demand before determining how the organization can respond.

Different departments contribute different information.

| Department | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer requirements, commitments, and account updates |
| Demand Planning | Historical analysis, forecasts, and demand consolidation |
| Marketing | Promotions, campaigns, and market activities |
| Product Management | Product launches and phase-outs |
| Finance | Revenue implications and financial assumptions |
| Supply Planning | Supply constraints and feasibility feedback |

The Demand Review should answer questions such as:

1. What is our expected demand?
2. What changed compared with the previous planning cycle?
3. Which customers are driving the changes?
4. Which demand increases or decreases require investigation?
5. Are there new customer projects?
6. What are the major demand risks and opportunities?
7. What assumptions need management validation?

### A simplified S&OP information flow

**Demand Information Sources**

Customer orders · Historical sales · Forecasts · Sales input · Market signals

↓

**Data Consolidation and Validation**

Remove duplicates · Check accuracy · Reconcile changes · Validate assumptions

↓

**Demand Analysis and Demand Plan**

Historical trends · Customer requirements · Forecast · Adjustments

↓

**S&OP Demand Review**

Risks · Opportunities · Changes · Business assumptions

↓

**Cross-functional Planning**

Supply · Inventory · Procurement · Production · Finance

One important professional concept:

**An agreed demand plan is not simply the sum of customer orders and forecasts.**

It is a validated, consolidated view of expected demand that considers historical evidence, current customer requirements, future business opportunities, and documented assumptions.

It also requires business judgment.

For example, a customer may submit a forecast showing 100,000 units.

But Sales may know that the customer's project has been delayed.

The analyst should not blindly accept the 100,000 units.

The forecast should be reviewed using available evidence and discussed with the relevant stakeholders.

This is how data analysis and business knowledge work together in S&OP.

# 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Before moving to the knowledge check, let's consolidate the most important lessons.

## 8.1 The fundamental concepts

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Demand Information | Data and insights used to understand customer requirements |
| Customer Orders | Formal customer purchasing requests |
| Actual Sales | Recorded quantity sold |
| Actual Demand | Customer requirements, including potentially unfulfilled demand |
| Historical Demand | Demand information from previous periods |
| Backlog | Outstanding unfulfilled customer requirements |
| Forecast | Estimated future demand |
| Independent Demand | Demand driven by external customer or market requirements |
| Dependent Demand | Demand derived from another product's requirements |
| Unconstrained Demand | Demand before applying internal supply limitations |
| Constrained Demand | Demand plan or quantity reflecting agreed constraints |

## 8.2 Six concepts you should never confuse

1. **Actual sales are not automatically equal to actual demand.** Shortages and lost sales can make actual sales lower than customer requirements.

2. **Customer orders are not the same as forecasts.** Orders represent recorded purchasing requests; forecasts represent expected future requirements.

3. **Open orders are not automatically overdue.** Some open orders are scheduled for future delivery.

4. **Backlog is not additional demand to be added blindly to a forecast.** It may already be represented in the forecast.

5. **Historical sales do not always represent historical demand accurately.** Supply shortages, stockouts, and other factors can distort the data.

6. **Unconstrained demand should remain visible even when supply cannot fulfill it.** Otherwise, the company may hide a real business problem.

## 8.3 What an S&OP analyst should be able to do

At the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Identify different sources of demand information.
- Distinguish actual sales, demand, orders, backlog, and forecasts.
- Organize demand information by time, product, customer, and quantity.
- Recognize common demand patterns.
- Identify demand data quality problems.
- Reconcile orders, forecasts, and demand adjustments.
- Detect unusual demand changes.
- Prepare a meaningful demand summary for S&OP.
