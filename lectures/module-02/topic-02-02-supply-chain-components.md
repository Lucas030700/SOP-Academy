# Module 2 – Supply Chain Management
## Topic 2.2: Supply Chain Components

**Main learning focus:** Understanding the different components of a supply chain, their responsibilities, and how they work together to move materials from suppliers into finished products and ultimately deliver them to customers.

For this lesson, gagamit tayo ng manufacturing environment, particularly an electronic components manufacturer, para madaling ma-connect ang concepts sa actual business operations.

---

# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What are we going to learn?

Sa Topic 2.1, pinag-aralan natin kung ano ang Supply Chain Management (SCM).

Naintindihan natin na ang supply chain ay isang interconnected network of organizations, people, resources, activities, and processes involved in delivering products or services to customers.

Ngayon, ang next question natin:

**Ano-ano ba talaga ang bumubuo sa isang supply chain?**

Hindi kasi isang department lang ang responsible sa supply chain. Maraming departments, companies, facilities, systems, at activities ang kailangang mag-coordinate para makagawa at makapag-deliver ng isang product.

For example, bago makarating sa customer ang isang electronic component:

1. May supplier na magbibigay ng raw materials.
2. May procurement team na bibili ng required materials.
3. May manufacturing team na magko-convert ng materials into finished products.
4. May warehouse na magma-manage ng inventory.
5. May logistics team na mag-aayos ng transportation.
6. May distributor or sales channel na magdadala ng product sa customer.

Lahat ng ito ay bahagi ng supply chain.

## 1.2 Why is this topic important?

Imagine na may customer na nag-order ng 100,000 electronic components.

May available production capacity ang factory, pero kulang ang raw materials.

Possible ba na makumpleto ang customer order?

Hindi, kahit available ang machines at manpower.

Another situation:

May raw materials, may production capacity, at natapos na ang manufacturing. Pero walang available truck or transportation service.

Possible bang makarating agad ang products sa customer?

Hindi rin.

This demonstrates an important principle:

**A supply chain is only as effective as the coordination between its components.**

Hindi sapat na magaling ang isang department. Kailangan connected at synchronized ang buong operation.

## 1.3 Connection to S&OP

Importante ito sa S&OP dahil ang S&OP ay hindi lang tungkol sa forecasting or production planning.

It connects the different business functions to answer one important question:

**Can our company realistically meet the expected customer demand using our available resources?**

Para masagot ito, kailangang maintindihan natin ang capabilities and limitations ng bawat supply chain component.

For example:

| Supply chain component | Question considered in S&OP |
|---|---|
| Suppliers | Available ba ang required materials? |
| Procurement | Kailan mako-confirm at mako-cover ang material requirements? |
| Manufacturing | Kaya ba ng production capacity ang demand? |
| Warehousing | Saan at gaano karaming inventory ang available? |
| Logistics | Kaya bang ma-deliver ang products on time? |
| Customers | Ano ang expected demand at delivery requirements? |
| Finance | Affordable and financially viable ba ang proposed plan? |

Ang objective natin ay makita ang supply chain as one connected business system, hindi bilang magkakahiwalay na departments.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Imagine that you are running a bakery

Let's use a simple example.

May bakery ka na gumagawa at nagbebenta ng 1,000 loaves of bread every day.

Para makapag-operate ang bakery, kailangan mo ng iba't ibang resources and activities.

**How the bakery supply chain works**

1. **Suppliers**

   May supplier na nagbibigay ng flour, sugar, yeast, eggs, and packaging materials.

2. **Procurement**

   Bumibili at nag-o-order ang bakery ng required ingredients.

3. **Manufacturing / Production**

   Ginagamit ng bakers ang ingredients para gumawa ng bread.

4. **Warehousing and Inventory**

   Ini-store ang ingredients at finished bread, at mino-monitor ang available stocks.

5. **Transportation and Logistics**

   Dinadala ang bread sa mga stores or customers.

6. **Distribution and Customers**

   Ibinebenta ang bread through stores, resellers, or direct customer orders.

Ngayon, imagine na may problema sa isang component.

- Kung walang flour supplier, walang ingredients.
- Kung hindi nag-order ang procurement, mauubusan ng ingredients.
- Kung walang production workers, walang bread na magagawa.
- Kung walang warehouse management, maaaring masira o mawala ang inventory.
- Kung walang transportation, hindi makarating ang bread sa stores.
- Kung walang customers, walang demand na pagbabasehan ng production.

Notice something?

**Each component depends on the performance of other components.**

Ito ang pinaka-importanteng concept na gusto kong maintindihan mo bago tayo mag-dive sa technical discussions.

## 2.2 The supply chain is a connected system

Hindi ibig sabihin na kapag successful ang isang department, successful na rin ang buong supply chain.

For example:

- Procurement may successfully purchase materials at the lowest price.
- Manufacturing may successfully achieve its production target.
- Warehouse may successfully maintain accurate inventory records.
- Logistics may successfully minimize transportation expenses.

Pero kung hindi tugma ang kanilang activities sa actual customer requirements, maaari pa ring magkaroon ng excess inventory, stockouts, delayed deliveries, at unnecessary costs.

Kaya ang objective ay hindi simply to optimize each department individually.

Ang objective ay i-optimize ang overall supply chain performance.

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 What are Supply Chain Components?

In technical terms, supply chain components are the interconnected functions, resources, processes, facilities, and organizational elements that enable the planning, sourcing, production, storage, movement, and delivery of goods or services.

Simple interpretation:

Ito ang mga pangunahing building blocks na kailangan para gumana ang supply chain.

May iba't ibang ways to classify these components. Para sa lesson natin, gagamit tayo ng practical manufacturing-oriented structure.

## 3.2 The major supply chain components

**The supply chain ecosystem**

*Simplified manufacturing supply chain structure*

**SUPPLIERS**  
Provide raw materials and components

↓

**PROCUREMENT**  
Purchases and secures required materials

↓

**MANUFACTURING / PRODUCTION**  
Converts materials into finished products

↓

**WAREHOUSING AND INVENTORY**  
Stores and controls materials and products

↓

**TRANSPORTATION AND LOGISTICS**  
Moves products between locations

↓

**DISTRIBUTION AND CUSTOMERS**  
Makes products available to end users

Supporting functions work across the entire supply chain:

Sales • Demand Planning • Supply Planning • Finance • Quality • IT

Important: This diagram shows a simplified flow, not a universal organizational structure. For example, procurement can report to supply chain, finance, or a separate purchasing organization. Warehousing can also be integrated with logistics.

## 3.3 Difference between Participants, Activities, and Components

Bago natin isa-isahin ang components, kailangan nating linawin ang tatlong terms na ito.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Participants | Sino ang involved? | Supplier, manufacturer, distributor, customer |
| Activities | Anong ginagawa? | Purchasing, production, storing, transporting |
| Components | Anong functional parts ang bumubuo sa supply chain? | Procurement, manufacturing, warehousing, logistics |

Example:

Let's say may isang manufacturing company.

- The supplier is a participant.
- Purchasing raw materials is an activity.
- Procurement is a supply chain component or function.

Magkakaugnay ang tatlo, pero hindi sila interchangeable.

**Important distinction:** Hindi lahat ng component ay isang separate company or department. A component may be a function performed by one team, multiple teams, or an external service provider.

## 3.4 Why do supply chain structures vary?

Hindi pare-pareho ang supply chain ng lahat ng companies.

Compare natin ang dalawang businesses.

| Aspect | Food manufacturer | Electronic components manufacturer |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials | Flour, sugar, oil | Ceramic powder, metals, chemicals |
| Main concern | Freshness and shelf life | Material specifications and quality |
| Production | Baking, packaging | Mixing, casting, stacking, firing |
| Inventory concern | Expiration and spoilage | Material traceability and stock availability |
| Delivery | Often frequent and time-sensitive | Based on customer schedules and order requirements |

Both companies need suppliers, procurement, manufacturing, inventory, and logistics.

However, their supply chain priorities and operational requirements differ.

Factors affecting supply chain structure include:

1. Industry and product characteristics.
2. Product shelf life and storage requirements.
3. Manufacturing process complexity.
4. Supplier location and availability.
5. Customer location and delivery requirements.
6. Company size and organizational structure.
7. Outsourcing strategy.
8. Technology and information systems.

For example, a company that outsources manufacturing may have no physical production facility of its own. However, production is still part of its supply chain through its contract manufacturer.

# 4. Detailed Discussion

Now, let's discuss the individual components in greater detail.

## 4.1 Suppliers and Sourcing

### A. What is a supplier?

A supplier is an individual or organization that provides materials, components, equipment, or services required by a business.

Simple explanation:

Sila ang pinanggagalingan ng resources na kailangan ng company para makapag-operate.

There are three common supplier categories:

| Supplier type | What they provide | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Raw material supplier | Basic materials | Barium titanate powder, metals, plastic resin |
| Component supplier | Partially manufactured items | Electronic chips, connectors, capacitors |
| Service provider | Services needed by the company | Transportation, equipment maintenance, warehousing |

### B. What is sourcing?

Sourcing refers to the process of identifying, evaluating, and selecting suppliers that can meet the company's requirements.

Hindi basta pinakamura ang supplier ang pipiliin.

Kailangan ding i-consider ang:

- Price and total acquisition cost.
- Product quality.
- Delivery reliability.
- Production capacity.
- Lead time.
- Financial stability.
- Geographic location.
- Risk of supply disruption.

### C. Supplier lead time

Supplier lead time is the time required between placing an order and receiving the materials.

Example:

- Purchase order date: November 1
- Material arrival: November 16

Supplier lead time = 15 days.

Bakit important ito?

Kasi kung 15 days ang lead time ng supplier, hindi ka basta-basta makaka-order today at makaka-expect na available agad ang material tomorrow.

### D. Supplier reliability

Supplier reliability refers to the supplier's ability to consistently meet agreed delivery, quantity, and quality requirements.

For example:

| Month | Required delivery | Actual delivery |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| February | 10,000 | 9,000 |
| March | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| April | 10,000 | 8,000 |

Kahit may supplier agreement, hindi ibig sabihin na laging available ang required materials.

### E. Single sourcing vs. multiple sourcing

**Single sourcing:** One supplier is used for a particular material or component.

Advantages:
- Easier supplier coordination.
- Potential volume discounts.
- More concentrated supplier relationship.

Risks:
- High dependency on one supplier.
- Greater exposure to supply disruption.

**Multiple sourcing:** Two or more qualified suppliers provide the same material or component.

Advantages:
- Reduced dependency.
- Alternative supply options.
- Potentially better supply flexibility.

Risks:
- More complicated coordination.
- Different supplier lead times and quality performance.
- Potentially lower volume discounts.

**S&OP connection:** Supplier availability and reliability influence how much material the company can realistically secure to support the production plan.

## 4.2 Procurement / Purchasing

### A. What is procurement?

Procurement is the broader business function responsible for obtaining the materials, products, and services needed by an organization.

It includes supplier selection, sourcing strategy, negotiations, purchasing, supplier coordination, and contract management.

### B. Procurement vs. Purchasing

Magka-related sila, pero hindi exactly the same.

| Procurement | Purchasing |
|---|---|
| Broader process | Specific transactional activity |
| Focuses on obtaining the best overall value | Focuses on ordering and buying |
| Includes sourcing and supplier evaluation | Includes purchase orders and order processing |
| Includes supplier relationship management | Includes order follow-up and purchasing transactions |

Think of procurement as the overall strategy and purchasing as one of the activities within that strategy.

### C. Procurement process

**Typical procurement workflow**

1. **Identify requirements**

   Determine what materials or services are needed.

2. **Supplier identification and evaluation**

   Identify qualified suppliers and assess their capabilities.

3. **Request quotation and negotiation**

   Obtain prices, lead times, terms, and delivery commitments.

4. **Purchase requisition (PR)**

   Internal request to purchase required items.

5. **Purchase order (PO)**

   Formal order issued to the supplier.

6. **Supplier confirmation and monitoring**

   Confirm quantities, promised dates, and order status.

7. **Receiving and inspection**

   Receive materials and verify quantity and quality.

8. **Invoice verification and payment**

   Reconcile purchasing documents and process payment.

Two important terms:

- **Purchase Requisition (PR):** Internal request asking for authorization to purchase.
- **Purchase Order (PO):** Official purchasing document sent to the supplier.

Hindi automatic na kapag may PR ay may PO na agad. May approval and purchasing controls muna, depending on company policy.

### D. Procurement constraints

Even if the production plan requires 50,000 units of material, procurement may not be able to secure the entire quantity.

Possible reasons:

- Supplier capacity limitation.
- Long supplier lead time.
- Minimum order quantity (MOQ).
- Budget limitations.
- Material allocation restrictions.
- Importation or customs delays.

**S&OP connection:** A production plan is only feasible if procurement can secure the required materials within the required timeframe.

## 4.3 Manufacturing / Production

### A. What is manufacturing?

Manufacturing is the process of converting raw materials and components into finished products using labor, equipment, technology, and production processes.

For an electronic components manufacturer, the process may involve:

1. Material preparation.
2. Mixing.
3. Casting.
4. Stacking.
5. Cutting.
6. Firing.
7. Electrical testing.
8. Sorting.
9. Final inspection and packaging.

### B. Production capacity

Production capacity refers to the amount of output a production system can produce within a given period under specified operating conditions.

Example:

A production line has a theoretical capacity of 10,000 units per day.

However, actual production may be lower because of:

- Equipment downtime.
- Changeovers.
- Manpower availability.
- Quality defects.
- Material shortages.
- Process bottlenecks.

This means theoretical capacity and actual available capacity are not necessarily the same.

### C. Work-in-Process (WIP)

WIP refers to materials or products that have entered the manufacturing process but have not yet become completed finished goods.

Example:

- Raw ceramic material: Raw material inventory.
- Material currently undergoing stacking: WIP.
- Completed and inspected electronic components: Finished goods inventory.

Why is WIP important?

Because it represents resources that are already committed to production but are not yet available for customer shipment as finished products.

Too much WIP may indicate:
- Production imbalance.
- Bottlenecks.
- Excessive waiting time.
- Poor production scheduling.

### D. Bottlenecks

A bottleneck is a process or resource that limits the throughput of the entire production system.

Example:

| Process | Capacity per day |
|---|---:|
| Casting | 12,000 units |
| Stacking | 10,000 units |
| Cutting | 9,000 units |
| Firing | 7,000 units |
| Sorting | 11,000 units |

The firing process has the lowest capacity.

Therefore, firing is the current bottleneck, assuming these capacities refer to comparable good-unit output and the processes are sequential.

Even if casting can produce 12,000 units per day, the factory cannot sustainably deliver 12,000 finished units per day if firing can only process 7,000.

**S&OP connection:** Production capacity determines whether the company can meet its expected demand within the planning horizon.

## 4.4 Warehousing and Inventory Management

### A. What is warehousing?

Warehousing is the activity of receiving, storing, protecting, controlling, and managing materials and products.

A warehouse is not simply a storage room.

It is an operational facility that helps maintain material availability and supports production and customer delivery.

### B. Three major inventory categories

| Inventory type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Raw Materials (RM) | Materials waiting for production | Ceramic powder, chemicals |
| Work-in-Process (WIP) | Materials undergoing manufacturing | Cast sheets, stacked units |
| Finished Goods (FG) | Completed products ready for shipment | Inspected electronic components |

### C. Main warehouse activities

1. Receiving: Accepting incoming materials.
2. Inspection: Checking quantity and quality.
3. Put-away: Moving materials into assigned storage locations.
4. Storage: Maintaining materials under required conditions.
5. Inventory control: Monitoring stock quantities and status.
6. Issuance: Releasing materials to production.
7. Picking and packing: Preparing finished goods for shipment.
8. Shipping: Releasing products for delivery.

### D. Inventory availability vs. physical inventory

This is an important distinction.

Imagine the system indicates that the warehouse has 10,000 units.

But:
- 2,000 units are under quality inspection.
- 1,000 units are reserved for another customer.
- 500 units are damaged.

The total physical quantity is 10,000, but the available inventory is only 6,500 units.

| Inventory status | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Total physical inventory | 10,000 |
| Under inspection | 2,000 |
| Reserved | 1,000 |
| Damaged | 500 |
| **Available inventory** | **6,500** |

This explains why physical inventory and usable inventory are not always equal.

### E. Inventory as a buffer

Inventory acts as a buffer against uncertainty.

For example:
- If supplier delivery is delayed, raw material inventory can support production.
- If production is temporarily disrupted, finished goods inventory can support customer orders.
- If demand suddenly increases, available inventory can help fulfill additional orders.

However, inventory is not free.

Excess inventory creates:
- Storage costs.
- Capital tied up in materials.
- Obsolescence risk.
- Potential deterioration.
- Additional handling requirements.

The objective is not to maximize inventory.

It is to maintain the appropriate inventory level to balance availability, cost, and risk.

## 4.5 Transportation and Logistics

### A. What is logistics?

Logistics is the planning, execution, and control of the movement and storage of goods, services, and related information from the point of origin to the point of consumption.

Simple explanation:

Logistics makes sure that materials and products move to the correct location, at the correct time, and in the correct condition.

### B. Transportation vs. Logistics

Transportation is only one part of logistics.

| Transportation | Logistics |
|---|---|
| Focuses on moving goods | Covers broader movement and storage activities |
| Uses trucks, ships, planes, trains | Includes transportation, warehousing, handling, and delivery coordination |
| Concerned with routes and transit | Concerned with the overall flow of goods |

### C. Inbound and outbound logistics

**Inbound logistics** refers to the movement of materials from suppliers into the manufacturing facility.

Example: Delivery of ceramic powder from a supplier to the factory.

**Outbound logistics** refers to the movement of finished products from the factory or warehouse toward customers or distribution centers.

Example: Shipping finished electronic components to a customer's manufacturing facility.

### D. Transportation modes

| Mode | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Road | Flexible and accessible | Traffic and road disruptions |
| Sea | Suitable for large international shipments | Longer transit time |
| Air | Fast | Usually more expensive |
| Rail | Efficient for large land-based shipments | Limited route availability |

### E. Logistics performance

Important logistics considerations include:

- Freight cost.
- Transit time.
- Delivery reliability.
- Shipment damage.
- Transportation availability.
- Customs clearance.
- Delivery scheduling.

**S&OP connection:** Even if production and inventory plans are feasible, delivery constraints can still prevent the company from meeting customer commitments.

## 4.6 Distribution Channels and Customers

### A. What is distribution?

Distribution is the process of making finished products available to customers through the appropriate channels and locations.

The product does not always travel directly from manufacturer to end customer.

There may be intermediaries.

### B. Distribution participants

| Participant | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Manufacturer | Produces the product |
| Distributor | Buys and distributes products to other businesses or sales channels |
| Wholesaler | Purchases goods in bulk and resells them |
| Retailer | Sells products directly to consumers |
| End customer | Uses or consumes the product |

### C. Direct vs. indirect distribution

**Direct distribution:**

Manufacturer → Customer

**Indirect distribution:**

Manufacturer → Distributor → Retailer → End customer

The choice depends on the company's market, customer base, geographic coverage, cost, and distribution strategy.

### D. Customer requirements

Customer requirements influence almost every upstream activity.

For example, a customer requests:

- 50,000 electronic components.
- Delivery within 30 days.
- Specific quality specifications.
- Specific packaging requirements.

The company must determine:

1. Can existing finished goods inventory fulfill the order?
2. If not, can production meet the quantity?
3. Are sufficient materials available?
4. Can suppliers deliver any missing materials on time?
5. Can the logistics network meet the delivery date?

Notice that the customer's requirements travel upstream as information, while materials and finished products generally travel downstream.

This is an important supply chain principle.

## 4.7 Supporting Supply Chain Functions

Hindi lahat ng supply chain components ay directly involved sa physical movement of materials.

May supporting functions na responsible for planning, coordination, financial control, quality, and information.

| Supporting function | Primary responsibility | Supply chain contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Sales and Marketing | Understand market and customer needs | Provides demand information and customer requirements |
| Demand Planning | Estimate future demand | Creates demand forecasts |
| Supply Planning | Evaluate supply capabilities | Develops feasible supply plans |
| Finance | Manage financial resources and costs | Evaluates financial impact and feasibility |
| Quality Assurance (QA) | Ensure product and material quality | Controls quality acceptance and release |
| Information Technology (IT) | Maintain business systems and data | Enables information visibility and coordination |

Let's understand some important distinctions.

**Sales and Marketing**

They help the company understand what customers want and what products may be needed.

**Demand Planning**

It estimates how much customers are expected to order or consume within a future period.

**Supply Planning**

It determines how the company can meet that demand using available inventory, suppliers, production capacity, and other resources.

**Finance**

It evaluates whether the supply chain plan makes financial sense.

For example, increasing production may require additional working capital, overtime costs, or new equipment investments.

**Quality Assurance**

It ensures that materials and products meet the required quality standards.

Materials that are physically present may still be unavailable for production if they have not passed quality inspection.

**Information Technology**

Systems such as ERP, MRP, WMS, and TMS help different departments share and manage supply chain information.

| System | Main purpose |
|---|---|
| ERP | Integrates business processes and information |
| MRP | Calculates material requirements |
| WMS | Manages warehouse activities |
| TMS | Supports transportation planning and execution |

These systems are important because supply chain decisions depend on accurate and timely information.

# 5. Practical Examples and Applications

Now that we understand the individual components, let's connect them through an actual manufacturing business scenario.

Ang goal natin dito ay makita kung paano nagiging isang continuous process ang lahat ng components.

## 5.1 Example: Electronic Components Manufacturing Company

Imagine a company called **ABC Electronics Manufacturing**, which produces Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors (MLCCs).

The company receives orders from electronics manufacturers that use capacitors in their products.

Let's assume the following business requirements:

**Illustrative business scenario**

| Item | Assumption |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 100,000 finished units per month |
| Production requirement | 100,000 good units per month |
| Raw material lead time | 20 days |
| Customer delivery target | 30 days |

All figures are illustrative assumptions for learning purposes, not actual company operating data.

### A. Raw material requirements

To manufacture 100,000 finished units, ABC Electronics needs the following materials.

| Material | Requirement |
|---|---:|
| Ceramic powder | 500 kg |
| Internal electrode material | 200 kg |
| External electrode material | 100 kg |
| Binder and additives | 50 kg |
| Packaging materials | 100,000 sets |

These requirements are determined based on the product's Bill of Materials (BOM).

**Bill of Materials (BOM)** is a structured list of materials and quantities required to manufacture a product.

For example, kung kailangan ng 5 grams of ceramic powder per finished unit:

\[
100,000 \times 5g = 500,000g
\]

Therefore:

\[
500,000g \div 1,000 = 500kg
\]

This tells procurement how much material needs to be secured, subject to inventory availability, yield assumptions, and production requirements.

### B. Supplier and procurement activities

The company has identified the following suppliers:

| Material | Supplier | Lead time |
|---|---|---:|
| Ceramic powder | Supplier A | 20 days |
| Electrode material | Supplier B | 15 days |
| Binder | Supplier C | 10 days |
| Packaging | Supplier D | 7 days |

The procurement team checks:

- Required quantity.
- Existing inventory.
- Supplier availability.
- Confirmed delivery dates.
- Purchase order status.

Suppose the warehouse already has 100 kg of ceramic powder.

Required quantity = 500 kg.

Available usable inventory = 100 kg.

Therefore, the remaining procurement requirement is:

\[
500-100=400kg
\]

Procurement needs to secure another 400 kg.

### C. Production planning and manufacturing

Once materials are available, supply planning evaluates whether manufacturing can produce the required quantity.

Assume the factory has the following daily production capacities:

| Process | Daily capacity |
|---|---:|
| Mixing | 5,000 |
| Casting | 5,000 |
| Stacking | 4,500 |
| Cutting | 4,000 |
| Firing | 3,500 |
| Sorting | 4,000 |

The lowest-capacity stage is firing, at 3,500 units per day.

If the factory operates for 26 days:

\[
3,500 \times 26=91,000
\]

Its theoretical monthly output based on this limiting stage is only 91,000 units, assuming comparable capacity measurements and no additional losses.

But the customer requires 100,000 good units.

This creates a supply-demand gap of:

\[
100,000-91,000=9,000
\]

The company must evaluate alternatives:

- Increase firing capacity.
- Introduce additional shifts.
- Reduce downtime.
- Improve process efficiency.
- Outsource part of the production.
- Negotiate a different delivery schedule.

This is where supply planning becomes important.

### D. WIP and finished goods inventory

As products move through manufacturing, they become WIP.

Example:

| Manufacturing status | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Completed casting | 20,000 |
| Completed stacking | 15,000 |
| Completed cutting | 10,000 |
| Undergoing firing | 8,000 |
| Completed final sorting | 5,000 |

These are illustrative quantities at different process stages. They should not simply be added together because they may represent different portions or stages of the same production flow.

After final inspection and acceptance, products are transferred into finished goods inventory.

Suppose the company completes 95,000 good units.

Customer demand is 100,000 units.

The company still has a shortage of 5,000 units unless existing usable finished goods inventory can cover the difference.

### E. Warehouse operations

The warehouse manages:

- Receiving raw materials.
- Verifying received quantities.
- Storing materials.
- Issuing materials to production.
- Tracking WIP and finished goods.
- Preparing finished goods for shipment.

The warehouse also needs inventory visibility.

For example, the system must distinguish between:

- Available inventory.
- Reserved inventory.
- Quality-hold inventory.
- Damaged inventory.
- Inventory already allocated to production.

Without this distinction, the company may believe it has enough stock when the actual usable quantity is insufficient.

### F. Distribution and delivery

Once finished goods are available, the logistics team coordinates transportation.

Suppose:

- Finished goods available: 100,000 units.
- Customer order: 100,000 units.
- Shipment requirement: November 30.
- Transportation transit time: 3 days.

The logistics team needs to arrange shipment early enough to meet the customer's required delivery date.

The job is not complete simply because production is finished.

The customer must receive the products according to the agreed delivery requirements.

## 5.2 Three Supply Chain Disruption Scenarios

Ito ang mas importanteng part ng practical application.

Sa actual operations, hindi laging smooth ang supply chain. May disruptions, constraints, and unexpected changes.

Let's examine three scenarios.

### Scenario 1: Supplier delays raw material delivery

**Situation:**

ABC Electronics requires 500 kg of ceramic powder.

The supplier originally committed to deliver the required 400 kg on November 10.

However, the supplier informs the company that delivery will be delayed by 10 days.

New expected arrival: November 20.

**What happens?**

**Impact sequence**

1. **Supplier**

   Delayed delivery of ceramic powder.

2. **Procurement**

   Must coordinate with the supplier and assess alternative sources.

3. **Warehouse**

   Existing inventory may not be enough to support production.

4. **Manufacturing**

   Production may be delayed or temporarily stopped.

5. **Logistics**

   Finished goods may not be available by the planned shipment date.

6. **Customer**

   Delivery commitment may be at risk.

**Operational consequences:**

- Production downtime.
- Lower production output.
- Increased WIP waiting for materials.
- Possible schedule changes.
- Delayed customer orders.

**Financial consequences:**

- Idle labor and equipment costs.
- Additional procurement or expedited freight costs.
- Potential overtime costs.
- Possible penalties for late delivery.
- Potential lost sales or customer dissatisfaction.

**Possible management actions:**

1. Check whether other qualified suppliers can provide the material.
2. Review existing inventory across warehouses.
3. Prioritize production orders based on customer requirements.
4. Request partial or expedited delivery from the supplier.
5. Evaluate whether available alternative materials are technically approved.
6. Communicate potential delivery changes to affected customers.

**S&OP perspective:**

The supply plan must be revised based on actual material availability.

Hindi puwedeng ipagpatuloy ang original production plan kung hindi naman available ang required raw materials.

### Scenario 2: Manufacturing experiences a production bottleneck

**Situation:**

ABC Electronics has sufficient raw materials.

However, the firing process experiences equipment problems.

Its normal capacity is 3,500 units per day.

Due to equipment issues, its capacity decreases to 2,000 units per day.

Assume the disruption lasts for 10 operating days.

The lost production opportunity is:

\[
(3,500-2,000)\times10
\]

\[
=15,000\text{ units}
\]

The company potentially loses the opportunity to produce 15,000 units during the disruption.

**What happens?**

**Impact sequence**

1. **Manufacturing**

   Firing capacity decreases.

2. **WIP**

   More products accumulate before the constrained process.

3. **Warehouse**

   Finished goods replenishment slows down.

4. **Supply Planning**

   Production capacity assumptions must be revised.

5. **Logistics**

   Available finished goods may be insufficient for planned shipments.

6. **Customers**

   Orders may be delayed or partially fulfilled.

**Operational consequences:**

- Increased WIP accumulation.
- Longer manufacturing lead times.
- Reduced production throughput.
- Potential changes to production schedules.
- Reduced finished goods availability.

**Financial consequences:**

- Higher maintenance expenses.
- Overtime costs.
- Additional equipment rental or outsourcing expenses.
- Possible lost revenue.
- Increased inventory holding costs for accumulated WIP.

**Possible management actions:**

1. Perform equipment troubleshooting and preventive maintenance.
2. Evaluate additional operating shifts.
3. Reallocate production to another qualified machine.
4. Prioritize customer orders.
5. Evaluate outsourcing, if technically and commercially feasible.
6. Review the production schedule and customer commitments.

**S&OP perspective:**

This is a supply-side capacity constraint.

The company needs to compare the available production capacity against expected demand and decide whether to increase capacity, adjust demand commitments, or accept a temporary supply shortage.

### Scenario 3: Finished goods are available, but transportation is delayed

**Situation:**

ABC Electronics successfully manufactures 100,000 units.

All products pass final inspection and are available in the finished goods warehouse.

However, the transportation provider informs the company that shipment will be delayed by five days because of port congestion or truck availability issues.

**What happens?**

**Impact sequence**

1. **Manufacturing**

   Production is completed successfully.

2. **Finished Goods Warehouse**

   Products are available but remain in storage.

3. **Logistics**

   Shipment and delivery schedules are delayed.

4. **Distribution**

   Products do not reach the intended distribution location on time.

5. **Customer**

   Customer receives the order later than expected.

**Operational consequences:**

- Increased finished goods inventory.
- Warehouse space may become constrained.
- Shipment schedules need to be revised.
- Customer production may be affected if the components are urgently required.

**Financial consequences:**

- Additional storage costs.
- Possible demurrage or detention charges, depending on the shipment.
- Expedited freight expenses.
- Possible contractual penalties.
- Potential customer dissatisfaction.

**Possible management actions:**

1. Contact alternative transportation providers.
2. Evaluate air freight or another transportation mode.
3. Prioritize urgent customer orders.
4. Check whether partial shipments are possible.
5. Coordinate revised delivery schedules with customers.
6. Evaluate the additional freight cost against the financial impact of late delivery.

**S&OP perspective:**

This is a logistics and delivery constraint, not a manufacturing capacity problem.

The company has already produced the required products. Increasing production would not solve the transportation delay.

That distinction is important.

## 5.3 Comparing the Three Disruptions

| Aspect | Supplier delay | Production bottleneck | Logistics delay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary affected component | Suppliers | Manufacturing | Logistics |
| Main problem | Material availability | Production capacity | Product movement |
| Inventory impact | Lower RM availability | Higher WIP, lower FG replenishment | Higher FG inventory |
| Production impact | May stop or slow down | Direct capacity reduction | Production may remain normal |
| Customer impact | Possible late delivery | Possible late delivery | Late delivery despite available FG |
| Main response | Secure materials | Recover capacity | Secure transportation |

Notice that all three disruptions can lead to late customer delivery, but the root causes are different.

This is a critical skill for an S&OP analyst:

**Do not treat every supply shortage as an inventory problem. Identify the actual constraint first.**

A company can have:
- A material shortage.
- A production shortage.
- An inventory availability shortage.
- A distribution or transportation shortage.

These are different business problems and require different solutions.

# 6. Formulas and Calculations

Hindi lahat ng supply chain components ay nangangailangan ng mathematical formulas. However, may several basic calculations na useful para masukat ang performance ng components.

Let's introduce five practical calculations.

## 6.1 Supplier On-Time Delivery (OTD)

Measures the percentage of supplier deliveries received on or before the agreed delivery date.

\[
OTD=\frac{\text{On-Time Deliveries}}{\text{Total Deliveries}}\times100
\]

**Example:**

A supplier has 20 deliveries in one month.

Out of these, 18 arrived on time.

\[
OTD=\frac{18}{20}\times100
\]

**90% OTD**

Interpretation: 90% of deliveries arrived on time, while 10% were late.

Why is it important?

Low supplier OTD may indicate a potential material availability risk.

## 6.2 Production Capacity Utilization

Measures how much of the available production capacity is being used.

\[
Capacity\ Utilization=
\frac{\text{Actual Output}}{\text{Available Capacity}}\times100
\]

**Example:**

- Available capacity: 100,000 units.
- Actual output: 85,000 units.

\[
=\frac{85,000}{100,000}\times100
\]

**85% utilization**

Interpretation: The production system used 85% of its available capacity.

Important: High utilization is not automatically good. Operating at very high utilization can reduce flexibility and increase the risk of queues, delays, and WIP accumulation.

## 6.3 Inventory Accuracy

Measures how closely the recorded inventory matches the actual physical inventory.

A simple unit-based accuracy calculation is:

\[
Inventory\ Accuracy=
\frac{\text{Matching Inventory Records}}{\text{Total Records Checked}}\times100
\]

**Example:**

- Records checked: 100.
- Matching records: 95.

\[
=\frac{95}{100}\times100=95\%
\]

Interpretation: 95% of the inventory records checked matched the physical counts.

Note: Companies may use other inventory accuracy methods, including quantity-based variance measurements.

## 6.4 Inventory Coverage / Days of Supply (DOS)

Estimates how many days the available inventory can support expected consumption or demand.

\[
DOS=\frac{\text{Available Inventory}}{\text{Average Daily Demand}}
\]

**Example:**

- Available finished goods: 30,000 units.
- Average daily demand: 1,000 units.

\[
DOS=\frac{30,000}{1,000}=30\ days
\]

**30 days of supply**

Interpretation: The current available inventory can theoretically cover 30 days of demand, assuming demand and inventory availability remain consistent.

## 6.5 Perfect Order Rate

Measures the percentage of orders fulfilled completely, on time, accurately, and without damage or documentation errors.

A simplified formula is:

\[
Perfect\ Order\ Rate=
\frac{\text{Perfect Orders}}{\text{Total Orders}}\times100
\]

**Example:**

- Total orders: 500.
- Perfect orders: 450.

\[
=\frac{450}{500}\times100=90\%
\]

Interpretation: 90% of orders met all defined fulfillment requirements.

### Quick reference

| Component | Useful measurement |
|---|---|
| Suppliers | On-Time Delivery (OTD) |
| Procurement | Purchase Order Lead Time |
| Manufacturing | Capacity Utilization, Production Yield |
| Warehousing | Inventory Accuracy, Inventory Turnover |
| Logistics | Transit Time, Freight Cost |
| Distribution | Order Fulfillment Rate |
| Overall supply chain | Perfect Order Rate, Customer Service Level |

Remember: KPI definitions can vary by company. Always verify the numerator, denominator, measurement period, and business rules before comparing performance.

# 7. Business Perspective and Decision-Making

This section is particularly important because eventually, hindi lang natin kailangang maintindihan ang supply chain components. Kailangan din nating matutunan kung paano gamitin ang information nila para gumawa ng business decisions.

## 7.1 The difference between departmental performance and supply chain performance

Let's imagine the following situation:

A procurement manager is evaluated based on purchasing cost.

He decides to purchase a large quantity of raw materials because the supplier offers a 10% volume discount.

From procurement's perspective, this may look like a successful decision.

However:

- Demand Planning expects demand to decrease.
- Warehouse capacity is already limited.
- Finance is concerned about working capital.
- Some materials may become obsolete.
- Production does not need the additional quantity.

So, was procurement's decision successful?

Not necessarily.

It may have reduced purchasing cost per unit but increased total supply chain costs.

This illustrates the difference between:

**Local optimization:** Improving the performance of one department.

**End-to-end optimization:** Improving the overall performance of the supply chain.

S&OP encourages end-to-end thinking.

## 7.2 How supply chain components affect business performance

| Component | Demand | Supply | Inventory | Cost | Customer service |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Suppliers | Indirect influence | Material availability | RM availability | Purchase cost | Material-related delays |
| Procurement | Indirect influence | Material acquisition | Purchasing quantities | Procurement cost | Material availability |
| Manufacturing | Indirect influence | Production output | WIP and FG | Conversion cost | Product availability |
| Warehousing | Indirect influence | Material accessibility | Inventory levels | Storage and handling | Order readiness |
| Logistics | Indirect influence | Delivery capability | Goods in transit | Freight cost | Delivery performance |
| Distribution | Customer order visibility | Product availability at locations | Channel inventory | Distribution cost | Product accessibility |
| Supporting functions | Forecast and customer insight | Planning and coordination | Inventory policy | Financial control | Service alignment |

The table illustrates that each component contributes differently to business performance.

## 7.3 How an S&OP analyst should think

Suppose Demand Planning forecasts that customer demand will increase from 100,000 to 130,000 units next month.

An S&OP analyst should not immediately conclude that production needs to increase by 30,000 units.

Instead, the analyst needs to investigate several questions.

**Demand perspective**
- Is the increase supported by actual customer orders?
- Is the forecast reliable?
- Is this a temporary increase or a long-term trend?

**Inventory perspective**
- How much usable finished goods inventory is available?
- How much WIP is expected to become finished goods?
- How much raw material inventory is available?

**Procurement perspective**
- Can suppliers provide the additional materials?
- Are supplier lead times compatible with the requested schedule?
- Are there material allocation constraints?

**Manufacturing perspective**
- Is sufficient production capacity available?
- Which process is the bottleneck?
- Will additional shifts or equipment be required?

**Logistics perspective**
- Can transportation support the increased shipment volume?
- Is sufficient warehouse and shipping capacity available?

**Finance perspective**
- What is the cost of increasing production?
- How much additional working capital is required?
- Will the additional demand generate sufficient revenue and margin?

The analyst then consolidates these findings into an integrated business assessment.

## 7.4 The role of cross-functional collaboration

Supply chain components should not operate in isolation.

For example:

| Department | Information to share |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer orders and market changes |
| Demand Planning | Forecast changes |
| Procurement | Supplier availability and delivery commitments |
| Manufacturing | Production capacity and constraints |
| Warehouse | Inventory availability and status |
| Logistics | Shipment capacity and delivery schedules |
| Finance | Cost, revenue, and working capital implications |

The purpose is to establish one coordinated understanding of the company's demand, supply capabilities, constraints, and financial implications.

**Professional insight:** A strong S&OP analyst does not simply collect departmental reports. The analyst identifies inconsistencies, challenges assumptions, investigates constraints, and translates information into decisions.

# 8. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate everything we've discussed.

## 8.1 The seven major operational components

| No. | Component | Main responsibility |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Suppliers and Sourcing | Provide required materials and services |
| 2 | Procurement / Purchasing | Secure materials and services |
| 3 | Manufacturing / Production | Convert materials into finished products |
| 4 | Warehousing and Inventory | Store, control, and manage inventory |
| 5 | Transportation and Logistics | Move and deliver materials and products |
| 6 | Distribution | Make products available through distribution channels |
| 7 | Customers | Generate demand and establish product requirements |

Supporting functions such as Sales, Demand Planning, Supply Planning, Finance, Quality, and IT enable and coordinate these activities.

## 8.2 Important concepts to remember

1. Supply chain components are interconnected.
2. Participants, activities, and components are different concepts.
3. Suppliers determine the availability of external resources.
4. Procurement secures the materials required by the company.
5. Manufacturing converts materials into finished products.
6. Inventory provides a buffer against supply and demand uncertainty.
7. Logistics ensures that products reach the required destination.
8. Distribution connects finished products to customers.
9. Supporting functions provide information, coordination, and control.
10. A disruption in one component can affect the entire supply chain.

## 8.3 The most important principle

**End-to-End Supply Chain Thinking**

A supply chain should not be evaluated solely based on how well individual departments perform.

It should be evaluated based on how effectively all components work together to satisfy customer demand while balancing cost, inventory, capacity, and operational risks.
