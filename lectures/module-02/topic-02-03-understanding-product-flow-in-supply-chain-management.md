# MODULE 2 – SUPPLY CHAIN MANAGEMENT
## Topic 2.3: Understanding Product Flow in Supply Chain Management

**Main Learning Objective:** Understand how physical products and materials move throughout the supply chain, how inventory changes at every stage, and how the movement of products affects manufacturing operations and S&OP decisions.

---

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

## 1.1 What are we going to learn?

In Topic 2.1, we discussed what Supply Chain Management (SCM) is.

In Topic 2.2, we discussed the major components of a supply chain, including suppliers, manufacturers, warehouses, distributors, retailers, and customers.

Now, in Topic 2.3, we will focus on something different:

**How do products actually move between these supply chain components?**

Imagine a manufacturing company producing electronic components.

Before the company can manufacture its products, it needs raw materials. These materials must be purchased from suppliers, transported to the factory, inspected, stored, and eventually issued to production.

After manufacturing, the products must undergo inspection, enter finished goods inventory, and eventually be delivered to customers.

All these physical movements are collectively called **Product Flow**.

## 1.2 Why is this topic important?

Understanding product flow helps a company determine:

- Where materials and products are currently located.
- How much inventory is available at each stage.
- Whether materials are ready for production.
- Whether production has enough materials to meet its schedule.
- Whether finished goods are available for customer orders.
- Where delays and bottlenecks occur.
- How much time products spend moving through the supply chain.

For S&OP, this is important because having sufficient inventory on paper does not necessarily mean that the inventory is available where and when it is needed.

For example:

A manufacturing company has 10,000 units of raw materials recorded in its inventory system.

However:
- 3,000 units are still in transit.
- 2,000 units are awaiting inspection.
- 1,000 units are defective and blocked.
- Only 4,000 units are available for production.

Although the company has 10,000 units in its inventory records, it cannot necessarily use all 10,000 units.

This distinction becomes very important when developing supply plans and determining production feasibility.

## 1.3 Connection to the S&OP process

Our previous topics established the supply chain structure. This topic explains how products move through that structure.

The relationship is:

| Topic | What we understand |
|---|---|
| 2.1 Supply Chain Management | What SCM is and why it exists |
| 2.2 Supply Chain Components | Who participates in the supply chain |
| 2.3 Product Flow | How products physically move between participants |
| 2.4 and succeeding topics | Further understanding of supply chain processes and their applications |

The key idea is that S&OP must consider not only how much product is needed but also whether the supply chain can physically move and transform the required materials into deliverable products.

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

## 2.1 What is Product Flow?

Let's use a simple example.

Imagine that you want to manufacture fried rice.

You need:
- Rice
- Eggs
- Garlic
- Cooking oil
- Vegetables
- Seasonings

You purchase these ingredients from different suppliers.

Once they arrive, you inspect them, store them, and prepare them for cooking.

Then, you cook the ingredients together to produce fried rice.

After cooking, you place the finished food in a container and deliver it to your customer.

Notice what happened?

The ingredients started as separate materials. They moved from suppliers to your kitchen, were stored, processed, transformed into a finished product, and eventually delivered to the customer.

That entire journey is product flow.

In manufacturing, the same concept applies, except the processes are more complicated.

Instead of cooking ingredients, companies use machines, production lines, workers, and specialized processes to transform raw materials into finished products.

## 2.2 Product Flow versus Information Flow versus Financial Flow

A supply chain does not operate through physical products alone.

There are three major flows that must work together.

### 1. Product Flow

**Supplier → Factory → Customer**

Physical movement of raw materials, WIP, finished goods, and other products.

### 2. Information Flow

**Orders ↔ Plans ↔ Inventory Data**

Movement of demand forecasts, purchase orders, production schedules, inventory records, and delivery information.

### 3. Financial Flow

**Payments ↔ Invoices ↔ Cash Flow**

Movement of money, payments, invoices, credit arrangements, and other financial transactions.

Let's make the differences clearer.

| Flow | Main concern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Product Flow | Where is the product going? | Raw materials delivered to the factory |
| Information Flow | What does the supply chain need to know? | Customer orders and production schedules |
| Financial Flow | How is the transaction paid for? | Company pays its supplier |

**Important:** These three flows are interconnected.

For example:

1. A customer sends an order to a manufacturer. (Information Flow)
2. The manufacturer produces and ships the requested products. (Product Flow)
3. The customer pays the manufacturer. (Financial Flow)

If information flow is inaccurate, the company may produce the wrong quantity.

If product flow is disrupted, deliveries may be delayed.

If financial flow is delayed, suppliers may refuse to release additional materials because of outstanding payments.

All three must be coordinated.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

## 3.1 Formal Definition of Product Flow

In Supply Chain Management, **Product Flow** refers to the physical movement of materials, components, semi-finished products, and finished goods throughout the supply chain, from their sources of origin to the final customer.

It involves activities such as:

- Procurement and material sourcing.
- Inbound transportation.
- Receiving and inspection.
- Inventory storage and handling.
- Material issuance.
- Manufacturing and processing.
- Finished goods storage.
- Order fulfillment.
- Outbound transportation.
- Customer delivery.

Product flow is not limited to transportation.

This is an important distinction.

Some people associate product flow only with trucks moving goods from one location to another.

However, product flow also includes what happens inside the manufacturing facility.

For example:

Raw materials moving from the warehouse to a production machine are part of product flow.

Finished products moving from one production process to another are also part of product flow.

## 3.2 The Three Major Types of Product Movement

We can divide product movement into three major categories.

### A. Inbound Flow

Movement of materials from suppliers toward the manufacturing facility.

Example:
- A supplier delivers ceramic powder to a manufacturing plant.
- The plant receives and inspects the materials.
- Accepted materials become available for storage and production.

### B. Internal Flow

Movement of materials and products within the manufacturing facility.

Example:
- Raw materials move from the warehouse to the mixing area.
- Mixed slurry moves to the next production process.
- Processed components move to inspection.

### C. Outbound Flow

Movement of finished products from the manufacturing facility toward customers.

Example:
- Finished goods are packed.
- Customer orders are prepared.
- Products are loaded onto delivery vehicles.
- Products are transported to the customer.

The complete product movement can be summarized as:

**INBOUND FLOW**

Supplier → Transportation → Receiving → Raw Material Warehouse

**INTERNAL FLOW**

Material Issuance → Manufacturing → WIP → Inspection → Finished Goods Warehouse

**OUTBOUND FLOW**

Order Processing → Packing → Shipping → Customer Delivery

This is the general manufacturing product flow.

Actual sequences vary depending on the company's production process, warehouse arrangement, outsourced operations, distribution network, and customer requirements.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 The Complete Product Journey

Let's follow a product from its raw material suppliers until it reaches the customer.

For our example, let's use a manufacturing company producing Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors (MLCC).

This example will help connect the concepts to actual manufacturing operations.

### Stage 1: Raw Material Suppliers

**What happens?**

Suppliers manufacture or source the materials required by the company.

Examples of raw materials:
- Barium titanate (BT) powder.
- Solvents.
- Binders.
- Plasticizers.
- Dispersants.
- Metal powders.

**Responsible functions:**
- Procurement.
- Supplier Management.
- Supplier Quality.
- Material Planning.

**Inventory classification:** Supplier inventory. These materials are generally not yet owned or physically received by the manufacturer.

**Information required:**
- Purchase orders.
- Material specifications.
- Required quantities.
- Requested delivery dates.
- Supplier lead times.
- Supplier production capacity.

**Potential problems:**
- Supplier production delays.
- Material shortages.
- Incorrect specifications.
- Supplier quality issues.
- Insufficient supplier capacity.

**S&OP connection:** If a supplier cannot provide the required materials on time, the manufacturing company may be unable to meet its production plan.

### Stage 2: Inbound Transportation

**What happens?**

Purchased materials are transported from the supplier to the manufacturing facility.

Examples:
- Truck delivery.
- Sea freight.
- Air freight.
- Rail transportation.

**Responsible functions:**
- Logistics.
- Transportation Management.
- Procurement.

**Inventory classification:** Goods in Transit (GIT), depending on the company's inventory ownership and accounting policies.

**Information required:**
- Shipment tracking.
- Estimated arrival date.
- Shipping documents.
- Transportation schedule.
- Shipment quantity.

**Potential problems:**
- Transportation delays.
- Customs clearance issues.
- Damaged shipments.
- Incorrect shipment quantities.
- Weather-related disruptions.

**S&OP connection:** Even if the supplier has already shipped the materials, the factory cannot necessarily use them until they arrive and complete the receiving process.

### Stage 3: Receiving and Inspection

**What happens?**

The receiving department checks the delivered materials against the purchase order and shipping documents.

Activities include:
- Quantity verification.
- Identification and labeling.
- Document verification.
- Sampling.
- Quality inspection.
- Material acceptance or rejection.

**Responsible functions:**
- Warehouse Receiving.
- Quality Assurance (QA).
- Incoming Quality Control (IQC).
- Procurement.

**Inventory classification:**

Usually, materials enter a receiving or inspection status, such as:

- Received, awaiting inspection.
- Quality inspection.
- Accepted.
- Rejected.
- Blocked or quarantined.

**Information required:**
- Purchase order.
- Delivery receipt.
- Material identification.
- Inspection results.
- Accepted and rejected quantities.

**Potential problems:**
- Incorrect quantity.
- Damaged packaging.
- Failed quality inspection.
- Missing documentation.
- Incorrect material identification.

**S&OP connection:** Materials awaiting inspection should not automatically be included in available inventory.

A shipment may have physically arrived, but it may still be unusable.

### Stage 4: Raw Material Warehousing

**What happens?**

Accepted raw materials are transferred to designated warehouse storage locations.

Activities:
- Put-away.
- Inventory recording.
- Material identification.
- Storage management.
- FIFO or FEFO implementation, where applicable.

**Responsible functions:**
- Warehouse Management.
- Inventory Control.
- Material Planning.

**Inventory classification:** Raw Materials (RM).

However, not all RM inventory is necessarily available for production. Its status must be considered.

**Information required:**
- Material code.
- Quantity.
- Storage location.
- Lot or batch number.
- Expiration date, if applicable.
- Quality status.

**Potential problems:**
- Incorrect inventory records.
- Material expiration.
- Improper storage conditions.
- Inventory misplacement.
- Excessive inventory.

**S&OP connection:** Raw material inventory helps determine whether the company has enough materials to support its planned production volume.

### Stage 5: Material Issuance to Production

**What happens?**

The warehouse releases materials to production based on authorized material requirements.

For example:

A production order requires:
- 500 kg of BT powder.
- 200 liters of solvent.
- 30 kg of binder.

The warehouse prepares and issues these materials to the production department.

**Responsible functions:**
- Warehouse.
- Material Control.
- Production Planning.
- Manufacturing.

**Inventory classification:**

The material quantity in the warehouse decreases.

The issued material becomes production-issued material or is recorded as WIP, depending on the company's accounting and inventory system.

Important distinction: Physical issuance does not always mean that all materials have immediately become accounting WIP. The company's inventory recognition rules determine this.

**Information required:**
- Production order.
- Bill of Materials (BOM).
- Material requirement.
- Material availability.
- Lot traceability.
- Material issuance records.

**Potential problems:**
- Insufficient materials.
- Wrong material issuance.
- Excessive material consumption.
- Incorrect BOM.
- Material traceability issues.

**S&OP connection:** A production plan must be supported by actual material availability.

A production schedule cannot become feasible simply because production capacity is available.

Materials must also be available.

### Stage 6: Manufacturing and Processing

**What happens?**

Raw materials undergo different manufacturing processes to create the required product.

For an MLCC manufacturing process, examples include:

- Slurry mixing.
- Casting.
- Stacking.
- Cutting.
- Firing.
- Termination and other subsequent processes.

The actual sequence depends on the manufacturing design and process configuration.

**Responsible functions:**
- Production.
- Manufacturing Engineering.
- Process Engineering.
- Maintenance.
- Quality Control.

**Inventory classification:** WIP.

**Information required:**
- Production schedule.
- Process specifications.
- Machine availability.
- Production quantities.
- Material consumption.
- Yield.
- Cycle time.
- Equipment downtime.

**Potential problems:**
- Machine breakdown.
- Low production yield.
- Excessive defects.
- Material shortages.
- Production bottlenecks.
- Unplanned downtime.

**S&OP connection:** Manufacturing capacity determines how much product can be produced within a given period.

If demand increases but production capacity is limited, the company may need overtime, additional shifts, subcontracting, or other capacity decisions.

### Stage 7: Work-in-Process (WIP)

**What happens?**

Products that have entered manufacturing but have not yet completed all required production operations are considered WIP.

For example:

A component has already completed casting and stacking but has not yet completed cutting and firing.

It is not raw material anymore, but it is not yet a finished product.

**Responsible functions:**
- Production.
- Production Control.
- Manufacturing Engineering.
- Inventory Control.

**Inventory classification:** WIP.

**Information required:**
- Current production stage.
- Quantity in each process.
- Production completion status.
- Defect quantity.
- Remaining operations.
- Expected completion date.

**Potential problems:**
- Excessive WIP accumulation.
- Long waiting times between processes.
- Production bottlenecks.
- Quality issues.
- Unbalanced production capacity.

**S&OP connection:** High WIP does not necessarily mean that the company has more sellable products.

The materials may still require several manufacturing operations before becoming finished goods.

### Stage 8: Finished Goods Inspection

**What happens?**

Products that have completed manufacturing undergo final inspection and testing before being released as acceptable finished goods.

For an MLCC manufacturer, activities may include:
- Electrical testing.
- Visual inspection.
- Sorting.
- Reliability testing, where required.
- Final quality verification.

**Responsible functions:**
- Quality Assurance.
- Quality Control.
- Testing Engineering.
- Production.

**Inventory classification:**
- Awaiting final inspection.
- Accepted finished goods.
- Rejected or defective goods.
- Blocked finished goods.

**Information required:**
- Inspection results.
- Test results.
- Product specifications.
- Defect rate.
- Accepted quantity.
- Lot disposition.

**Potential problems:**
- High defect rates.
- Failed electrical tests.
- Inspection delays.
- Quality holds.
- Product rejection.

**S&OP connection:** Production output is not necessarily equal to sellable output.

For example, a company produces 10,000 units, but 500 units fail final inspection.

Only 9,500 units are accepted, assuming the remaining units pass all applicable release requirements.

### Stage 9: Finished Goods Warehousing

**What happens?**

Accepted finished products are transferred to the finished goods warehouse.

Activities:
- Finished goods receipt.
- Product labeling.
- Storage allocation.
- Inventory recording.
- Stock monitoring.

**Responsible functions:**
- Finished Goods Warehouse.
- Inventory Control.
- Quality Assurance.

**Inventory classification:** Finished Goods (FG).

However, some finished goods may be allocated to specific customers or orders and may not be freely available for other orders.

**Information required:**
- Product code.
- Quantity.
- Lot number.
- Quality release status.
- Warehouse location.
- Customer allocation.

**Potential problems:**
- Excess inventory.
- Incorrect stock records.
- Product obsolescence.
- Improper storage.
- Stock allocation errors.

**S&OP connection:** Finished goods inventory is one of the major factors in determining whether customer demand can be fulfilled without additional production.

### Stage 10: Order Processing and Fulfillment

**What happens?**

Customer orders are processed and matched against available finished goods.

Activities:
- Order validation.
- Inventory allocation.
- Picking.
- Packing.
- Shipping documentation.
- Dispatch preparation.

**Responsible functions:**
- Sales.
- Customer Service.
- Order Management.
- Warehouse.
- Logistics.

**Inventory classification:**
- Available FG.
- Allocated FG.
- Picked or packed FG.
- Staged for shipment.

**Information required:**
- Customer purchase order.
- Requested delivery date.
- Ordered quantity.
- Product specifications.
- Available inventory.
- Shipping requirements.

**Potential problems:**
- Insufficient FG.
- Incorrect order quantities.
- Wrong product allocation.
- Picking errors.
- Delayed order processing.

**S&OP connection:** Finished goods availability must be aligned with actual customer demand and requested delivery dates.

### Stage 11: Outbound Transportation

**What happens?**

Products are loaded and transported from the manufacturing facility or distribution center toward the customer.

**Responsible functions:**
- Logistics.
- Transportation Management.
- Warehouse.
- Shipping.

**Inventory classification:** Shipped or Goods in Transit, depending on the point of inventory ownership transfer.

**Information required:**
- Shipping schedule.
- Shipment quantity.
- Delivery destination.
- Carrier information.
- Estimated delivery date.
- Shipping documents.

**Potential problems:**
- Vehicle shortages.
- Transportation delays.
- Damaged products.
- Incorrect shipment documentation.
- Customs delays.

**S&OP connection:** Even if finished goods are available and the order is ready, transportation limitations can still prevent timely delivery.

### Stage 12: Customer Delivery

**What happens?**

Products reach the customer and are received according to the agreed delivery requirements.

Activities:
- Delivery confirmation.
- Quantity verification.
- Customer receiving inspection.
- Proof of delivery.
- Delivery discrepancy reporting.

**Responsible functions:**
- Logistics.
- Customer Service.
- Customer Receiving.
- Sales.

**Inventory classification:** Customer inventory after the applicable ownership transfer.

**Information required:**
- Delivery receipt.
- Proof of delivery.
- Customer order.
- Received quantity.
- Delivery date.
- Customer acceptance or discrepancy report.

**Potential problems:**
- Late delivery.
- Incorrect quantity.
- Damaged goods.
- Customer rejection.
- Delivery to the wrong destination.

**S&OP connection:** The ultimate objective is not simply to produce goods. It is to deliver the right products, in the right quantities, at the right time, to the right customers.

## 4.2 Understanding Inventory Transformation

Now that we understand the product journey, let's discuss an important concept:

**Inventory does not remain in the same classification throughout the supply chain.**

As products move through manufacturing, their status and form change.

Let's understand the major inventory classifications.

### A. Raw Materials (RM)

Raw materials are materials purchased or sourced from suppliers that are intended to be used in manufacturing.

Examples:
- BT powder.
- Solvents.
- Binder resins.
- Metal powders.
- Packaging materials.

These materials have not yet undergone the manufacturing transformation required to become the company's intended product.

### B. Work-in-Process (WIP)

WIP refers to materials and products that have already entered the manufacturing process but have not yet completed all required production operations.

Example:

A ceramic component has completed casting and stacking but is still waiting for cutting.

It is WIP because manufacturing has already started, but the product is not yet complete.

### C. Semi-Finished Goods

Semi-finished goods are products that have completed a defined portion of manufacturing and may be stored, transferred, or used as inputs for subsequent manufacturing operations.

Example:

A component has completed a major production stage and is held as an intermediate product before additional processing.

**Important distinction:** Some companies classify semi-finished goods under WIP, while others maintain a separate inventory category or stock type in their ERP system.

The classification depends on the company's manufacturing and accounting structure.

### D. Finished Goods (FG)

Finished goods are products that have completed all required manufacturing and quality release requirements and are ready for sale or delivery.

Example:

An MLCC product has completed its required manufacturing processes, passed final inspection, and received the necessary quality release.

It can now be treated as finished goods.

### E. Goods in Transit (GIT)

Goods in transit are products or materials currently being transported between locations.

Examples:
- Raw materials being transported from suppliers to factories.
- Finished goods being transported from factories to distribution centers.
- Finished goods being transported to customers.

An important technical consideration:

Goods in transit is primarily a location or movement status, not necessarily a separate production stage.

The company may still own the inventory while it is in transit, depending on the contractual shipping terms and applicable accounting policies.

### 4.2.1 Inventory Transformation Diagram

**RAW MATERIALS**

Purchased materials

↓

Material issuance and manufacturing start

↓

**WORK-IN-PROCESS**

Materials undergoing manufacturing

↓

Intermediate production completion

↓

**SEMI-FINISHED GOODS**

Intermediate products, where separately classified

↓

Remaining manufacturing and final inspection

↓

**FINISHED GOODS**

Quality-released products ready for fulfillment

↓

Shipping and delivery

↓

**CUSTOMER**

Product received by customer

Goods in Transit may occur between any two locations in this journey, not only after finished goods production.

### 4.2.2 Why Is Inventory Classification Important?

Let's use a simple example.

A manufacturing company has the following inventory:

| Inventory status | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Available raw materials | 4,000 |
| Raw materials awaiting inspection | 2,000 |
| Blocked raw materials | 1,000 |
| Raw materials in transit | 3,000 |
| **Total recorded quantity** | **10,000** |

Can the company use all 10,000 units for production?

No.

Only 4,000 units are confirmed as available in this example.

The other quantities have different statuses:

- Awaiting inspection: Not yet released for use.
- Blocked: Not available for normal production consumption.
- In transit: Not yet physically received at the factory.

This gives us a very important principle:

**Total Inventory is not the same as Available Inventory.**

Inventory must be evaluated according to its physical location, ownership, quality status, allocation status, and operational availability.

### 4.2.3 Common Inventory Statuses

| Status | Meaning | Usable? |
|---|---|---|
| Available | Released and ready for use | Generally yes |
| Quality Hold | Awaiting quality disposition | No |
| Blocked | Restricted from normal use | No |
| Rejected | Failed applicable requirements | No |
| Allocated | Reserved for an order or requirement | Not freely available |
| In Transit | Being transported | Not yet available at destination |
| Expired | Beyond applicable shelf-life requirements | Generally no |

A product can physically exist in a warehouse without being available for production or sale.

This is why inventory status management is essential.

## 4.3 Understanding Inbound and Outbound Product Flow

We will now examine the three major product movements in greater detail.

### A. Inbound Flow

Inbound flow refers to the movement of materials from suppliers to the company's manufacturing or distribution facility.

Typical inbound activities:

1. Material requirement identification.
2. Purchase requisition.
3. Purchase order creation.
4. Supplier confirmation.
5. Supplier production and preparation.
6. Transportation.
7. Receiving.
8. Quality inspection.
9. Material acceptance.
10. Warehouse put-away.

**Example:**

An MLCC manufacturing company requires 5,000 kg of BT powder next month.

The procurement department sends a purchase order to the supplier.

The supplier confirms the quantity and delivery date.

The supplier ships the material.

Upon arrival, the receiving team checks the quantity and the IQC team performs the required inspection.

After acceptance, the material becomes available for production according to the company's release procedures.

**Important inbound terms:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Supplier Lead Time | Time required by the supplier to fulfill an order |
| Purchase Order (PO) | Formal document requesting materials or services |
| ETA | Estimated Time of Arrival |
| Receiving | Physical acceptance and recording of incoming goods |
| IQC | Incoming Quality Control |
| Put-away | Transferring received materials to designated storage locations |

**Common inbound problems:**

- Supplier delivery delays.
- Incorrect material quantities.
- Failed incoming inspection.
- Transportation disruption.
- Long receiving or inspection lead times.

**Business impact:**

If inbound materials are delayed, production may experience material shortages even when production capacity is available.

### B. Internal Flow

Internal flow refers to the movement of materials and products within the company's facility.

This includes:
- Warehouse to production.
- Production process to production process.
- Production to inspection.
- Inspection to finished goods warehouse.

Let's use an MLCC manufacturing example.

**Illustrative internal manufacturing flow**

Raw Material Warehouse (BT Powder, Solvents, Binder)

↓

Slurry Mixing (Materials combined and processed)

↓

Casting (Ceramic sheets produced)

↓

Stacking and Cutting (Components formed and prepared)

↓

Firing and Subsequent Processes (Product processed and completed)

↓

Final Inspection and Finished Goods (Accepted products released)

Each process receives materials from the preceding stage and provides output to the succeeding stage.

This creates dependencies between production operations.

For example:

If casting experiences downtime, stacking may eventually run out of materials.

If firing has limited capacity, unfinished products may accumulate before firing.

This is an example of a **production bottleneck**.

A bottleneck is a process or resource that limits the overall output of the production system.

**Common internal flow problems:**

- Excessive WIP.
- Material handling delays.
- Unbalanced production capacity.
- Machine downtime.
- Poor production sequencing.
- Quality failures.
- Incorrect material transfers.

**S&OP connection:** Internal product flow helps determine whether the planned production volume can move through all manufacturing stages within the required timeframe.

### C. Outbound Flow

Outbound flow refers to the movement of finished goods from the manufacturing facility or distribution center toward customers.

Typical activities:

1. Finished goods availability verification.
2. Customer order confirmation.
3. Inventory allocation.
4. Picking and packing.
5. Shipping documentation.
6. Loading and dispatch.
7. Transportation.
8. Customer delivery.
9. Proof of delivery.

**Example:**

A customer orders 20,000 MLCC units.

The company checks available finished goods inventory.

If 20,000 acceptable and unallocated units are available, the warehouse can prepare the order.

The products are packed, shipped, and delivered to the customer.

However, if only 15,000 units are available, the company may need to:

- Produce the remaining 5,000 units.
- Check alternative inventory locations.
- Negotiate a partial shipment.
- Discuss a revised delivery date.

**Common outbound problems:**

- Insufficient FG inventory.
- Shipping delays.
- Transportation capacity limitations.
- Wrong product or quantity.
- Customer delivery restrictions.

**S&OP connection:** Outbound flow directly affects customer service and the company's ability to fulfill demand.

## 4.4 How Delays in One Stage Affect the Entire Supply Chain

This is one of the most important concepts in this topic.

A supply chain operates as an interconnected system.

A problem in one stage can create consequences in other stages.

Let's examine an example.

**Scenario: Supplier delivery delay**

A supplier is expected to deliver 5,000 kg of raw materials on November 20.

However, because of a transportation problem, the shipment arrives on November 27.

The factory's production schedule requires these materials starting November 22.

What happens?

### Supply chain disruption example

1. **Supplier Delay**

   Raw materials arrive seven days later than planned.

2. **Material Shortage**

   The factory does not have sufficient available raw materials.

3. **Production Disruption**

   The affected production orders cannot start as scheduled.

4. **WIP and Production Schedule Impact**

   Downstream processes may experience idle time or schedule changes.

5. **Finished Goods Shortage**

   The expected finished goods output is delayed.

6. **Customer Delivery Delay**

   Customer orders may miss their committed delivery dates.

7. **Business Impact**

   The company may incur additional costs, lose sales opportunities, or experience reduced customer satisfaction.

Notice that the original problem occurred during inbound transportation.

However, the consequences extended all the way to customer delivery.

This is why supply chain professionals must understand the entire product journey rather than focusing only on their individual departments.

## 4.5 Understanding Bottlenecks and Inventory Accumulation

Let's consider another example.

A factory has three production processes.

| Process | Daily capacity |
|---|---:|
| Casting | 10,000 units |
| Stacking | 8,000 units |
| Firing | 5,000 units |

Assuming the same unit of measure and comparable output basis, what happens?

Casting can produce 10,000 units per day.

Stacking can process 8,000 units per day.

Firing can process only 5,000 units per day.

The firing process has the lowest capacity.

Therefore, it limits the overall production output to approximately 5,000 units per day, assuming no other constraints.

**Illustrative production capacity**

| Process | Capacity | Relative capacity |
|---|---:|---:|
| Casting | 10,000 units | 100% |
| Stacking | 8,000 units | 80% |
| Firing | 5,000 units | 50% |

**Firing is the bottleneck.**

If casting continues producing 10,000 units per day while firing processes only 5,000 units, WIP may accumulate between these processes.

Possible consequences:

- Increased WIP inventory.
- Longer manufacturing lead time.
- Increased material handling.
- More storage requirements.
- Higher working capital tied up in inventory.

This demonstrates an important supply chain principle:

**Increasing production at one stage does not automatically increase total supply chain output.**

The entire process must be considered.

# 5. PRACTICAL EXAMPLES AND APPLICATIONS

Let's consolidate everything through a realistic manufacturing scenario.

## 5.1 Complete Manufacturing Product Flow Example

Suppose an MLCC manufacturer receives an order for 100,000 finished units.

Its planned production process requires raw materials, multiple manufacturing stages, and final inspection.

Here is an illustrative product flow:

| Stage | Activity | Quantity |
|---|---|---:|
| Supplier | Raw materials supplied | 120,000-unit equivalent |
| Receiving | Accepted raw materials | 120,000-unit equivalent |
| Production | Materials issued | 110,000-unit equivalent |
| Manufacturing | Production output | 105,000 units |
| Final Inspection | Accepted finished goods | 100,000 units |
| Final Inspection | Rejected products | 5,000 units |
| Customer Delivery | Products shipped | 100,000 units |

The example assumes a simplified one-to-one equivalent for the material and finished-product quantities. Actual manufacturing BOMs may involve multiple materials, consumption ratios, and process losses.

### What can we understand from this example?

1. Receiving 120,000 units' worth of materials does not mean that 120,000 finished products are immediately available.
2. Some materials may remain in raw material inventory.
3. Some materials may be consumed during production.
4. Production may experience yield losses.
5. Only quality-accepted finished goods can be considered released FG.
6. Customer demand must be matched against available finished goods and delivery schedules.

## 5.2 Incorrect vs. Correct Supply Chain Interpretation

| Incorrect interpretation | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|
| All received materials are available for production. | Materials must pass the required release requirements. |
| All issued materials are finished products. | Issued materials may become WIP and require additional processing. |
| All production output is sellable. | Production output must meet applicable quality requirements. |
| Total inventory equals available inventory. | Inventory must be evaluated according to its status and allocation. |
| Increasing one machine's output increases total production. | Overall production is constrained by the system's bottlenecks. |
| Shipped products are automatically delivered. | Shipment and customer delivery are separate milestones. |

# 6. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

Product flow is primarily a process concept, but several calculations help S&OP analysts understand inventory movement and operational performance.

Let's study four useful calculations.

## 6.1 Inventory Balance

This tells us how inventory changes over a specific period.

**Formula:**

[
Ending\ Inventory = Beginning\ Inventory + Receipts - Issues
]

Where:

- Beginning Inventory = Starting inventory.
- Receipts = Inventory received during the period.
- Issues = Inventory released or consumed during the period.

**Example:**

A warehouse starts with 10,000 kg of raw materials.

During the month:
- Receives 5,000 kg.
- Issues 8,000 kg to production.

Calculation:

[
EI = 10,000 + 5,000 - 8,000
]

[
\boxed{EI = 7,000\ kg}
]

Interpretation:

The warehouse has 7,000 kg remaining at the end of the month, assuming no other inventory adjustments.

## 6.2 Production Yield

Production yield measures the proportion of input or production output that meets the required quality criteria.

For a simplified process yield calculation:

[
Yield(\%) = \frac{Good\ Output}{Total\ Input} \times 100
]

**Example:**

A manufacturing process receives 10,000 units.

After processing:
- 9,500 units are accepted.
- 500 units are rejected.

[
Yield = \frac{9,500}{10,000}\times100
]

[
\boxed{Yield = 95\%}
]

Interpretation:

For every 100 units entering the process, approximately 95 units become acceptable output.

This is important because production planning must consider expected yield.

If the company needs 10,000 good units and expects a 95% yield, it needs approximately:

[
Required\ Input = \frac{10,000}{0.95}
]

[
\boxed{Required\ Input \approx 10,526.32\ units}
]

In actual production, the required quantity must be rounded appropriately and may need additional allowances for other losses.

## 6.3 Production Lead Time

Production lead time refers to the time required for a product to move through the production process.

A simplified formula:

[
Production\ Lead\ Time = Processing + Waiting + Movement + Inspection
]

**Example:**

| Activity | Time |
|---|---:|
| Processing | 8 hours |
| Waiting | 12 hours |
| Movement | 2 hours |
| Inspection | 2 hours |
| **Total** | **24 hours** |

Therefore:

[
\boxed{Production\ Lead\ Time = 24\ hours}
]

Notice that only 8 hours were actual processing time.

The other 16 hours were spent waiting, moving, or undergoing inspection.

This is important because reducing manufacturing lead time does not always require increasing machine speed.

Sometimes, the biggest opportunity is reducing waiting time between processes.

## 6.4 Inventory Available for Production

A simplified operational calculation:

[
Available\ Inventory = Physical\ Inventory - Unavailable\ Inventory
]

**Example:**

| Inventory status | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Total physical inventory | 20,000 |
| Quality hold | 2,000 |
| Blocked inventory | 1,000 |
| Expired inventory | 500 |
| **Available inventory** | **16,500** |

Calculation:

[
20,000 - 2,000 - 1,000 - 500
]

[
\boxed{16,500\ units}
]

This is a simplified calculation. In an actual ERP environment, allocations, reservations, ownership, and other stock restrictions must also be considered.

# 7. BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE AND S&OP DECISION-MAKING

Now let's move beyond understanding product movement and examine how an S&OP analyst uses this information.

The main objective is to determine whether the supply chain can support the expected demand.

## 7.1 Product Flow and Demand Planning

Demand planning determines how much product customers are expected to need.

However, knowing the expected demand is not enough.

The company must understand how much finished goods inventory is available and how much additional production is required.

Example:

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Forecasted demand | 100,000 |
| Available FG | 30,000 |
| Additional requirement | 70,000 |

The company needs to produce or otherwise obtain 70,000 additional units, assuming no other supply sources or inventory changes.

Demand planning tells us what customers need.

Product flow tells us where the products are and how they can reach the customers.

## 7.2 Product Flow and Supply Planning

Supply planning evaluates whether the company can meet the demand requirement using available resources.

It must consider:

- Raw material availability.
- Supplier delivery schedules.
- Manufacturing capacity.
- Production lead time.
- WIP levels.
- Finished goods inventory.
- Transportation capacity.

Example:

Demand requires 100,000 units.

However:
- Available FG = 20,000 units.
- Production capacity = 60,000 units.
- Expected good output = 57,000 units.

Total potential supply:

[
20,000 + 57,000 = 77,000
]

Supply gap:

[
100,000 - 77,000 = 23,000
]

The company has a potential shortage of 23,000 units.

Possible decisions:

- Increase production capacity.
- Introduce overtime.
- Outsource production.
- Expedite materials.
- Reallocate finished goods.
- Negotiate customer delivery schedules.

These decisions must be evaluated through the S&OP process.

## 7.3 Product Flow and Inventory Management

Inventory exists at different stages of the supply chain.

| Inventory | Business purpose |
|---|---|
| Raw Materials | Support future production |
| WIP | Represent products undergoing manufacturing |
| Semi-Finished Goods | Support subsequent manufacturing stages |
| Finished Goods | Support customer orders |
| Goods in Transit | Represent inventory being moved between locations |

The S&OP analyst must understand the inventory position across the entire supply chain.

For example:

A company may have high total inventory but insufficient finished goods.

This can happen when too many materials are accumulated as raw materials or WIP while finished goods production is constrained.

This creates a potential mismatch between inventory composition and customer requirements.

## 7.4 Product Flow and Business KPIs

Several operational KPIs help measure product flow performance.

| KPI | What it measures | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Supplier On-Time Delivery | Supplier delivery reliability | Measures inbound performance |
| Production Yield | Good output relative to input | Measures manufacturing effectiveness |
| Manufacturing Lead Time | Time required to manufacture products | Measures production responsiveness |
| WIP Inventory | Inventory within production | Indicates work accumulation |
| Finished Goods Inventory | Completed product inventory | Supports customer fulfillment |
| Order Fulfillment Rate | Ability to fulfill customer orders | Measures supply performance |
| On-Time Delivery (OTD) | Delivery performance against commitment | Measures customer service |
| Inventory Accuracy | Agreement between physical and recorded inventory | Supports planning accuracy |

These KPIs help identify where product flow is inefficient.

## 7.5 How an S&OP Analyst Uses Product Flow Information

Imagine that management asks:

"Can we fulfill next month's customer demand of 150,000 units?"

As an S&OP analyst, you should not immediately answer based on finished goods inventory alone.

You should examine the complete supply position.

| Area | Question to investigate |
|---|---|
| Raw Materials | Do we have enough usable materials? |
| Inbound | Will incoming materials arrive on time? |
| WIP | How many units are currently undergoing production? |
| Production | Can manufacturing complete the required quantity? |
| Capacity | Are there bottlenecks or machine limitations? |
| Quality | What is the expected good production yield? |
| Finished Goods | How many units are available and unallocated? |
| Outbound | Can logistics deliver the products on time? |

Your responsibility is to identify the gaps between expected demand and achievable supply.

This is where product flow becomes useful in S&OP.

# 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Before moving to the next topic, make sure you understand these fundamental concepts.

**1. Product Flow**

Product flow refers to the physical movement of materials and products from suppliers through manufacturing and distribution until customer delivery.

**2. Three Supply Chain Flows**

- Product Flow: Movement of physical goods.
- Information Flow: Movement of planning and operational information.
- Financial Flow: Movement of money and financial transactions.

**3. Three Major Product Movement Categories**

- Inbound: Supplier to manufacturing facility.
- Internal: Movement within manufacturing operations.
- Outbound: Manufacturing facility to customer.

**4. Inventory Transformation**

Products may move through different inventory classifications:

Raw Materials → WIP → Semi-Finished Goods → Finished Goods → Customer.

Goods in Transit can occur between different supply chain locations.

**5. Inventory Availability**

Total inventory does not automatically equal available inventory.

Quality holds, blocked stocks, expired materials, and allocations must be considered.

**6. Production Bottlenecks**

The slowest or most capacity-constrained stage can limit the output of the entire manufacturing system.

**7. Supply Chain Interdependence**

A disruption in one stage can affect other stages and eventually customer delivery.

**8. S&OP Application**

S&OP uses product flow information to determine whether demand can be supported by available materials, production capacity, inventory, and distribution resources.
