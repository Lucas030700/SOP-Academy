# Module 2 – Supply Chain Management
## Topic 2.5: Product Classification and Product Families

**Main learning focus:** Understanding how companies organize thousands of different products into manageable groups for planning, manufacturing, inventory management, and S&OP.

Welcome to Topic 2.5!

Sa previous topic, **Topic 2.4: Understanding Demand Information**, pinag-aralan natin kung saan nanggagaling ang demand information, anong types of demand data ang ginagamit, at paano ito kinokolekta at ini-interpret.

Ngayon, may isang importanteng tanong tayong kailangang sagutin:

**Kung ang isang manufacturing company ay may 500, 1,000, or even 10,000 different SKUs, kailangan ba nilang gumawa ng separate demand and supply plan para sa bawat isa?**

Technically, possible. Pero magiging complicated, time-consuming, at mahirap i-manage.

Dito pumapasok ang **Product Classification and Product Families**.

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

## 1.1 What are we going to learn?

Pag-aaralan natin kung paano inaayos at pinapangkat ng isang company ang kanilang products based on similarities and business requirements.

For example, imagine an electronic components manufacturer producing:

- Different types of capacitors.
- Different capacitance values.
- Different voltage ratings.
- Different dimensions.
- Different material compositions.
- Different product configurations.

Although these products may belong to the same general product category, hindi ibig sabihin na pare-pareho sila ng manufacturing requirements.

Kaya kailangang magkaroon ng organized product structure.

## 1.2 Why is this important?

Imagine ikaw ang S&OP Analyst ng company na mayroong 3,000 SKUs.

Every month, kailangan mong i-review ang:

- Demand forecast.
- Production requirements.
- Raw material availability.
- Production capacity.
- Inventory levels.
- Revenue projections.

Kung individual SKU agad ang pag-uusapan sa executive meeting, magiging napakaraming details.

Instead, companies organize products into groups, such as product families, so they can discuss demand and supply at a higher level.

**The objective is to simplify planning without losing important operational details.**

## 1.3 Connection to Topic 2.4

Sa Topic 2.4, natutunan natin kung ano ang demand information.

Ngayon, gagamitin natin ang information na iyon para maintindihan kung paano ito ino-organize.

| Topic 2.4 | Topic 2.5 |
|---|---|
| What products do customers demand? | How are those products organized? |
| Where does demand information come from? | Which products belong together? |
| How much demand exists? | How can demand be consolidated? |
| How do we interpret demand? | At what product level should we plan? |

The connection is straightforward:

**Demand Information → Product Classification → Demand Aggregation → S&OP Planning**

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

## 2.1 Imagine a supermarket

Imagine pumunta ka sa supermarket.

Thousands of individual items ang available. Kung walang organization, lahat ng products ay magkakahalo.

Instead, nakaayos sila into categories:

| Product Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Beverages | Water, juice, soft drinks |
| Dairy | Milk, cheese, yogurt |
| Personal Care | Shampoo, soap, toothpaste |
| Household | Detergent, tissue, cleaning products |

Under Beverages, puwede pang magkaroon ng smaller groups:

- Bottled water.
- Carbonated drinks.
- Fruit juices.
- Energy drinks.

At under bottled water, may iba't ibang brands, sizes, and variants.

Notice something?

Hindi lahat ng products ay tinitingnan individually agad. May hierarchy or organized structure.

Ito ang basic idea ng product classification.

## 2.2 Now, apply it to manufacturing

Let's use an electronic components manufacturer.

Suppose the company produces 100 different capacitor SKUs.

Instead of treating all 100 products as completely unrelated items, the company can organize them according to their characteristics.

For example:

- Capacitors using similar dielectric materials.
- Capacitors with similar manufacturing processes.
- Capacitors using the same production equipment.
- Capacitors serving similar market applications.

These groups can become product families, depending on the company's planning purpose.

Why?

Because kung may 20 products na gumagamit ng similar production processes, mas madaling pag-aralan ang kanilang combined demand and capacity requirements.

Instead of reviewing 20 separate products immediately, puwedeng magsimula sa isang group-level discussion.

## 2.3 The central idea

Think of product classification as organizing a large library.

- Product portfolio: The entire library.
- Product category: A section of the library.
- Product family: A collection of related books within that section.
- Product model: A particular book edition or title.
- SKU: The exact identifiable item or version.

The purpose is to make a large and complicated product environment easier to understand and manage.

However, remember this important distinction:

**Products that look similar are not necessarily products that should be planned together.**

For manufacturing and S&OP, their production requirements matter just as much as their physical appearance.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

## 3.1 What is Product Classification?

**Product Classification** is the systematic process of organizing products into categories or groups based on selected characteristics, business requirements, operational similarities, or planning objectives.

In simple terms, it is a structured way of identifying which products belong together and why.

Companies use product classification to:

1. Organize product information.
2. Simplify demand forecasting.
3. Consolidate demand and supply requirements.
4. Analyze inventory performance.
5. Evaluate manufacturing capacity.
6. Support financial reporting.
7. Improve S&OP decision-making.

### Important distinction: Product Classification vs. Inventory Classification

Magkaiba ang purpose ng dalawang ito.

| Product Classification | Inventory Classification |
|---|---|
| Groups products based on product relationships and characteristics | Groups inventory based on inventory management criteria |
| Focuses on what products are and how they relate | Focuses on how inventory should be controlled |
| Examples: Capacitors, Resistors, Ceramic Capacitors | Examples: ABC, XYZ, critical inventory |
| Supports product hierarchy and aggregate planning | Supports inventory policies and prioritization |

For example:

A capacitor can belong to the Ceramic Capacitor product family.

At the same time, it can be classified as an A-class inventory item because it contributes significantly to annual inventory consumption value.

So, one product can have different classifications depending on the purpose.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Understanding Product Hierarchy

Ito ang isa sa pinakamahalagang concepts sa topic na ito.

A **Product Hierarchy** is the structured arrangement of products into different levels, from the broadest grouping down to the most specific identifiable item.

Let's examine six commonly used levels.

Important: Hindi universal ang exact structure. May companies na gumagamit ng additional levels, may companies naman na pinagsasama ang ibang levels.

### A. The Six Levels of Product Hierarchy

The hierarchy below shows a common conceptual structure, from broadest to most specific:

1. **Product Portfolio** – All products offered or managed by the company.
2. **Product Category** – Broad product classification.
3. **Product Family** – Related products grouped for planning or operational purposes.
4. **Product Group** – A more specific grouping within a family, if the company uses this level.
5. **Product Model** – A defined product design or specification.
6. **SKU** – The specific identifiable inventory item or variant.

Let's understand each level.

#### 1. Product Portfolio

**Definition:** The complete collection of products that a company offers, manufactures, or manages.

Layman's explanation: Ito ang lahat ng products ng company.

Example:

An electronic components manufacturer has the following portfolio:

- Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors (MLCC).
- Resistors.
- Inductors.
- Ferrite components.

Lahat ng ito ay bahagi ng product portfolio.

**Purpose in S&OP:** Gives management an overall view of the company's business, demand, revenue, and resource requirements.

#### 2. Product Category

**Definition:** A broad classification of products that share a general product type, purpose, or market classification.

Example:

| Product Category | Products |
|---|---|
| Passive Components | Capacitors, resistors, inductors |
| Semiconductor Components | Diodes, transistors |
| Electromechanical Components | Connectors, switches |

Notice na mas malawak ang category kaysa product family.

**Purpose in S&OP:** Helps management analyze business performance at a broad product level.

#### 3. Product Family

**Definition:** A group of related products that share meaningful characteristics relevant to a particular business or planning purpose.

Example:

Under the Capacitors category:

- General-purpose MLCC.
- High-voltage MLCC.
- Automotive-grade MLCC.
- High-capacitance MLCC.

These are illustrative families. The actual grouping depends on how the company defines its products and planning requirements.

**Purpose in S&OP:** Allows the company to consolidate demand and supply requirements among related products.

This is particularly important because product families can become the primary level used in aggregate S&OP discussions.

#### 4. Product Group

**Definition:** A more specific subdivision of products within a product family.

This level is optional. Some companies use it; others go directly from family to model.

Example:

Under General-Purpose MLCC:

- Small-size MLCC.
- Medium-size MLCC.
- Large-size MLCC.

Another company might instead group them according to capacitance ranges or voltage ratings.

**Purpose in S&OP:** Provides additional planning detail without immediately going down to individual SKUs.

#### 5. Product Model

**Definition:** A specific product design, series, or defined configuration that may have multiple variants.

Example:

Imagine a manufacturer has an MLCC model designated as:

GRM21 Series

This model may have several variations in capacitance, voltage rating, tolerance, and other specifications.

Important: Product model naming conventions are company-specific. A model may also be called a product series, platform, or part family in some organizations.

**Purpose in S&OP:** Helps connect demand forecasts and production requirements to a defined product design or configuration.

#### 6. Stock Keeping Unit (SKU)

**Definition:** A unique identifier assigned to a specific product or inventory item that a company needs to distinguish and track.

Example:

A particular MLCC may have the following specifications:

- Size: 0805.
- Capacitance: 10 µF.
- Rated voltage: 10 V.
- Tolerance: ±10%.
- Dielectric: X7R.

A variation in one or more inventory-relevant specifications may require a different SKU.

**Purpose in S&OP:** Provides the detailed product-level information needed for forecasting, inventory tracking, production execution, and material planning.

### 4.2 Comparing the Six Levels

Let's use a simplified example.

| Hierarchy | Example |
|---|---|
| Product Portfolio | Electronic Components |
| Product Category | Capacitors |
| Product Family | General-Purpose MLCC |
| Product Group | 0805-size MLCC |
| Product Model | Model X Series |
| SKU | Model X, 10 µF, 10 V, ±10%, X7R |

The important distinction:

- Portfolio = Everything the company manages.
- Category = Broad type of product.
- Family = Related products grouped for planning.
- Group = A more specific subdivision.
- Model = Defined design or product series.
- SKU = Exact identifiable item.

**Remember:** These are conceptual levels, not a mandatory structure. Some companies have different terminology, hierarchy depth, or even multiple overlapping hierarchies.

## 4.3 Understanding SKU (Stock Keeping Unit)

Now let's discuss SKU more deeply because this is where product classification becomes very practical.

### What exactly is an SKU?

Imagine you manufacture a capacitor.

You produce the following:

| Product | Size | Capacitance | Voltage |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0805 | 10 µF | 10 V |
| B | 0805 | 10 µF | 25 V |
| C | 0805 | 22 µF | 10 V |
| D | 1206 | 10 µF | 10 V |

These products may look almost identical, but they have different specifications.

For inventory and manufacturing purposes, the company needs to distinguish them.

That is why SKUs exist.

### A. Why does every SKU need identification?

Because companies need to know exactly:

- What product was ordered?
- What product is currently in stock?
- What product should be manufactured?
- What raw materials are required?
- What product should be shipped?
- What product is experiencing a shortage?

If two different product variants are treated as one item, the company can experience inventory discrepancies and incorrect production plans.

### B. SKU vs. Product Model vs. Part Number vs. Product Code

These terms are related but not necessarily interchangeable.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| SKU | Unique inventory identification | Specific 0805, 10 µF, 10 V variant |
| Product Model | Defined design or product series | Model X |
| Part Number | Identifier assigned to a specific component or part | PN-10025 |
| Product Code | A company-defined code used to identify a product | CAP-0805-10U |

An important clarification:

A part number can also function as an SKU in some companies. Similarly, a product code may be the actual inventory identifier.

The terminology depends on the company's ERP and master-data structure.

### C. When does a product variant become a different SKU?

Consider the following changes:

| Variation | Possible SKU Impact |
|---|---|
| Different dimensions | Usually a different SKU |
| Different capacitance | Usually a different SKU |
| Different voltage rating | Usually a different SKU |
| Different material composition | Different SKU if separately identified and controlled |
| Different tolerance | Different SKU if commercially or operationally distinguished |
| Different packaging | May have a different SKU depending on inventory management |
| Different customer-specific configuration | May have a separate SKU |

The key principle is that the company must distinguish inventory items that are not interchangeable for its business requirements.

Not every minor difference automatically creates a new SKU. That depends on product master-data rules.

### D. Why SKU accuracy matters

Imagine the ERP system reports:

- 10,000 pieces of Product A.
- Customer requires 10,000 pieces of Product B.

If Product A and Product B are incorrectly treated as interchangeable, the inventory report may show sufficient stock.

But operationally, the company has a shortage of Product B.

This can lead to:

- Incorrect demand fulfillment.
- Excess inventory of the wrong variant.
- Production rescheduling.
- Additional manufacturing costs.
- Delayed customer deliveries.

This is why SKU-level accuracy is essential even when S&OP operates at a higher aggregation level.

## 4.4 Understanding Product Families

Ito ang central concept ng ating lesson.

### What is a Product Family?

A product family is a group of related products that share meaningful characteristics and can be analyzed or planned together for a specific purpose.

Let's simplify.

Imagine a factory produces 30 different products.

Although they have different specifications, 20 of them use:

- The same production technology.
- Similar raw materials.
- The same manufacturing equipment.
- Similar production processes.

It may make sense to group those 20 products into a common product family for capacity and supply planning.

Why?

Because their operational requirements have meaningful similarities.

### A. Common characteristics used to define product families

| Classification Basis | Example |
|---|---|
| Product technology | MLCC, resistor, inductor |
| Material composition | Products using similar dielectric materials |
| Manufacturing process | Products using similar casting and firing processes |
| Equipment requirements | Products using the same production lines |
| Capacity requirements | Products consuming similar machine hours |
| Product specifications | Products with similar voltage or capacitance ranges |
| Market application | Automotive, industrial, consumer electronics |
| Customer segment | Automotive OEM, industrial customers |

### B. Why should similar products be grouped together?

There are several reasons.

**1. Simplified demand planning**

Instead of forecasting 50 products independently during the initial planning discussion, the company can review their combined demand at family level.

**2. Simplified supply planning**

If products share manufacturing resources, their combined demand can provide a useful starting point for supply planning.

**3. Better capacity visibility**

Companies can understand how much production capacity a family requires.

**4. Better management reporting**

Executives can see which product families are growing, declining, or consuming more resources.

**5. More efficient S&OP discussions**

Instead of reviewing hundreds of individual SKUs in an executive meeting, the team can focus on significant product-family trends and exceptions.

### C. Why shouldn't products be grouped based only on appearance?

This is a very important manufacturing consideration.

Suppose two products look almost identical.

| Characteristic | Product A | Product B |
|---|---|---|
| Physical size | 0805 | 0805 |
| General application | Consumer electronics | Automotive |
| Material | Standard dielectric | Specialized dielectric |
| Manufacturing process | Standard firing profile | Special firing profile |
| Quality requirement | Standard | Automotive-grade requirements |

They may look similar, but their manufacturing and quality requirements differ.

If the company groups them together without considering those differences, the family-level supply plan may become misleading.

**Product similarity must be evaluated based on the purpose of classification.**

A family suitable for commercial demand analysis may not be suitable for detailed capacity planning.

## 4.5 Product Classification in Manufacturing

There are three major ways to classify products depending on the intended use.

### A. Based on Product Characteristics

This approach focuses on what the product physically or technically is.

| Characteristic | Example |
|---|---|
| Size | 0603, 0805, 1206 |
| Specification | 10 µF, 22 µF, 100 µF |
| Voltage | 10 V, 25 V, 50 V |
| Material composition | X7R, C0G, Y5V |
| Design | Standard or customized |
| Performance | High-capacitance or high-voltage |

**Application:** Product catalog management, product segmentation, demand analysis.

### B. Based on Manufacturing Requirements

This approach focuses on how the product is manufactured.

| Characteristic | Example |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing process | Casting, stacking, cutting, firing |
| Production equipment | Machine A or Machine B |
| Raw materials | BT powder, binder, additives |
| Lead time | Standard or extended production lead time |
| Capacity consumption | Machine hours per 1,000 units |
| Production technology | Different firing or material technologies |

**Application:** Supply planning, production planning, and capacity analysis.

For example, two MLCC models may belong to different commercial product families but share the same casting and firing equipment.

For capacity planning, it may be useful to analyze them together.

### C. Based on Business Characteristics

This approach focuses on the business value and market role of the products.

| Characteristic | Example |
|---|---|
| Customer segment | Automotive, industrial, consumer |
| Revenue contribution | High-revenue vs. low-revenue |
| Strategic importance | Key strategic products |
| Market application | Automotive electronics, smartphones |
| Product lifecycle | New, mature, declining, obsolete |

**Application:** Financial planning, business strategy, portfolio review, and executive decision-making.

### D. One product can belong to multiple classifications

This is an important professional concept.

A particular capacitor may simultaneously be classified as:

- Product family: General-Purpose MLCC.
- Manufacturing group: Firing Line A.
- Customer segment: Automotive.
- Lifecycle classification: Mature.
- Inventory classification: A-class.

These classifications are not necessarily contradictory.

They answer different business questions.

| Business Question | Appropriate Classification |
|---|---|
| What products are commercially related? | Product family |
| What products use the same production resources? | Manufacturing group |
| Which products serve the same customers? | Customer segment |
| Which products contribute most to revenue? | Revenue classification |
| Which inventory items need tighter control? | Inventory classification |

**Professional insight:** A company may need multiple product hierarchies or classification dimensions instead of forcing every planning purpose into one hierarchy.

## 4.6 Product Families and Aggregate Planning

Now let's connect product families directly to S&OP.

### A. What is Aggregate Planning?

**Aggregate Planning** is the process of planning demand, production, capacity, and other resources at a summarized level rather than immediately planning every individual product.

Sa madaling salita:

Instead of asking, "How many pieces of each individual SKU should we produce?"

The company initially asks:

"How much total production do we need for this product family?"

This allows the company to evaluate overall demand and supply requirements before going into detailed product-level planning.

### B. Aggregate Demand vs. SKU-Level Demand

Let's assume a company produces four MLCC SKUs under one product family.

Its monthly forecast is:

| SKU | Forecast |
|---|---:|
| SKU-A | 10,000 |
| SKU-B | 15,000 |
| SKU-C | 20,000 |
| SKU-D | 5,000 |
| **Total** | **50,000** |

The family-level forecast is:

\[
10,000+15,000+20,000+5,000
\]

\[
\boxed{50,000\text{ units}}
\]

So:

- SKU-level demand = Individual forecast per product.
- Aggregate demand = Combined forecast of the selected product group.

**Visualizing demand aggregation**

| SKU | Monthly demand |
|---|---:|
| SKU-A | 10,000 |
| SKU-B | 15,000 |
| SKU-C | 20,000 |
| SKU-D | 5,000 |
| **Product Family Total** | **50,000** |

Illustrative monthly demand, in units.

### C. Why plan at product-family level?

Imagine that you have 500 SKUs.

If every monthly S&OP discussion starts with 500 individual demand lines, the discussion can become overwhelming.

But if these SKUs belong to 15 product families, the initial discussion can focus on those 15 families.

This helps management understand:

- Which families have increasing demand?
- Which families have declining demand?
- Which families require additional capacity?
- Which families are experiencing supply constraints?
- Which families have excess inventory?

### D. How does aggregate planning become SKU-level planning?

Important: Aggregate planning does not eliminate detailed SKU planning.

It provides the higher-level plan that must eventually be translated into detailed requirements.

Example:

The S&OP team agrees that the MLCC family requires 50,000 units next month.

The detailed planning team then uses the SKU-level forecasts, orders, priorities, inventory, and other constraints to determine the product mix.

| SKU | Family Allocation |
|---|---:|
| SKU-A | 10,000 |
| SKU-B | 15,000 |
| SKU-C | 20,000 |
| SKU-D | 5,000 |
| **Total** | **50,000** |

The product-family total is the same, but the individual SKU requirements remain important.

### E. The risk of aggregation

Consider this situation:

| SKU | Demand | Available Inventory |
|---|---:|---:|
| SKU-A | 10,000 | 15,000 |
| SKU-B | 15,000 | 5,000 |
| SKU-C | 20,000 | 20,000 |
| SKU-D | 5,000 | 10,000 |
| **Total** | **50,000** | **50,000** |

At family level:

- Total demand = 50,000.
- Total inventory = 50,000.
- Net shortage = Zero.

Does that mean the company can fulfill all demand?

**No.**

SKU-B has a shortage of 10,000 units, while SKU-A and SKU-D have excess inventory.

The products may not be interchangeable.

This demonstrates an important principle:

**Aggregate inventory can look sufficient while individual SKU shortages still exist.**

This is one of the biggest limitations of excessive aggregation.

# 5. PRACTICAL EXAMPLES AND APPLICATIONS

Let's build a more realistic manufacturing scenario.

## 5.1 Illustrative Business Scenario: Electronic Components Manufacturer

Imagine a company called **ABC Electronics Manufacturing**.

The company manufactures several types of electronic components, including capacitors, resistors, and inductors.

For this example, we will focus on its capacitor business.

### A. Establishing the product hierarchy

ABC Electronics organizes its capacitor products as follows:

| Category | Product Family | Product Models |

| Capacitors | General-Purpose MLCC | GP-0805, GP-1206 |
| Capacitors | High-Voltage MLCC | HV-0805, HV-1206 |
| Capacitors | Automotive MLCC | AU-0805, AU-1206 |

**Example: GP-0805 Model**

## 5.3 Identifying Products That Should Not Be Grouped Together

Let's introduce an additional manufacturing consideration.

Suppose General-Purpose MLCC and Automotive MLCC use similar basic manufacturing processes.

Should they automatically be grouped into one family?

Not necessarily.

Consider the following:

| Requirement | General-Purpose MLCC | Automotive MLCC |
|---|---|---|
| Basic manufacturing technology | MLCC | MLCC |
| Raw material | Standard formulation | Specialized formulation |
| Quality requirements | Standard | More stringent |
| Traceability | Standard controls | Specific traceability requirements |
| Customer qualification | General | Automotive qualification |
| Production restrictions | Standard | Potentially restricted equipment/process |

If ABC Electronics groups both families into one general MLCC family for all planning purposes, it may overlook their differences.

For example:

- Automotive products may require qualified production resources.
- Specialized raw materials may have different procurement lead times.
- Production scheduling may need stricter controls.
- Customer commitments may have different service requirements.

**The lesson:** A common technology does not automatically mean a common planning family.

However, they may still be combined for certain high-level financial or total-capacity analyses, provided their differences are retained in the detailed plan.

## 5.4 How Incorrect Classification Creates Misleading Plans

Let's examine a practical situation.

Suppose ABC Electronics has:

| Product Family | Demand | Production Capacity |
|---|---:|---:|
| General-Purpose MLCC | 50,000 | 60,000 |
| Automotive MLCC | 20,000 | 15,000 |
| **Total** | **70,000** | **75,000** |

At the total company level:

- Demand = 70,000.
- Capacity = 75,000.
- Available capacity = 5,000.

Management might conclude that there is sufficient capacity.

But look at the family-level picture:

| Product Family | Demand | Capacity | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| General-Purpose MLCC | 50,000 | 60,000 | 10,000 excess capacity |
| Automotive MLCC | 20,000 | 15,000 | 5,000 shortage |
| **Total** | **70,000** | **75,000** | **5,000 excess capacity** |

The company has excess capacity overall, but Automotive MLCC still has a 5,000-unit capacity shortage.

Why?

Because capacity is not necessarily interchangeable.

A production line qualified for General-Purpose MLCC may not be approved or suitable for Automotive MLCC.

This is called a **capacity allocation or capacity constraint issue**.

The total capacity figure alone is insufficient to make the correct decision.

# 6. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

May ilang basic calculations na kailangan mong maintindihan para magamit ang product hierarchy in actual S&OP work.

## 6.1 Product-Family Demand Aggregation

The basic formula is:

\[
D_f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_i
\]

Where:

- \(D_f\) = Total demand of a product family.
- \(D_i\) = Demand of an individual SKU.
- \(n\) = Number of SKUs within the family.

Example:

| SKU | Forecast |
|---|---:|
| A | 12,000 |
| B | 18,000 |
| C | 10,000 |

\[
D_f=12,000+18,000+10,000
\]

\[
\boxed{D_f=40,000}
\]

Interpretation: The product family has a total forecast demand of 40,000 units.

## 6.2 Product-Family Demand Contribution

This tells us what percentage of total company demand comes from a particular product family.

Formula:

\[
\text{Demand Contribution}=
\frac{\text{Family Demand}}{\text{Total Demand}}\times100
\]

Example:

- General-Purpose MLCC = 50,000.
- Total MLCC demand = 125,000.

\[
\frac{50,000}{125,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{40\%}
\]

Interpretation:

General-Purpose MLCC represents 40% of total MLCC demand.

This helps identify which families contribute most to the company's demand requirements.

## 6.3 Product-Family Forecast Growth

This measures how much the demand of a product family increases or decreases between two periods.

Formula:

\[
\text{Growth Rate}=
\frac{D_{\text{Current}}-D_{\text{Previous}}}
{D_{\text{Previous}}}\times100
\]

Example:

| Period | Demand |
|---|---:|
| Previous Month | 40,000 |
| Current Month | 50,000 |

\[
\frac{50,000-40,000}{40,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{25\%}
\]

Interpretation:

The family has experienced a 25% increase in forecast demand.

This may require a review of production capacity, raw materials, and inventory availability.

## 6.4 Weighted Average Selling Price (Optional Financial Application)

Product families can also be analyzed financially.

Suppose a family has two SKUs:

| SKU | Forecast Quantity | Selling Price |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | ₱10 |
| B | 3,000 | ₱20 |

Total revenue:

\[
R=(1,000\times10)+(3,000\times20)
\]

\[
R=₱70,000
\]

Weighted average selling price:

\[
WASP=\frac{R}{\text{Total Units}}
\]

\[
WASP=\frac{70,000}{4,000}
\]

\[
\boxed{₱17.50/\text{unit}}
\]

Why weighted average?

Because hindi equal ang demand volume ng dalawang SKUs.

Kung simple average lang ang gagamitin:

\[
\frac{10+20}{2}=₱15
\]

Mali ang representation nito ng actual average selling price because SKU-B has a larger sales volume.

**Professional insight:** Family-level demand should not automatically be treated as family-level revenue using one arbitrary selling price. Product mix matters.

# 7. BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE AND DECISION-MAKING

Now let's connect everything to the responsibilities of an S&OP Analyst.

Product classification is not merely an administrative task. It influences how the company makes planning decisions.

## 7.1 Product Families in Demand Planning

The Demand Planning team uses product families to:

- Consolidate individual SKU forecasts.
- Identify demand trends.
- Compare actual demand against forecast.
- Identify growing or declining product segments.
- Review demand changes with Sales and Marketing.

Example:

If Automotive MLCC demand increases by 30%, the demand planning team needs to understand whether the increase is caused by:

- New customer demand.
- Additional customer orders.
- New product introductions.
- Changes in market requirements.

## 7.2 Product Families in Demand Review

During Demand Review, the team can review demand at family level.

Example:

| Family | Previous Forecast | New Forecast | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| General-Purpose | 50,000 | 55,000 | +10% |
| High-Voltage | 30,000 | 27,000 | -10% |
| Automotive | 20,000 | 30,000 | +50% |

This makes significant demand changes easier to identify.

However, the team should investigate which SKUs are driving those changes.

A 50% increase in Automotive MLCC could be caused by only one SKU.

That distinction matters.

## 7.3 Product Families in Supply Planning

Supply Planning uses the product hierarchy to translate demand into production and supply requirements.

The team evaluates:

- Production availability.
- Raw material requirements.
- Supplier capacity.
- Manufacturing lead times.
- Inventory availability.
- Production constraints.

Example:

If High-Voltage MLCC demand increases, the team checks whether the required materials and production resources are available.

## 7.4 Product Families in Capacity Planning

Product-family classification helps identify how much capacity is required by related products.

But be careful:

Different products can consume different amounts of machine time.

For example:

| Product | Required Machine Time per 1,000 Units |
|---|---:|
| SKU-A | 2 hours |
| SKU-B | 3 hours |
| SKU-C | 5 hours |

A family forecast of 10,000 units does not necessarily translate into the same capacity requirement for every product mix.

This is why capacity planning may need SKU-level or product-specific resource consumption factors.

## 7.5 Product Families in Inventory Analysis

Companies can analyze inventory at family level to identify:

- Total inventory exposure.
- Inventory concentration.
- Excess inventory trends.
- Shortage risks.
- Inventory distribution across product groups.

But family-level inventory should never replace SKU-level inventory visibility.

Remember our earlier example: total inventory can equal total demand while individual SKUs still have shortages.

## 7.6 Product Families in Financial Planning

The Finance team can use product families to evaluate:

- Revenue by product family.
- Gross margin contribution.
- Product mix.
- Revenue growth.
- Profitability trends.

For example, Automotive MLCC may have lower unit demand but higher revenue or margins than General-Purpose MLCC.

Therefore, the highest-volume product family is not automatically the most financially important.

## 7.7 Product Families in Executive S&OP

Executive S&OP requires a summarized view of the business.

Executives generally need to understand:

1. Which product families are growing?
2. Which families have declining demand?
3. Which families have supply constraints?
4. Which families require additional capacity?
5. Which families are contributing to revenue or profitability?
6. What decisions require management approval?

Product families make these discussions more manageable.

However, when a significant problem occurs, the team must be able to drill down into the affected SKUs, models, materials, and resources.

## 7.8 The Golden Rule of Product Classification in S&OP

A good product hierarchy should satisfy three conditions:

| Requirement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Visibility | Management can understand business performance |
| Planning relevance | Products grouped together have meaningful similarities for the planning purpose |
| Traceability | The team can drill down from aggregate plans to individual products |

A product hierarchy that simplifies reporting but hides important supply constraints is not a good planning structure.

Likewise, a hierarchy that is so detailed that nobody can use it effectively is also problematic.

The goal is the right level of detail for the decision being made.

# 8. COMMON MISTAKES AND MISCONCEPTIONS

Before we finish, let's discuss mistakes that can happen when companies design and use product hierarchies.

| Common Mistake | Why It's Wrong | Possible Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Treating product categories and families as identical | Categories are generally broader; families have more specific relationships | Inappropriate demand consolidation |
| Grouping products based only on names or appearance | Similar-looking products may have different manufacturing requirements | Incorrect capacity planning |
| Ignoring raw material differences | Products may require different materials or suppliers | Material shortages |
| Ignoring production constraints | Similar products may not share interchangeable equipment | Misleading available capacity |
| Using inconsistent hierarchies across departments | Sales, Finance, and Operations may report different groupings | Conflicting reports and decisions |
| Aggregating too much | Individual SKU shortages can disappear in total figures | Missed customer commitments |
| Creating too many product families | Planning becomes unnecessarily detailed | More administrative workload |
| Incorrect SKU master data | Products may be identified or grouped incorrectly | Inventory and planning errors |

## 8.1 A particularly important mistake: Inconsistent product hierarchy

Imagine:

- Sales classifies a product under Automotive MLCC.
- Production classifies it under General-Purpose MLCC.
- Finance classifies it under a different product category.

During the S&OP meeting, their reports may show different demand and revenue figures.

This creates confusion.

The solution is to establish standardized product master data and agreed classification rules.

This is closely related to **Master Data Management (MDM)**, which refers to maintaining consistent and reliable core business information across systems and departments.

## 8.2 Another important mistake: Assuming all products in one family have identical requirements

A product family does not mean every SKU has exactly the same:

- Production lead time.
- Material consumption.
- Machine-hour requirement.
- Yield rate.
- Inventory policy.
- Customer priority.

Product families simplify planning, but they do not eliminate individual product differences.

This is why good S&OP practices combine aggregate planning with detailed operational analysis.

# 9. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Let's consolidate the entire lesson.

## 9.1 Important terminology

| Term | What You Should Remember |
|---|---|
| Product Classification | Organizing products based on selected characteristics or business purposes |
| Product Portfolio | All products managed or offered by a company |
| Product Category | Broad product grouping |
| Product Family | Related products grouped for a specific planning or operational purpose |
| Product Group | More specific subdivision of a family, when used |
| Product Model | A particular product design or series |
| SKU | Unique identifier for a specific inventory item |
| Product Hierarchy | Structured arrangement of products into levels |
| Aggregate Planning | Planning demand and resources at a summarized level |
| Demand Aggregation | Combining individual product demand into a larger group |

## 9.2 Five concepts you must understand

**1. Product classification simplifies complexity.**

Companies may have thousands of SKUs. Organizing them into meaningful groups makes planning and reporting manageable.

**2. Product hierarchy provides structure.**

You should understand the distinction between portfolio, category, family, group, model, and SKU.

**3. Product families are important in S&OP.**

They allow companies to consolidate demand and supply information into meaningful planning groups.

**4. Aggregation does not eliminate SKU-level planning.**

The company must still maintain individual SKU visibility to identify shortages, inventory issues, and specific production requirements.

**5. Product families should be designed around planning purposes.**

Commercial similarities, manufacturing similarities, and financial similarities are not always the same.

## 9.3 The complete concept

### How Product Classification Supports S&OP

1. **Individual Products / SKUs** – Detailed product information and demand.
2. **Product Hierarchy** – Models, groups, families, and categories.
3. **Product-Family Demand and Supply Aggregation** – Summarized requirements and planning visibility.
4. **S&OP Analysis and Decision-Making** – Demand, capacity, inventory, and financial performance.
5. **Detailed Operational Plans** – SKU-level requirements and execution.
