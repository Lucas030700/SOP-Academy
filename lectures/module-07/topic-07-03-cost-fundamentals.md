# Module 7 – Financial Planning
## Topic 7.3: Cost Fundamentals

**Level 1 → Level 2 → Level 3**  
**S&OP Professional Development**

**Main learning objective:** Understand how manufacturing costs are generated, calculated, monitored, and influenced by business decisions within Sales & Operations Planning (S&OP).

Sa topic na ito, pag-aaralan natin kung paano nagkakaroon ng cost ang isang manufacturing company, saan nanggagaling ang mga gastos, paano ito kinukuwenta, at paano naaapektuhan ng production and supply planning decisions ang overall business cost.

Importante ito dahil hindi sapat na alam lang ng company kung gaano karaming products ang kailangang ibenta at gawin. Kailangan din nitong malaman:

Ang isang magandang S&OP plan ay hindi lamang achievable operationally. Dapat financially reasonable din.

# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What is Cost?

**Layman's definition:**

Ang cost ay halaga ng resources na ginagamit o isinasakripisyo ng isang company para makagawa ng product, makapagbigay ng service, o maisagawa ang isang business activity.

Halimbawa, imagine na gumagawa ang isang factory ng electronic components.

Para makagawa ng 1,000 units, kailangan nito ng:

| Resource | Illustrative cost |
|---|---:|
| Raw materials | ₱100,000 |
| Direct labor | ₱25,000 |
| Machine electricity | ₱10,000 |
| Factory overhead | ₱15,000 |
| **Total manufacturing cost** | **₱150,000** |

Ibig sabihin, gumastos ang factory ng ₱150,000 para makagawa ng 1,000 units.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Cost per Unit}=\frac{150,000}{1,000}
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱150/unit}
\]

Bawat unit ay may manufacturing cost na ₱150 based sa assumptions natin.

## 1.2 Why Do Companies Monitor Costs?

Hindi lang para makatipid ang companies. May mas malaking business purpose ang cost monitoring.

1. **Profitability:** Para malaman kung kumikita ba ang company sa bawat product.
2. **Pricing:** Para magkaroon ng basis sa pagdetermine ng selling price.
3. **Budgeting:** Para ma-estimate ang financial requirements ng operations.
4. **Efficiency:** Para makita kung may excessive material consumption, labor hours, or machine operating costs.
5. **Planning:** Para malaman kung anong production volume ang financially practical.
6. **Decision-making:** Para ma-compare ang alternative production and supply strategies.

For example:

Suppose ang selling price ay ₱200 per unit, at ang manufacturing cost ay ₱150 per unit.

May ₱50 difference between selling price and manufacturing cost.

Pero hindi pa natin masasabi na ₱50 ang final profit dahil mayroon pang selling expenses, administrative expenses, financing costs, taxes, at iba pang business costs.

Dito papasok ang connection sa Topic 7.4: Profit and Margin.

## 1.3 Connection to Previous and Next Topics

| Topic | Main question | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| 7.1 Financial Planning | What are our financial targets and plans? | Establishes the financial direction. |
| 7.2 Revenue and Sales Planning | How much revenue do we expect to generate? | Establishes expected sales and revenue. |

**Revenue tells us how much money we expect to earn. Cost tells us how much resources we need to spend. Profit tells us what remains after deducting expenses from revenue.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Cost Through a Manufacturing Example

Imagine na may factory na gumagawa ng MLCC (Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors).

Para makagawa ng finished goods, kailangan nito ng:

- BT powder and other raw materials.
- Binder and chemical materials.
- Production operators.
- Casting and firing machines.
- Electricity and utilities.
- Maintenance personnel.
- Factory facilities.

Lahat ng resources na ginagamit sa manufacturing process ay may corresponding cost.

Pero may importanteng distinction:

Hindi lahat ng binabayaran ng company ay automatically manufacturing cost.

For example:

| Business activity | Example |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing | Raw materials used in production |
| Sales | Sales commissions |
| Administration | Office supplies |
| Logistics | Delivery of finished goods |
| Maintenance | Machine maintenance |
| Finance | Interest on loans |

Magkakaiba ang purpose ng mga gastos na ito, kaya kailangan silang ma-classify correctly.

## 2.2 Cost, Expense, Expenditure, and Cash Outflow

Ito ang isa sa pinakamahalagang distinctions sa financial accounting.

Madalas ginagamit ang mga terms na ito interchangeably sa everyday conversations, pero technically magkaiba sila.

### A. Cost

Value ng resources na ginagamit o acquired para sa isang purpose.

Example: Bumili ang company ng ₱500,000 worth of raw materials.

Ang ₱500,000 ay acquisition cost ng materials.

### B. Expense

Cost na kinikilala na bilang nagamit o consumed para makabuo ng revenue sa isang accounting period.

Example:

- Bumili ang company ng ₱500,000 raw materials.
- ₱300,000 lamang ang nagamit sa production.
- Ang remaining ₱200,000 ay nasa inventory pa.

Illustratively:

| Item | Amount | Classification |
|---|---:|---|
| Materials purchased | ₱500,000 | Expenditure and acquisition cost |
| Materials consumed | ₱300,000 | Manufacturing cost |
| Materials remaining | ₱200,000 | Inventory asset |

Kapag ang finished goods ay naibenta, ang corresponding product cost ay magiging Cost of Goods Sold (COGS).

### C. Expenditure

Ang expenditure ay spending or commitment of resources to acquire something.

Example:

Nag-approve ang company ng ₱2 million para bumili ng bagong production machine.

Ang ₱2 million ay capital expenditure (CapEx).

Hindi ibig sabihin na buong ₱2 million ay magiging expense agad sa parehong buwan.

### D. Cash Outflow

Ito naman ang actual movement of cash palabas ng company.

Example:

- November: Bumili ng raw materials worth ₱500,000 on credit.
- December: Binayaran ang supplier ng ₱500,000.

Ang cash outflow ay nangyari noong December, kahit noong November pa na-acquire ang materials.

### Comparison

| Term | Simple meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Value of resources | ₱500,000 materials |
| Expense | Cost recognized in the income statement | Materials cost associated with sold products |
| Expenditure | Spending or commitment to acquire resources | Purchase of a machine |
| Cash outflow | Actual cash payment | Payment to supplier |

**Important principle:** Cost recognition, expense recognition, and cash payment do not necessarily happen at the same time.

This is especially important in S&OP because production plans, inventory plans, procurement commitments, and cash flow plans have different timing.

# 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the formal accounting and cost management concepts.

## 3.1 Relationship Between Cost, Revenue, and Profitability

At a basic level:

\[
\text{Profit}=\text{Revenue}-\text{Total Expenses}
\]

For a manufacturing company, we can break down the relationship:

\[
\text{Gross Profit}=\text{Revenue}-\text{COGS}
\]

Where:

- Revenue = income generated from selling products.
- COGS = cost of products that were sold.
- Gross Profit = amount remaining after deducting the cost of sold products.

Example:

| Financial item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sales revenue | ₱1,000,000 |
| Cost of Goods Sold | ₱600,000 |
| **Gross Profit** | **₱400,000** |
| Operating expenses | ₱200,000 |
| **Operating Profit** | **₱200,000** |

Notice that the company generated ₱1 million in revenue, but its operating profit is only ₱200,000.

This is why monitoring revenue alone is insufficient.

A company can increase its sales volume but still experience declining profitability if its costs increase faster than revenue.

# 4. Types of Costs

Ito ang core foundation ng Cost Fundamentals.

May iba't ibang paraan para i-classify ang costs. Importanteng maintindihan na hindi mutually exclusive ang lahat ng classifications.

Meaning, one cost can belong to several categories simultaneously.

For example:

Ang raw material na ginagamit sa manufacturing ay maaaring:

- Variable cost.
- Direct cost.
- Manufacturing cost.
- Product cost.
- Relevant cost, depending on the decision being evaluated.

Let's discuss each classification.

## 4.1 Fixed Costs versus Variable Costs

Ang classification na ito ay based on how costs behave when production volume changes.

### A. Fixed Costs

**Definition:** Costs that remain constant in total within a relevant range of production activity, regardless of changes in production volume.

Simpleng explanation:

Kahit gumawa ang factory ng 1,000 units or 2,000 units, pareho pa rin ang total cost ng particular resource, as long as within its existing capacity.

Example:

Monthly factory rent = ₱100,000.

| Production volume | Monthly rent |
|---:|---:|
| 1,000 units | ₱100,000 |
| 2,000 units | ₱100,000 |
| 3,000 units | ₱100,000 |
| 4,000 units | ₱100,000 |

Hindi nadadagdagan ang rent dahil lamang dumami ang production.

**Examples of fixed costs:**
- Factory building rent.
- Salaried factory management.
- Depreciation of machinery using the straight-line method.
- Certain insurance costs.
- Fixed monthly equipment lease payments.

Important: Fixed does not mean permanent or impossible to change. It means fixed within a specified period and relevant operating range.

### B. Variable Costs

**Definition:** Costs that change in total in proportion to changes in production volume or activity.

Example:

Raw material requirement = ₱50 per unit.

| Production volume | Material cost |
|---:|---:|
| 1,000 units | ₱50,000 |
| 2,000 units | ₱100,000 |
| 3,000 units | ₱150,000 |
| 4,000 units | ₱200,000 |

As production increases, total material cost also increases.

**Examples:**
- Raw materials.
- Production consumables.
- Packaging materials.
- Piece-rate labor.
- Certain machine operating consumables.

### C. The Important Difference: Total Cost versus Cost per Unit

Ito ang madalas nakakalito sa beginners.

**Fixed cost:**
- Total fixed cost remains constant.
- Fixed cost per unit decreases as production increases.

**Variable cost:**
- Total variable cost increases as production increases.
- Variable cost per unit generally remains constant.

Example:

Monthly fixed factory cost = ₱100,000.

| Production | Total fixed cost | Fixed cost per unit |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | ₱100,000 | ₱100 |
| 2,000 | ₱100,000 | ₱50 |
| 4,000 | ₱100,000 | ₱25 |

Formula:

\[
Fixed\ Cost/unit=\frac{Total\ Fixed\ Cost}{Production\ Volume}
\]

Bakit bumababa ang fixed cost per unit?

Dahil hinahati natin ang same ₱100,000 fixed cost sa mas maraming finished units.

This concept is important when evaluating production volume and capacity utilization.

## 4.2 Direct Costs versus Indirect Costs

Ang classification na ito ay based on whether a cost can be directly traced to a particular product, production order, or cost object.

### A. Direct Costs

Costs that can be specifically and economically traced to a particular product or production activity.

Examples:

| Direct cost | Manufacturing example |
|---|---|
| Direct materials | BT powder used in a specific product |
| Direct labor | Operator labor directly working on a production order |
| Product-specific packaging | Packaging used for a particular product |

Example:

If Product A consumes ₱30 worth of raw materials per unit, that material cost can be directly assigned to Product A.

### B. Indirect Costs

Costs that support manufacturing but cannot be conveniently traced to an individual product or unit.

These are usually allocated across products using an appropriate allocation basis.

Examples:

- Factory electricity.
- Factory supervisor salary.
- Equipment maintenance.
- Factory building depreciation.
- General factory supplies.

Example:

Suppose monthly factory electricity is ₱200,000.

The electricity supports several machines producing different products.

Instead of assigning the entire ₱200,000 to one product, the company may allocate the cost based on machine hours, power consumption, or another suitable cost driver.

**Key distinction:**

Direct versus indirect is about traceability.

Fixed versus variable is about cost behavior.

Therefore, these classifications are different.

For example:

| Cost | Fixed/Variable | Direct/Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials | Variable | Direct |
| Factory rent | Fixed | Indirect |
| Production labor paid per unit | Variable | Direct |
| Factory supervisor salary | Fixed | Indirect |
| Machine electricity | Often variable or mixed | Often indirect |

A cost can be fixed and direct, or variable and indirect, depending on the circumstances.

## 4.3 Manufacturing Costs versus Non-Manufacturing Costs

This classification is based on whether the cost is related to manufacturing products or supporting other business functions.

### A. Manufacturing Costs

Costs incurred to convert raw materials into finished products.

There are three primary components:

1. Direct Materials.
2. Direct Labor.
3. Manufacturing Overhead.

We will discuss these in detail in Section 5.

### B. Non-Manufacturing Costs

Costs associated with selling, administration, distribution, and other non-production activities.

Examples:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Selling expenses | Sales commission, advertising |
| Administrative expenses | HR, accounting, office salaries |
| Distribution expenses | Finished goods delivery |
| General business expenses | Corporate office utilities |

**Why is this distinction important?**

Because manufacturing costs generally become part of inventory before being recognized as COGS when the products are sold.

Non-manufacturing expenses are generally recognized as expenses in the period in which they are incurred, subject to applicable accounting rules.

## 4.4 Product Costs versus Period Costs

This is closely connected to manufacturing versus non-manufacturing costs.

### A. Product Costs

Costs assigned to the products manufactured.

For a manufacturing company, these include:

- Direct materials.
- Direct labor.
- Manufacturing overhead.

Product costs are initially recorded as inventory assets.

The accounting flow is:

**Raw Materials Inventory → Work in Process (WIP) → Finished Goods Inventory → Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)**

- Raw Materials Inventory: materials acquired but not yet issued to production.
- Work in Process: products currently undergoing manufacturing, including accumulated direct materials, direct labor, and manufacturing overhead.
- Finished Goods Inventory: completed products not yet sold.
- COGS: product costs recognized when the corresponding finished goods are sold.

### B. Period Costs

Costs recognized as expenses in the accounting period in which they are incurred rather than being included in inventory.

Examples:
- Office salaries.
- Marketing expenses.
- Corporate office rent.
- General administrative expenses.

### Product versus Period Cost Comparison

| Item | Classification |
|---|---|
| Raw materials used in production | Product cost |
| Production operator wages | Product cost |
| Factory machine depreciation | Product cost |
| Marketing campaign | Period cost |
| HR department salary | Period cost |
| Corporate office rent | Period cost |

Remember:

**Product cost follows the product through inventory until sale. Period cost is generally expensed in the period incurred.**

## 4.5 Relevant Costs versus Irrelevant Costs

This classification is particularly important for S&OP decision-making.

Hindi ito primarily based on the nature of the expense. It depends on the specific decision being evaluated.

### A. Relevant Costs

Costs that will change between the available alternatives and are expected to occur in the future.

Example:

Your factory has an unexpected increase in demand.

You have two alternatives:

- Option A: Produce using overtime.
- Option B: Outsource production to a subcontractor.

Relevant costs include:

| Option A: Overtime | Option B: Subcontracting |
|---|---|
| Additional overtime wages | Supplier production charge |
| Additional electricity | Transportation |
| Additional consumables | Additional inspection cost |
| Additional maintenance impact | Supplier qualification costs, if applicable |

These costs matter because they differ depending on the decision.

### B. Irrelevant Costs

Costs that do not change between the alternatives or are already incurred and cannot be recovered.

Example:

The company already purchased a machine last year for ₱10 million.

Now it needs to decide whether to use overtime or subcontract production.

The original ₱10 million machine purchase cost is a sunk cost.

It should not influence the decision between those two alternatives simply because it was already spent.

### C. Sunk Costs

A sunk cost is a cost already incurred that cannot be recovered through the current decision.

Example:

A company spent ₱500,000 on a feasibility study.

The study is completed. The company is now deciding whether to proceed with a new production project.

The ₱500,000 already spent is generally irrelevant to the forward-looking decision.

**Important:** A cost can be relevant in one decision and irrelevant in another.

For example, factory rent is generally irrelevant when choosing between two production schedules within the same existing facility. But it may become relevant if the decision is whether to open a new factory.

# 5. Manufacturing Cost Components

Now let's go deeper into the three major components of manufacturing cost.

## 5.1 Direct Materials (DM)

Direct materials are raw materials that become part of the finished product and can be traced to the product.

Example in MLCC manufacturing:

- Barium titanate (BT) powder.
- Binder.
- Plasticizer.
- Other materials incorporated into the product.

Suppose one unit requires:

| Material | Cost per unit |
|---|---:|
| BT powder | ₱12 |
| Binder | ₱4 |
| Additives | ₱3 |
| Other direct materials | ₱6 |
| **Total direct materials** | **₱25** |

Therefore:

\[
DM=₱25/unit
\]

For 10,000 units:

\[
DM=10,000 \times ₱25
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱250,000}
\]

Note: In actual cost accounting, material costs may also include applicable purchase costs, freight-in, and other costs necessary to bring materials to their present location and condition, subject to accounting policies.

## 5.2 Direct Labor (DL)

Direct labor refers to wages and related labor costs for employees directly involved in manufacturing the product.

Examples:
- Production operators.
- Machine operators.
- Assembly personnel.
- Other employees whose work can be traced to production.

Suppose:

- Direct labor cost = ₱120/hour.
- Labor requirement = 0.10 hour/unit.

Then:

\[
DL=120 \times 0.10
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱12/unit}
\]

For 10,000 units:

\[
10,000 \times ₱12=₱120,000
\]

Important: The 0.10 hour is an illustrative labor standard. Actual labor cost may include overtime premiums, benefits, and other employer costs depending on the company's accounting policy.

## 5.3 Manufacturing Overhead (MOH)

Manufacturing overhead consists of manufacturing costs other than direct materials and direct labor.

Examples:

| Cost | Possible behavior |
|---|---|
| Factory electricity | Variable or mixed |
| Factory rent | Fixed |
| Machine depreciation | Fixed |
| Machine maintenance | Fixed, variable, or mixed |
| Factory supervisor salary | Fixed |
| Indirect materials | Variable or mixed |
| Factory supplies | Variable or mixed |

Suppose monthly manufacturing overhead is:

| Component | Monthly cost |
|---|---:|
| Factory rent | ₱100,000 |
| Machine depreciation | ₱80,000 |
| Factory electricity | ₱60,000 |
| Maintenance | ₱30,000 |
| Factory supervision | ₱50,000 |
| **Total MOH** | **₱320,000** |

These are illustrative amounts.

If the factory produces 10,000 units:

\[
MOH/unit=\frac{320,000}{10,000}
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱32/unit}
\]

## 5.4 Total Manufacturing Cost

The basic formula is:

\[
\boxed{Manufacturing\ Cost=DM+DL+MOH}
\]

Using our example:

| Component | Cost per unit |
|---|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱25 |
| Direct labor | ₱12 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱32 |
| **Total manufacturing cost per unit** | **₱69** |

For 10,000 units:

\[
10,000 \times ₱69
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱690,000}
\]

This is the total manufacturing cost of the 10,000 units under our simplified assumptions.

## 5.5 Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM)

Important distinction:

Manufacturing cost incurred during a period is not always equal to the cost of products completed during that period.

Why?

Because some products may still be undergoing manufacturing at the beginning or end of the accounting period.

These unfinished products are called Work in Process (WIP).

The formula is:

\[
\boxed{COGM=Beginning\ WIP+Total\ Manufacturing\ Costs-Ending\ WIP}
\]

Where:

- Beginning WIP = value of unfinished goods at the start.
- Total Manufacturing Costs = DM + DL + MOH incurred during the period.
- Ending WIP = value of unfinished goods at the end.

### Step-by-step example

Suppose:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning WIP | ₱50,000 |
| Direct materials used | ₱250,000 |
| Direct labor | ₱120,000 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱320,000 |
| Ending WIP | ₱40,000 |

Step 1: Calculate total manufacturing costs.

\[
250,000+120,000+320,000
\]

\[
=₱690,000
\]

Step 2: Calculate COGM.

\[
COGM=50,000+690,000-40,000
\]

\[
\boxed{COGM=₱700,000}
\]

Interpretation:

The factory completed products with a manufacturing cost value of ₱700,000 during the period.

## 5.6 Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

COGS represents the cost of finished goods that were actually sold during the accounting period.

Not all manufactured products are automatically sold.

Some remain in Finished Goods Inventory.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{COGS=Beginning\ FG+COGM-Ending\ FG}
\]

Where:

- Beginning FG = beginning finished goods inventory.
- COGM = cost of goods manufactured.
- Ending FG = remaining finished goods inventory.

### Step-by-step example

Using the previous COGM:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning finished goods | ₱100,000 |
| COGM | ₱700,000 |
| Ending finished goods | ₱150,000 |

Therefore:

\[
COGS=100,000+700,000-150,000
\]

\[
\boxed{COGS=₱650,000}
\]

Interpretation:

The company manufactured products worth ₱700,000, but only ₱650,000 worth of product cost was recognized as COGS.

The remaining ₱150,000 represents the ending finished goods inventory.

## 5.7 Introduction to Standard Cost versus Actual Cost

Companies need a reference to determine whether their actual manufacturing performance is within expectations.

This is where standard costing comes in.

### A. Standard Cost

The predetermined expected cost to manufacture a product under specified operating conditions.

Example:

| Component | Standard cost per unit |
|---|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱25 |
| Direct labor | ₱12 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱30 |
| **Standard manufacturing cost** | **₱67** |

This means the company expects to manufacture one unit at ₱67.

### B. Actual Cost

The actual cost incurred based on real manufacturing activities and resource consumption.

Example:

| Component | Standard | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱25 | ₱27 |
| Direct labor | ₱12 | ₱11 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱30 | ₱34 |
| **Total** | **₱67** | **₱72** |

The actual cost is ₱5 higher per unit.

For 10,000 units:

\[
10,000 \times (72-67)
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱50,000\ unfavorable}
\]

The company needs to investigate why actual costs exceeded standard costs.

Possible reasons:
- Higher raw material prices.
- Excess material consumption.
- Higher scrap or rejection rates.
- Overtime.
- Higher electricity consumption.
- Lower production efficiency.
- Underutilized production capacity.

**S&OP connection:** The demand and supply plan determines the expected production volume, while the standard and actual cost analysis helps evaluate the financial consequences of executing that plan.

# 6. Cost Behavior and Cost Drivers

Now that we understand the manufacturing cost components, let's examine how these costs behave when production volume changes.

This is particularly important in S&OP because demand forecasts directly influence production requirements.

## 6.1 Total Fixed Cost versus Fixed Cost per Unit

Let's use a factory with a monthly fixed manufacturing cost of ₱200,000.

| Production volume | Total fixed cost | Fixed cost per unit |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱200 |
| 2,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱100 |
| 4,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱50 |
| 5,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱40 |
| 10,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱20 |

Formula:

\[
Fixed\ Cost/unit=\frac{Total\ Fixed\ Cost}{Production\ Volume}
\]

**Business interpretation:**

As production increases, the factory spreads its fixed costs across more units.

This is called fixed cost absorption.

However, it does not mean the company automatically becomes more profitable. Additional production must have sufficient demand, and additional variable costs must also be considered.

## 6.2 Total Variable Cost versus Variable Cost per Unit

Suppose the variable manufacturing cost is ₱40 per unit.

| Production volume | Variable cost per unit | Total variable cost |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | ₱40 | ₱40,000 |
| 2,000 | ₱40 | ₱80,000 |
| 4,000 | ₱40 | ₱160,000 |
| 5,000 | ₱40 | ₱200,000 |
| 10,000 | ₱40 | ₱400,000 |

Formula:

\[
Total\ Variable\ Cost=Variable\ Cost/unit \times Volume
\]

Notice the difference:

- Fixed cost changes per unit, but not in total.
- Variable cost changes in total, but generally not per unit.

These assumptions hold within the relevant range and under stable operating conditions.

## 6.3 Semi-Variable or Mixed Costs

Hindi lahat ng costs ay purely fixed or variable.

Some costs contain both fixed and variable components.

These are called mixed costs.

Example: Machine electricity.

Suppose a factory has:

- Basic monthly electricity charge: ₱20,000.
- Additional electricity cost: ₱5 per production unit.

Then:

\[
Total\ Electricity\ Cost=20,000+(5 \times Volume)
\]

| Production volume | Fixed portion | Variable portion | Total electricity cost |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | ₱20,000 | ₱5,000 | ₱25,000 |
| 2,000 | ₱20,000 | ₱10,000 | ₱30,000 |
| 4,000 | ₱20,000 | ₱20,000 | ₱40,000 |
| 6,000 | ₱20,000 | ₱30,000 | ₱50,000 |

Other examples:

- Equipment maintenance contracts with additional usage charges.
- Telephone and communication expenses.
- Utilities with fixed service charges.
- Labor with fixed salaries plus overtime.

## 6.4 Economies of Scale

**Economies of scale** occur when increasing production volume results in a lower average cost per unit, under appropriate operating conditions.

Example:

A factory has ₱200,000 fixed monthly costs and ₱40 variable cost per unit.

| Volume | Fixed cost/unit | Variable cost/unit | Total cost/unit |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | ₱200 | ₱40 | ₱240 |
| 2,000 | ₱100 | ₱40 | ₱140 |
| 4,000 | ₱50 | ₱40 | ₱90 |
| 5,000 | ₱40 | ₱40 | ₱80 |
| 10,000 | ₱20 | ₱40 | ₱60 |

Why does this happen?

Because the fixed manufacturing cost is distributed over a larger number of units.

However, economies of scale have limits.

At some point, increasing production may require:

- Additional production shifts.
- New equipment.
- Additional supervisors.
- Additional factory space.
- More maintenance.
- Higher overtime expenses.

Eventually, the average cost may stop decreasing or even increase.

## 6.5 Capacity Utilization and Unit Cost

Capacity utilization measures how much of the available production capacity is being used.

Formula:

\[
Capacity\ Utilization=\frac{Actual\ Output}{Available\ Capacity}\times100
\]

Example:

A factory has a monthly practical capacity of 10,000 units.

Actual production is 7,000 units.

\[
=\frac{7,000}{10,000}\times100
\]

\[
=\boxed{70\%}
\]

Now assume monthly fixed manufacturing costs are ₱200,000.

At 70% utilization:

\[
Fixed\ Cost/unit=\frac{200,000}{7,000}
\]

\[
=₱28.57
\]

At 100% utilization:

\[
Fixed\ Cost/unit=\frac{200,000}{10,000}
\]

\[
=₱20
\]

The fixed cost per unit is lower at full utilization.

But there is an important business consideration:

**Higher capacity utilization is not automatically better.**

Operating continuously at maximum capacity can result in:

- Increased equipment wear.
- Higher maintenance requirements.
- More breakdown risks.
- Less flexibility for urgent orders.
- Higher overtime.
- Increased production bottlenecks.

An S&OP analyst must balance cost efficiency with operational flexibility.

# 7. Formulas and Calculations

Let's consolidate the important formulas and understand how to use them.

## 7.1 Total Cost

\[
\boxed{TC=FC+VC}
\]

Where:

| Variable | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| TC | Total cost | ₱ |
| FC | Total fixed cost | ₱ |
| VC | Total variable cost | ₱ |

Example:

- Fixed cost = ₱200,000.
- Variable cost = ₱40 per unit.
- Production = 5,000 units.

First, calculate total variable cost:

\[
VC=40 \times 5,000
\]

\[
VC=₱200,000
\]

Then:

\[
TC=200,000+200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{TC=₱400,000}
\]

## 7.2 Total Variable Cost

\[
\boxed{TVC=V \times Q}
\]

Where:

- TVC = Total variable cost.
- V = Variable cost per unit.
- Q = Production volume in units.

Example:

Variable cost per unit = ₱75.

Production volume = 8,000 units.

\[
TVC=75 \times 8,000
\]

\[
\boxed{TVC=₱600,000}
\]

## 7.3 Average Cost per Unit

\[
\boxed{AC=\frac{TC}{Q}}
\]

Where:

- AC = Average cost per unit.
- TC = Total cost.
- Q = Production volume.

Example:

Total cost = ₱800,000.

Production = 10,000 units.

\[
AC=\frac{800,000}{10,000}
\]

\[
\boxed{AC=₱80/unit}
\]

Interpretation:

On average, the company incurs ₱80 to manufacture one unit based on the cost and production figures included in the calculation.

## 7.4 Manufacturing Cost per Unit

\[
\boxed{MC/unit=\frac{DM+DL+MOH}{Q}}
\]

Example:

| Component | Total cost |
|---|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱300,000 |
| Direct labor | ₱150,000 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱250,000 |
| **Total manufacturing cost** | **₱700,000** |

Production volume = 10,000 units.

\[
MC/unit=\frac{700,000}{10,000}
\]

\[
\boxed{₱70/unit}
\]

## 7.5 COGM

\[
\boxed{COGM=Beginning\ WIP+TMC-Ending\ WIP}
\]

Where:

- TMC = Total Manufacturing Costs.
- WIP = Work in Process.

Example:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning WIP | ₱30,000 |
| Total manufacturing costs | ₱500,000 |
| Ending WIP | ₱50,000 |

\[
COGM=30,000+500,000-50,000
\]

\[
\boxed{COGM=₱480,000}
\]

## 7.6 COGS

\[
\boxed{COGS=Beginning\ FG+COGM-Ending\ FG}
\]

Example:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning finished goods | ₱80,000 |
| COGM | ₱480,000 |
| Ending finished goods | ₱100,000 |

\[
COGS=80,000+480,000-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{COGS=₱460,000}
\]

## 7.7 Cost Variance

Cost variance compares actual cost against a predetermined standard or budget.

\[
\boxed{Cost\ Variance=Actual\ Cost-Standard\ Cost}
\]

Example:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Standard cost | ₱500,000 |
| Actual cost | ₱550,000 |
| Cost variance | ₱50,000 |

\[
550,000-500,000=₱50,000
\]

**Unfavorable (U):** The company spent ₱50,000 more than its standard cost.

Now consider another example:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Standard cost | ₱500,000 |
| Actual cost | ₱470,000 |
| Cost variance | -₱30,000 |

\[
470,000-500,000=-₱30,000
\]

**Favorable (F):** The company spent ₱30,000 less than the standard cost.

### Important: Favorable Does Not Always Mean Good

Suppose a factory reduces raw material usage, resulting in lower material cost.

That may be favorable.

But what if the reduction causes:

- Lower product quality.
- Higher rejection rates.
- More customer complaints.
- Lower product reliability.

Then the cost savings may actually create higher costs elsewhere.

Therefore, cost variance must be interpreted alongside quality, productivity, and customer service indicators.

### Cost Variance Percentage

A useful supplementary calculation is:

\[
Variance\%=\frac{Actual-Standard}{Standard}\times100
\]

Using the previous example:

\[
=\frac{550,000-500,000}{500,000}\times100
\]

\[
=\boxed{10\%\ Unfavorable}
\]

This helps compare cost performance across products or production periods with different cost levels.

### Common Calculation Errors

| Mistake | Why it is incorrect |
|---|---|
| Dividing fixed cost by units and treating it as total fixed cost | That calculation gives fixed cost per unit. |
| Treating all factory costs as variable | Some factory costs remain fixed within the relevant range. |
| Using production volume instead of sold volume for COGS | COGS reflects products sold, not simply products manufactured. |
| Adding ending WIP in COGM | Ending WIP must be deducted. |
| Ignoring beginning inventory | This produces an incomplete inventory reconciliation. |
| Comparing actual cost with standard cost without matching volume | The comparison may be misleading. |
| Assuming favorable variance means operational success | Quality and efficiency must also be considered. |

**Professional note:** For a meaningful standard cost variance, compare actual cost with the standard cost allowed for the actual output, using consistent cost scope and accounting treatment. Comparing actual spending with a fixed original budget despite a different production volume can produce misleading results. We will use this principle in later financial analysis.

# 8. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's combine everything into one manufacturing scenario.

## Scenario: MLCC Manufacturing Factory

Imagine that an MLCC factory needs to evaluate its manufacturing costs under different production volumes.

The Finance and Operations teams have prepared the following illustrative monthly cost assumptions.

### 8.1 Manufacturing Cost Structure

**A. Direct Materials**

| Material | Cost per unit |
|---|---:|
| BT powder | ₱15 |
| Binder and additives | ₱8 |
| Other direct materials | ₱7 |
| **Total** | **₱30** |

**B. Direct Labor**

| Item | Value |
|---|---:|
| Labor cost per hour | ₱120 |
| Standard labor hours per unit | 0.10 hour |
| **Labor cost per unit** | **₱12** |

Calculation:

\[
120 \times 0.10=₱12/unit
\]

**C. Variable Manufacturing Overhead**

| Cost component | Per unit |
|---|---:|
| Electricity and utilities | ₱5 |
| Consumables | ₱3 |
| Variable maintenance | ₱2 |
| **Total** | **₱10** |

**D. Fixed Manufacturing Overhead**

| Cost component | Monthly cost |
|---|---:|
| Factory rent | ₱80,000 |
| Machine depreciation | ₱70,000 |
| Fixed maintenance contracts | ₱30,000 |
| Factory supervision | ₱20,000 |
| **Total** | **₱200,000** |

### 8.2 Calculate the Total Manufacturing Cost

First, calculate variable manufacturing cost per unit:

\[
30+12+10=₱52/unit
\]

Then calculate total manufacturing cost:

\[
\boxed{TMC=FC+(VC/unit \times Q)}
\]

Where:

- FC = ₱200,000 monthly fixed manufacturing cost.
- VC/unit = ₱52.
- Q = Production volume.

Now let's evaluate three production scenarios.

### Scenario A: Low Production Volume

Production = 5,000 units.

Step 1: Calculate variable manufacturing cost.

\[
52 \times 5,000=₱260,000
\]

Step 2: Add fixed manufacturing cost.

\[
260,000+200,000=₱460,000
\]

Step 3: Calculate manufacturing cost per unit.

\[
\frac{460,000}{5,000}=₱92
\]

**Result: ₱92 per unit**

### Scenario B: Medium Production Volume

Production = 10,000 units.

Step 1:

\[
52 \times 10,000=₱520,000
\]

Step 2:

\[
520,000+200,000=₱720,000
\]

Step 3:

\[
\frac{720,000}{10,000}=₱72
\]

**Result: ₱72 per unit**

### Scenario C: High Production Volume

Production = 20,000 units.

Step 1:

\[
52 \times 20,000=₱1,040,000
\]

Step 2:

\[
1,040,000+200,000=₱1,240,000
\]

Step 3:

\[
\frac{1,240,000}{20,000}=₱62
\]

**Result: ₱62 per unit**

### 8.3 Compare All Three Scenarios

| Metric | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Production volume | 5,000 | 10,000 | 20,000 |
| Direct materials | ₱150,000 | ₱300,000 | ₱600,000 |
| Direct labor | ₱60,000 | ₱120,000 | ₱240,000 |
| Variable overhead | ₱50,000 | ₱100,000 | ₱200,000 |
| Fixed overhead | ₱200,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱200,000 |
| **Total cost** | **₱460,000** | **₱720,000** | **₱1,240,000** |
| **Cost per unit** | **₱92** | **₱72** | **₱62** |

### 8.4 What Can We Learn?

Notice three things.

**Observation 1: Total manufacturing cost increases.**

As production increases from 5,000 to 20,000 units, total manufacturing cost increases from ₱460,000 to ₱1,240,000.

This is expected because the company consumes more materials, labor, and variable overhead.

**Observation 2: Unit manufacturing cost decreases.**

Cost per unit decreases from ₱92 to ₱62.

Why?

Because the same ₱200,000 fixed overhead is spread over a larger production volume.

**Observation 3: High production is not necessarily the best decision.**

Suppose customer demand is only 5,000 units.

Producing 20,000 units just to achieve a lower manufacturing cost per unit may create:

- Excess finished goods inventory.
- Additional storage requirements.
- Higher working capital requirements.
- Potential obsolescence.
- Increased risk of unsold products.

The company may have a lower calculated manufacturing cost per unit but a worse overall financial position.

This is one of the most important lessons in S&OP:

**The lowest manufacturing cost per unit does not automatically produce the best business outcome.**

## 8.5 Capacity Utilization Analysis

Assume the factory's monthly practical capacity is 20,000 units.

| Scenario | Production | Capacity utilization |
|---|---:|---:|
| Low | 5,000 | 25% |
| Medium | 10,000 | 50% |
| High | 20,000 | 100% |

At low utilization, fixed overhead is spread across fewer units.

At high utilization, fixed overhead absorption improves.

However, 100% utilization leaves very little room for unexpected demand, machine downtime, or maintenance.

In a real factory, we would also investigate whether 20,000 units is sustainable capacity, theoretical capacity, or practical capacity.

These are not necessarily the same.

## 8.6 Introducing Production Efficiency

So far, we assumed that variable manufacturing cost remains ₱52 per unit regardless of volume.

In reality, this may not happen.

For example, increasing production could lead to:

- More machine downtime.
- Increased rejection rate.
- Higher scrap consumption.
- Additional overtime.
- Lower production efficiency.

Suppose the high-volume scenario experiences additional costs:

| Additional cost | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Overtime premium | ₱30,000 |
| Additional maintenance | ₱15,000 |
| Additional scrap | ₱25,000 |
| **Total additional cost** | **₱70,000** |

Revised total manufacturing cost:

\[
1,240,000+70,000=₱1,310,000
\]

Revised unit cost:

\[
\frac{1,310,000}{20,000}
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱65.50/unit}
\]

Instead of ₱62, the actual cost per unit becomes ₱65.50.

This demonstrates why cost planning must consider both volume and operational efficiency.

# 9. Cost Management in S&OP

Ito na ang pinaka-importanteng connection ng topic sa actual S&OP responsibilities.

Sa S&OP, hindi lang natin tinatanong kung kaya bang gawin ng factory ang required demand.

Kailangan din nating malaman kung magkano ang magiging financial impact ng iba't ibang ways to fulfill that demand.

## 9.1 How Demand Planning Affects Costs

Suppose the demand plan changes.

| Month | Original demand | Revised demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 8,000 |
| February | 10,000 | 15,000 |
| March | 10,000 | 18,000 |

What happens?

January:
- Lower production requirements.
- Potential underutilization.
- Higher fixed cost per unit.

February:
- Higher production requirements.
- Additional labor may be required.
- Procurement requirements increase.

March:
- Potential capacity constraints.
- Overtime or subcontracting may become necessary.
- Material supply risks increase.

An S&OP analyst must identify these cost implications before finalizing the plan.

## 9.2 How Supply Planning Decisions Affect Costs

Let's discuss four important scenarios.

### A. Overtime

Overtime allows a company to increase production without immediately investing in additional permanent capacity.

However, overtime creates additional labor costs.

Example:

Normal hourly rate = ₱120.

Overtime hourly rate = ₱180.

Additional overtime hours = 100.

\[
Overtime\ Cost=180 \times 100
\]

\[
=₱18,000
\]

If the same 100 hours were worked at the normal rate, the cost would be ₱12,000.

Additional overtime premium:

\[
18,000-12,000=₱6,000
\]

The additional premium is the incremental overtime cost in this simplified example.

**S&OP question:** Is overtime cheaper than subcontracting or increasing regular production capacity?

### B. Subcontracting

Subcontracting means transferring some production requirements to an external supplier or manufacturing partner.

Example:

| Cost component | Internal production | Subcontracting |
|---|---:|---:|
| Manufacturing cost | ₱70/unit | ₱85/unit |
| Transportation | Included internally | ₱3/unit |
| Additional inspection | ₱0 | ₱2/unit |
| **Total relevant cost** | **₱70/unit** | **₱90/unit** |

Subcontracting costs ₱20 more per unit based on these assumptions.

But if internal production is already at full capacity, subcontracting may still be the better decision because it prevents:

- Customer delivery delays.
- Excessive overtime.
- Production disruptions.
- Lost sales opportunities.

The decision must consider more than unit cost.

### C. Inventory Build-Ahead

Inventory build-ahead means producing products earlier than their actual demand period.

Example:

Expected demand:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 5,000 |
| February | 15,000 |

The factory has limited February capacity.

One option is to produce 5,000 extra units in January and store them for February demand.

Potential benefits:
- Avoids February overtime.
- Reduces capacity pressure.
- Supports customer delivery requirements.
- Enables more stable production.

Potential costs:
- Inventory holding costs.
- Warehouse requirements.
- Working capital tied up in inventory.
- Obsolescence risk.
- Possible additional handling.

**Important:** Build-ahead production does not necessarily increase the product's manufacturing cost in the period when it is completed, but it can increase inventory carrying costs and change the timing of cash requirements.

### D. Production Rescheduling

Production rescheduling means changing when and where production occurs to better match demand and available capacity.

Example:

The original production schedule is:

| Week | Production |
|---|---:|
| Week 1 | 5,000 |
| Week 2 | 5,000 |
| Week 3 | 15,000 |
| Week 4 | 15,000 |

However, the factory has lower utilization in the first two weeks.

The planner may move some Week 3 and Week 4 production earlier.

Potential benefits:
- Better utilization.
- Reduced overtime.
- Smoother production workload.
- Lower capacity-related costs.

Potential disadvantages:
- Higher early inventory.
- More warehouse requirements.
- Additional changeovers.
- Material availability constraints.

The best schedule balances total operating costs, not just production costs.

## 9.3 How an S&OP Analyst Identifies Cost Risks and Opportunities

As an S&OP analyst, you will likely work with demand, supply, inventory, and financial data.

You need to identify situations where operational plans create financial consequences.

Here are some examples:

| Operational indicator | Possible cost implication |
|---|---|
| Forecast increases significantly | Additional materials and production costs |
| Capacity utilization approaches maximum | Overtime or subcontracting |
| Low production utilization | Higher fixed cost per unit |
| High scrap rate | Higher material consumption |
| Excess inventory | Increased carrying cost |
| Frequent production rescheduling | Additional changeover and planning costs |
| Supplier lead-time increases | Expediting and premium freight |
| Low production yield | Higher cost per good unit |
| Machine downtime increases | Higher maintenance and lost production costs |

A good S&OP analyst does not simply report that production cost increased.

They investigate:

1. What changed?
2. Why did it change?
3. How much financial impact did it create?
4. Is it temporary or recurring?
5. What alternatives are available?
6. What is the recommended action?

## 9.4 Cost Performance KPIs

These are useful indicators that may appear in manufacturing and S&OP reviews.

| KPI | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Manufacturing Cost per Unit | Average manufacturing cost for each unit |
| Material Cost per Unit | Material cost consumed per unit |
| Labor Cost per Unit | Direct labor cost per unit |
| Overhead Cost per Unit | Manufacturing overhead allocated per unit |
| Cost Variance % | Difference between actual and standard/budget |
| Scrap Cost | Financial impact of material and production losses |
| Overtime Cost | Additional labor expense |
| Inventory Carrying Cost | Cost of holding inventory |
| Capacity Utilization | Usage of available production capacity |
| Cost of Poor Quality | Financial impact of defects and quality failures |

An important consideration: Always check how a company's KPI is defined. For example, manufacturing cost per unit may use total production, good output, or another defined denominator.

## 9.5 Finance and Operations Collaboration

Cost management is not the responsibility of Finance alone.

Both Finance and Operations must work together.

| Function | Main responsibility |
|---|---|
| Demand Planning | Provides expected demand volume and timing |
| Supply Planning | Develops feasible supply and production plans |
| Manufacturing Operations | Provides actual production, labor, and efficiency data |
| Procurement | Provides material prices, supplier conditions, and purchasing risks |
| Finance | Validates costs, budgets, standards, and financial impacts |
| S&OP Leadership | Evaluates alternatives and approves business direction |

### Example: Monthly S&OP Discussion

Imagine that the demand plan increases by 20%.

The Supply Planning team proposes overtime.

Operations estimates additional production capacity.

Procurement identifies potential raw material price increases.

Finance calculates the incremental manufacturing cost.

The S&OP team then evaluates:

- Is the additional demand profitable?
- Is overtime financially justified?
- Is subcontracting a better option?
- Should the company build inventory earlier?
- Can production be redistributed across plants?
- What is the impact on total operating costs?

The final decision should reflect the combined operational and financial consequences.

# 10. Business Perspective and Decision-Making

Let's now move from understanding costs to thinking like an S&OP professional.

## 10.1 Cost Is Not Just About Spending Less

Imagine two production alternatives.

| Metric | Option A | Option B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Production volume | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Manufacturing cost/unit | ₱70 | ₱65 |
| Rejection rate | 1% | 5% |
| Customer delivery performance | 98% | 90% |

At first glance, Option B appears cheaper.

However, it has higher rejection and lower delivery performance.

If the company chooses Option B based solely on unit manufacturing cost, it may overlook additional costs associated with defects, rework, and customer service failures.

A professional S&OP analyst must evaluate total business impact.

## 10.2 Five Important Questions Before Making a Cost Decision

1. **Cost efficiency:** Will this decision reduce total cost or only cost per unit?
2. **Capacity:** Can the production system support the proposed volume?
3. **Inventory:** Will the decision create excess inventory?
4. **Service:** Will customer demand be fulfilled on time?
5. **Profitability:** Will the financial benefits outweigh the additional costs and risks?

## 10.3 The Difference Between Operational and Financial Optimization

Operational optimization focuses on improving manufacturing performance.

Examples:
- Higher machine utilization.
- Lower downtime.
- Higher yield.
- Better production scheduling.

Financial optimization considers the financial consequences of those improvements.

Examples:
- Lower cost per good unit.
- Lower inventory carrying cost.
- Reduced overtime expense.
- Improved gross margin.
- Better working capital utilization.

The two must work together.

A production schedule that maximizes utilization may not necessarily maximize profitability if it creates excess inventory.

# 11. Key Takeaways

Before proceeding to the knowledge check, let's consolidate the most important concepts.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Cost | Value of resources used or acquired for a purpose |
| Expense | Cost recognized in the income statement |
| Expenditure | Spending or commitment to acquire resources |
| Cash outflow | Actual movement of cash out of the business |
| Fixed cost | Remains constant in total within the relevant range |
| Variable cost | Changes in total with activity volume |
| Direct cost | Can be traced to a cost object |
| Indirect cost | Cannot be conveniently traced to a cost object |
| Manufacturing cost | Direct materials + direct labor + manufacturing overhead |
| Product cost | Initially included in inventory |
| Period cost | Generally expensed in the period incurred |
| Relevant cost | Future cost that differs between alternatives |
| COGM | Cost of goods completed during the period |
| COGS | Cost of finished goods sold during the period |
| Standard cost | Predetermined expected cost |
| Actual cost | Cost actually incurred |
| Cost variance | Actual cost minus standard or budgeted cost |

**Seven formulas to remember:**

\[
TC=FC+VC
\]

\[
TVC=Variable\ Cost/unit \times Q
\]

\[
Average\ Cost/unit=\frac{Total\ Cost}{Q}
\]

\[
Manufacturing\ Cost=DM+DL+MOH
\]

\[
COGM=Beginning\ WIP+TMC-Ending\ WIP
\]

\[
COGS=Beginning\ FG+COGM-Ending\ FG
\]

\[
Cost\ Variance=Actual-Standard
\]

The biggest lesson:

**In S&OP, the objective is not simply to minimize manufacturing cost. It is to develop a demand and supply plan that balances cost, capacity, inventory, customer service, and profitability.**
