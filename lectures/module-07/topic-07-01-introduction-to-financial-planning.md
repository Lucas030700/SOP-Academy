# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What will we discuss?

Sa previous modules, pinag-aralan natin kung paano magplano ng demand, production, supply, inventory, at paano mag-resolve ng demand-supply imbalances.

Pero may isang importanteng tanong na kailangan pang sagutin:

**Even if the company can meet customer demand, will the business actually make money from its operational plan?**

For example:

A manufacturing company receives a forecast of 100,000 units next month.

The Operations team confirms:
- Available ang production capacity.
- Sufficient ang raw materials.
- Available ang manpower.
- Kayang ma-deliver ang 100,000 units.

Operationally, feasible ang plan.

Pero paano kung:
- Masyadong mataas ang material costs?
- Kailangan ng expensive overtime?
- Magiging excessive ang finished goods inventory?
- Mababa ang selling price compared sa production cost?
- Kailangan ng malaking cash investment para bumili ng raw materials?

Possible na successful ang production operations pero hindi financially attractive ang business plan.

Dito pumapasok ang **Financial Planning**.

## 1.2 Why is financial planning important?

Companies do not operate just to manufacture products or achieve production targets.

They operate to create business value while maintaining financial sustainability.

Financial planning helps companies understand:

1. How much revenue they expect to generate.
2. How much money they need to spend.
3. How much profit they expect to earn.
4. How much cash they need to support operations.
5. How much inventory investment is required.
6. Whether the operational plan supports the company's financial objectives.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

Let's start with a simple manufacturing business.

Imagine that you own a company producing industrial components.

You sell each component for ₱100.

Your customer forecast indicates that you will sell 1,000 units next month.

Your expected sales revenue is:

\[
₱100 \times 1,000 = ₱100,000
\]

Mukhang maganda, right?

But manufacturing those products requires money.

| Expense | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Raw materials | ₱35,000 |
| Direct labor | ₱15,000 |
| Factory overhead | ₱10,000 |
| Total manufacturing cost | ₱60,000 |

Your remaining amount after manufacturing costs:

\[
₱100,000-₱60,000=₱40,000
\]

Pero hindi pa tapos.

May additional expenses ka pa:

| Expense | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Office salaries | ₱8,000 |
| Marketing | ₱3,000 |
| Administration | ₱2,000 |
| Total operating expenses | ₱13,000 |

Your remaining profit:

\[
₱40,000-₱13,000=₱27,000
\]

**Simple financial picture**

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sales revenue | ₱100,000 |
| Manufacturing costs | ₱60,000 |
| Operating expenses | ₱13,000 |
| **Operating profit** | **₱27,000** |

Illustrative simplified example. Assumes all 1,000 units are sold and all stated costs are recognized in the period.

Now imagine that the Operations team proposes increasing production to 1,500 units.

Production can accommodate the additional 500 units.

But will that automatically increase profit?

Not necessarily.

What if the additional production requires:
- Overtime labor.
- Additional machine operating hours.
- Expedited raw material purchases.
- More warehouse space.
- Additional inventory that customers will not immediately purchase.

The company might produce more but generate less cash or even lower profit.

**This is the fundamental purpose of financial planning: to understand the money-related consequences of business decisions before committing resources.**

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 What is Financial Planning?

**Financial Planning** is the process of estimating, organizing, evaluating, and managing a company's expected financial performance and resource requirements over a defined period.

It translates business activities into financial measures.

For manufacturing companies, it connects:

- Sales volume
- Selling prices
- Production quantities
- Material consumption
- Labor requirements
- Manufacturing overhead
- Inventory levels
- Operating expenses
- Revenue and profitability
- Cash requirements

The objective is to determine whether the company's business plans are financially sustainable and aligned with its financial targets.

## 3.2 Operational Planning vs. Financial Planning

These two planning disciplines are closely related, but they answer different questions.

| Operational Planning | Financial Planning |
|---|---|
| What should we produce? | How much will production cost? |
| How much material is required? | How much money is required to purchase materials? |
| How much capacity is available? | What is the financial impact of capacity utilization? |
| How many employees are needed? | What are the labor expenses? |
| How much inventory should we maintain? | How much capital is tied up in inventory? |
| Can we meet customer demand? | Can we meet demand profitably? |

**Example:**

Operations proposes producing 50,000 units.

Finance evaluates:
- Material expenditure.
- Labor costs.
- Manufacturing overhead.
- Inventory investment.
- Expected revenue.
- Expected profitability.

Both departments are examining the same production plan, but from different perspectives.

### Why operational feasibility does not guarantee financial feasibility

Consider these three situations:

| Situation | Operational result | Financial implication |
|---|---|---|
| Excess production | Production target achieved | Excess inventory and tied-up cash |
| Overtime production | Demand fulfilled | Higher labor costs |
| Expedited procurement | Materials arrive on time | Higher purchasing and transportation costs |

The key lesson:

**Operational feasibility means the company can execute the plan. Financial feasibility means the company can justify and sustain the plan financially.**

A good S&OP plan must consider both.

## 3.3 Relationship Between S&OP and Financial Planning

Traditional operational planning focuses on balancing demand and supply.

Financial planning adds another dimension: evaluating the financial consequences of that balance.

Consider this relationship:

**Demand Planning**  
Expected customer demand and sales volume

↓

**Supply Planning**  
Production, capacity, materials, and labor requirements

↓

**Inventory and Reconciliation**  
Inventory positioning and demand-supply trade-offs

↓

**Financial Planning**  
Revenue, costs, profit, working capital, and cash flow

↓

**Integrated Business Plan**  
One aligned operational and financial direction

In actual S&OP practice, this is not strictly a one-way process. Financial evaluation can trigger changes to demand assumptions, supply plans, inventory policies, or business priorities.

For example:

Finance identifies that the proposed inventory increase requires too much working capital.

Operations may then evaluate:
- Reducing production batch sizes.
- Improving production scheduling.
- Increasing inventory turnover.
- Negotiating shorter supplier lead times.
- Prioritizing products with higher demand.

This creates a continuous feedback loop between Operations and Finance.

# 4. Detailed Discussion: Key Financial Concepts

Let's establish the basic financial vocabulary first. These concepts will appear repeatedly throughout Module 7.

## 4.1 Revenue, Expenses, Costs, and Profit

These four terms are related, but they are not interchangeable.

**A. Revenue**

Revenue is the amount earned by a company from selling products or providing services during a particular accounting period.

Example:

A company sells 10,000 units at ₱150 per unit.

\[
Revenue=10,000\times ₱150
\]

\[
Revenue=₱1,500,000
\]

Important: Revenue is generally recognized when the applicable accounting revenue-recognition requirements are met, not necessarily when cash is received.

**B. Cost**

Cost is the monetary value of resources consumed or acquired to produce goods or deliver services.

Manufacturing examples:
- Raw materials.
- Direct labor.
- Factory electricity.
- Machine depreciation.
- Production-related maintenance.

**C. Expense**

An expense is a cost recognized against income during an accounting period.

Examples:
- Office salaries.
- Marketing expenses.
- Administrative expenses.
- Rent.
- Depreciation.

A useful distinction:

Not every cost becomes an expense immediately.

For example, raw materials purchased but not yet consumed may remain as inventory on the Balance Sheet. The cost generally becomes part of COGS when the corresponding finished products are sold.

**D. Profit**

Profit is the financial amount remaining after deducting applicable costs and expenses from revenue.

\[
Profit=Revenue-Expenses
\]

However, there are different levels of profit, which we will discuss later.

## 4.2 Fixed Costs vs. Variable Costs

This classification explains how costs behave when production volume changes.

**Fixed Costs**

Costs that generally remain constant within a relevant operating range and time period, regardless of short-term production volume.

Examples:
- Factory building rent.
- Salaried factory supervision.
- Equipment depreciation.
- Fixed monthly service contracts.

**Variable Costs**

Costs that generally change with production or sales volume.

Examples:
- Raw materials.
- Packaging materials.
- Piece-rate labor.
- Production consumables.

| Production volume | Fixed cost | Variable cost |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1,000 units | ₱100,000 | ₱50,000 |
| 2,000 units | ₱100,000 | ₱100,000 |
| 3,000 units | ₱100,000 | ₱150,000 |

**How costs behave as production increases**

Illustrative values in thousands of pesos. Variable cost is ₱50 per unit; fixed cost remains ₱100,000.

| Production volume | Fixed cost (₱000) | Variable cost (₱000) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1,000 | 100 | 50 |
| 2,000 | 100 | 100 |
| 3,000 | 100 | 150 |
| 4,000 | 100 | 200 |

**Important concept:**

Fixed cost per unit decreases as production increases, assuming total fixed costs remain unchanged.

For example:

- ₱100,000 fixed cost ÷ 1,000 units = ₱100/unit.
- ₱100,000 fixed cost ÷ 2,000 units = ₱50/unit.

This is one reason companies consider production volume when evaluating manufacturing efficiency.

However, fixed costs are not unlimited. Additional shifts, equipment, or facilities can create step-fixed costs when capacity thresholds are exceeded.

## 4.3 Direct Costs vs. Indirect Costs

This classification answers another question:

**Can we directly trace the cost to a specific product, production batch, or cost object?**

| Direct costs | Indirect costs |
|---|---|
| Direct materials | Factory electricity |
| Direct production labor | Factory supervision |
| Product-specific components | General factory maintenance |
| Traceable packaging | Factory security |

Example:

A company manufactures Product A and Product B.

The material used specifically for Product A can be traced directly to Product A.

However, electricity consumed by a shared production facility may need to be allocated between both products.

That makes it an indirect cost.

**Remember:** Direct versus indirect describes traceability. Fixed versus variable describes cost behavior. These are two different classifications.

A cost can be:
- Direct and variable.
- Indirect and fixed.
- Indirect and variable.
- Direct and fixed, depending on the circumstances.

## 4.4 Operating Expenses vs. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

This is especially important in manufacturing financial analysis.

**Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** represents the cost of products sold during the accounting period.

Manufacturing COGS commonly includes:
- Direct materials consumed.
- Direct labor.
- Allocated manufacturing overhead.

**Operating Expenses (OPEX)** are expenses associated with running the business that are not included in the cost of producing the goods sold.

Examples:
- Sales department salaries.
- Office administration.
- Marketing.
- Corporate IT.
- General administrative expenses.

| Manufacturing example | Typical classification |
|---|---|
| Raw materials consumed in production | Manufacturing cost |
| Production operator wages | Manufacturing cost |
| Factory equipment depreciation | Manufacturing overhead |
| Sales team salaries | OPEX |
| Marketing campaign | OPEX |
| Corporate office rent | OPEX |

A critical distinction:

Raw materials purchased are not automatically COGS. They first enter inventory, are consumed in production, become part of finished goods, and generally become COGS when the products are sold.

This connects directly to Module 5: Inventory Management.

## 4.5 Profitability and Contribution Margin

Profitability measures how effectively a company generates profit from its business activities.

One important concept is **Contribution Margin**.

Contribution margin tells us how much revenue remains after deducting variable costs.

This remaining amount is available to cover fixed costs and then generate profit.

Example:

Selling price = ₱100/unit.

Variable cost = ₱60/unit.

\[
CM=₱100-₱60=₱40
\]

The company earns ₱40 contribution per unit.

If the company sells 1,000 units:

\[
Total\ CM=₱40\times1,000
\]

\[
Total\ CM=₱40,000
\]

If fixed costs are ₱25,000:

\[
Profit=₱40,000-₱25,000
\]

\[
Profit=₱15,000
\]

**Why is this useful in S&OP?**

Suppose production capacity is limited.

The company has two products:

| | Product A | Product B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Selling price | ₱100 | ₱150 |
| Variable cost | ₱70 | ₱100 |
| Contribution per unit | ₱30 | ₱50 |

Product B has a higher contribution per unit.

However, if Product A requires one machine hour and Product B requires three machine hours, Product A may generate a higher contribution per constrained machine hour.

This is where financial analysis becomes useful in supply planning and demand prioritization.

## 4.6 Introduction to Working Capital

Working capital represents the short-term financial resources available to support daily business operations.

A basic formula is:

\[
Working\ Capital=Current\ Assets-Current\ Liabilities
\]

**Current Assets** are resources expected to be realized, sold, or consumed within the normal operating cycle or generally within 12 months.

Examples:
- Cash.
- Accounts receivable.
- Raw materials inventory.
- Work-in-process inventory.
- Finished goods inventory.

**Current Liabilities** are obligations generally due within 12 months or the normal operating cycle.

Examples:
- Accounts payable.
- Short-term loans.
- Accrued expenses.
- Current portions of long-term debt.

### Why does working capital matter in S&OP?

Imagine that the company decides to increase production from 10,000 units to 20,000 units.

It may need:
- More raw materials.
- More work-in-process inventory.
- More finished goods.
- More supplier payments.

Even if the company expects higher future sales, it may need additional cash to finance the increased inventory before customers pay.

This is why inventory planning and financial planning must work together.

## 4.7 Introduction to Cash Flow

Cash flow measures the movement of actual cash into and out of the business.

Examples of cash inflows:
- Customer payments.
- Loan proceeds.
- Investment proceeds.

Examples of cash outflows:
- Supplier payments.
- Employee salaries.
- Rent.
- Equipment purchases.
- Loan repayments.

**Important distinction: Profit is not the same as cash flow.**

Example:

A company sells ₱500,000 worth of products on 30-day credit terms.

The sale may be recognized as revenue today.

But the customer will pay after 30 days.

Therefore:

- Revenue can increase today.
- Accounting profit can increase today.
- Cash may not arrive until later.

A company can be profitable but experience cash shortages.

Conversely, it can receive cash from a loan without generating any profit from that transaction.

This distinction becomes extremely important when planning inventory and procurement.

# 5. Financial Statements and Business Planning

Now that we understand the basic financial terms, let's connect them to the three major financial statements.

These statements are important because they provide different views of the company's financial condition.

Think of them as three different questions:

1. Income Statement: Is the company making money?
2. Balance Sheet: What does the company own and owe?
3. Cash Flow Statement: Where is the company's cash coming from and going?

## 5.1 Income Statement

The **Income Statement**, also called the Profit and Loss Statement (P&L), summarizes a company's revenues, costs, and expenses over a specific period.

It tells us whether the company generated a profit or loss.

A simplified manufacturing Income Statement:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sales Revenue | ₱1,000,000 |
| Less: COGS | (₱600,000) |
| **Gross Profit** | **₱400,000** |
| Less: Operating Expenses | (₱200,000) |
| **Operating Profit** | **₱200,000** |

This is a simplified example before interest and income taxes.

### How does S&OP affect the Income Statement?

| Operational decision | Financial impact |
|---|---|
| Increase sales volume | Potentially higher revenue |
| Increase selling price | Higher revenue per unit |
| Increase material consumption | Higher manufacturing costs |
| Increase overtime | Higher labor costs |
| Improve production efficiency | Potentially lower unit costs |
| Reduce scrap and defects | Lower material losses and rework costs |
| Improve product mix | Potentially higher gross profit |
| Increase finished goods sales | Higher recognized COGS alongside revenue |

Notice that operational decisions directly affect financial performance.

## 5.2 Balance Sheet

The **Balance Sheet** presents a company's financial position at a specific point in time.

It shows:

- Assets: What the company owns or controls.
- Liabilities: What the company owes.
- Equity: The residual interest of owners in the business.

The fundamental accounting equation is:

\[
Assets=Liabilities+Equity
\]

Example:

| Assets | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | ₱200,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | ₱300,000 |
| Inventory | ₱500,000 |
| Equipment and other assets | ₱1,000,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **₱2,000,000** |

| Liabilities and Equity | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts Payable | ₱400,000 |
| Loans | ₱600,000 |
| Equity | ₱1,000,000 |
| **Total Liabilities and Equity** | **₱2,000,000** |

The two sides must balance.

### How does S&OP affect the Balance Sheet?

Consider these operational decisions:

**A. Increasing raw material inventory**

The company purchases additional raw materials.

- Inventory increases.
- Cash decreases if paid immediately.
- Accounts payable increases if purchased on credit.

**B. Increasing finished goods inventory**

The company produces more products than customers currently need.

- Finished goods inventory increases.
- Cash may have already been spent on materials and labor.
- Working capital becomes tied up in inventory.

**C. Increasing sales on credit**

The company delivers products to customers who will pay later.

- Accounts receivable increases.
- Revenue may be recognized.
- Cash has not necessarily been received.

This is why a production plan should not be evaluated using the Income Statement alone.

## 5.3 Cash Flow Statement

The **Cash Flow Statement** summarizes actual cash movements during a particular period.

It has three major sections:

| Section | Meaning | Manufacturing example |
|---|---|---|
| Operating Activities | Cash from normal business operations | Customer collections, supplier payments, salaries |
| Investing Activities | Cash used for or received from long-term investments | Purchasing production equipment |
| Financing Activities | Cash from borrowing, repayment, and owner financing | Bank loans and loan repayments |

### How does S&OP affect cash flow?

Example:

The company decides to purchase ₱1 million worth of raw materials.

If it pays immediately:

- Cash decreases by ₱1 million.
- Inventory increases by ₱1 million.
- No immediate reduction in profit simply because the materials were purchased.

When the materials are consumed and the products are eventually sold, the related manufacturing cost is recognized through COGS, subject to the applicable accounting treatment.

This distinction is fundamental.

## 5.4 Revenue vs. Profit vs. Cash

Let's compare these three concepts using one business transaction.

A manufacturer sells 100 units at ₱1,000 each.

The cost of those units is ₱70,000.

The customer will pay after 30 days.

| Financial measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱100,000 |
| COGS | ₱70,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱30,000 |
| Cash received immediately | ₱0 |

The transaction can generate revenue and gross profit without an immediate cash collection.

This is why S&OP needs to consider both financial performance and cash requirements.

**Remember these distinctions**

- **Revenue:** Value of goods and services sold or delivered under applicable recognition rules.
- **Profit:** Financial result after deducting recognized costs and expenses.
- **Cash:** Actual money received and paid.

## 5.5 Budget, Forecast, and Actual Results

These three terms are commonly used in business planning and financial reporting.

| Term | Definition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | Approved financial plan or target | Establish financial expectations |
| Forecast | Updated estimate of expected future results | Reflect the latest business outlook |
| Actual | Financial results already realized and recorded | Measure actual performance |

Let's use a manufacturing example.

The company's original monthly sales budget is 50,000 units.

During the month, customer demand changes.

The updated forecast becomes 45,000 units.

At the end of the month, actual sales are 42,000 units.

| Measure | Sales volume |
|---|---:|
| Budget | 50,000 units |
| Forecast | 45,000 units |
| Actual | 42,000 units |

### What does this tell us?

Budget vs. Actual:

\[
42,000-50,000=-8,000\ units
\]

The company sold 8,000 fewer units than its original target.

Forecast vs. Actual:

\[
42,000-45,000=-3,000\ units
\]

The company sold 3,000 fewer units than its latest forecast.

These comparisons help Finance and Operations understand performance gaps.

**Important S&OP concept:** The forecast should represent the latest realistic expectation, not simply repeat the original budget.

The budget is generally a financial commitment or target. The forecast is an updated view of what the company now expects to happen.

# 6. Financial Integration in S&OP

Ito ang pinaka-importanteng section ng topic.

We now combine the concepts from Demand Planning, Supply Planning, Inventory Management, and Demand-Supply Reconciliation.

The goal is to translate operational plans into financial outcomes.

## 6.1 Translating Demand Plans into Revenue Expectations

From Module 3, we learned that demand planning estimates future customer requirements.

However, demand volume alone does not tell us how much revenue the company expects to generate.

We need selling prices and the expected sales mix.

Example:

A manufacturer sells two products.

| Product | Forecast sales | Selling price |
|---|---:|---:|
| Product A | 10,000 units | ₱100 |
| Product B | 5,000 units | ₱200 |

Expected revenue:

Product A:

\[
10,000\times ₱100=₱1,000,000
\]

Product B:

\[
5,000\times ₱200=₱1,000,000
\]

Total expected revenue:

\[
₱1,000,000+₱1,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱2,000,000}
\]

Notice something important.

Product A and Product B have different sales volumes and selling prices, but they contribute equal revenue.

This is why financial planning needs the product mix, not just the total forecast quantity.

## 6.2 Translating Supply Plans into Production and Operating Costs

From Module 4, we learned how to develop production and supply plans.

Now we need to determine their financial implications.

Example:

The production plan requires:

| Requirement | Quantity | Unit cost | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Raw materials | 15,000 kg | ₱20/kg | ₱300,000 |
| Direct labor | 2,000 hours | ₱150/hour | ₱300,000 |
| Factory overhead | Fixed assumption | - | ₱100,000 |
| **Total manufacturing cost** | | | **₱700,000** |

This allows Finance to evaluate the expected manufacturing expenditure.

But remember:

Manufacturing cost incurred is not necessarily equal to COGS for the same period.

Some products may remain in work-in-process or finished goods inventory.

That distinction is particularly important when production exceeds sales.

## 6.3 Understanding Inventory Investment and Working Capital

From Module 5, we learned how to determine inventory requirements.

Now we evaluate the financial implications.

Imagine the company increases safety stock from 2,000 units to 5,000 units.

Assume the average inventory cost is ₱80 per unit.

Previous inventory investment:

\[
2,000\times ₱80=₱160,000
\]

New inventory investment:

\[
5,000\times ₱80=₱400,000
\]

Additional inventory investment:

\[
₱400,000-₱160,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱240,000}
\]

The company now needs an additional ₱240,000 invested in inventory.

### Is this necessarily bad?

Not always.

Higher inventory can:
- Reduce stockout risk.
- Improve customer service.
- Protect against supplier delays.
- Support production continuity.

But it can also:
- Increase storage costs.
- Increase obsolescence risk.
- Tie up working capital.
- Increase financing requirements.

The correct decision depends on the balance between customer service benefits and financial consequences.

## 6.4 Connecting Operational Plans with Financial Targets

Suppose the company's management establishes the following monthly targets:

| Financial target | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱5,000,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱1,500,000 |
| Operating Profit | ₱800,000 |
| Maximum inventory investment | ₱2,000,000 |

The operational teams need to determine whether their plans can support these targets.

For example:

- Can the demand plan generate ₱5 million in revenue?
- Can the supply plan meet the required sales volume?
- Are material and labor costs consistent with the gross profit target?
- Will inventory remain within the approved investment limit?
- Can the company support the plan without excessive overtime or expedited procurement?

This is where operational planning becomes financially integrated.

## 6.5 Identifying Differences Between Operational and Financial Plans

Sometimes, Operations and Finance have different expectations.

Consider this scenario:

| Item | Operations plan | Financial target |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly production | 60,000 units | 50,000 units |
| Finished goods inventory | 15,000 units | 8,000 units |
| Overtime | Required | Minimize |
| Material purchases | Higher | Within approved budget |

What does this tell us?

The operational plan may be technically achievable, but it does not align with the financial targets.

The S&OP team must investigate:

1. Is production higher because of an expected increase in demand?
2. Is the company building inventory to protect against supplier risk?
3. Is overtime unavoidable?
4. Can production schedules be adjusted?
5. Will the inventory increase generate sufficient business benefits?

The objective is not to force Operations to follow Finance blindly.

The objective is to identify the reasons for the gap and develop an integrated plan that balances operational requirements with financial performance.

# 7. Formulas and Calculations

Let's now move from concepts to calculations.

These are foundational formulas that an S&OP analyst should understand.

## 7.1 Revenue

**Formula:**

\[
Revenue=Selling\ Price\times Sales\ Quantity
\]

Where:

| Variable | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Selling Price | Price per product sold | ₱/unit |
| Sales Quantity | Number of units sold | Units |
| Revenue | Total sales value | ₱ |

Example:

- Selling price = ₱250/unit.
- Sales quantity = 4,000 units.

Step 1:

\[
Revenue=₱250\times4,000
\]

Step 2:

\[
\boxed{Revenue=₱1,000,000}
\]

**Interpretation:** The company expects to generate ₱1 million in revenue from selling 4,000 units at ₱250 each.

Note that this calculation assumes one selling price and does not account for discounts, returns, or other adjustments.

## 7.2 Total Cost

**Formula:**

\[
Total\ Cost=Fixed\ Cost+Variable\ Cost
\]

If variable cost is expressed per unit:

\[
Total\ Cost=Fixed\ Cost+(Variable\ Cost/Unit\times Production\ Quantity)
\]

Example:

- Fixed cost = ₱100,000.
- Variable cost = ₱50/unit.
- Production quantity = 3,000 units.

Step 1: Calculate total variable cost.

\[
₱50\times3,000=₱150,000
\]

Step 2: Calculate total cost.

\[
₱100,000+₱150,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Total\ Cost=₱250,000}
\]

Interpretation: The company incurs ₱250,000 in total cost under the stated assumptions.

For this simplified example, all costs are assumed to relate to the same production period and volume.

## 7.3 Gross Profit

**Formula:**

\[
Gross\ Profit=Revenue-COGS
\]

Where COGS is the cost of products sold during the period.

Example:

- Revenue = ₱1,000,000.
- COGS = ₱650,000.

\[
Gross\ Profit=₱1,000,000-₱650,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Gross\ Profit=₱350,000}
\]

Interpretation: The company retains ₱350,000 after accounting for the cost of the products sold, before operating expenses and other applicable items.

### Gross Margin

Gross Margin measures gross profit as a percentage of revenue.

**Formula:**

\[
Gross\ Margin=\frac{Gross\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Using our example:

\[
=\frac{₱350,000}{₱1,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Gross\ Margin=35\%}
\]

Interpretation:

For every ₱100 in revenue, the company generates ₱35 in gross profit.

This is useful when comparing product profitability, business units, or financial performance across periods.

## 7.4 Contribution Margin

**Formula:**

\[
Contribution\ Margin=Revenue-Total\ Variable\ Costs
\]

For a single product:

\[
CM/Unit=Selling\ Price-Variable\ Cost/Unit
\]

Example:

- Selling price = ₱200/unit.
- Variable cost = ₱120/unit.
- Sales volume = 5,000 units.

Step 1: Contribution per unit.

\[
₱200-₱120=₱80
\]

Step 2: Total contribution margin.

\[
₱80\times5,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Total\ CM=₱400,000}
\]

### Contribution Margin Ratio

\[
CM\ Ratio=\frac{Contribution\ Margin}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Revenue:

\[
₱200\times5,000=₱1,000,000
\]

Therefore:

\[
CM\ Ratio=\frac{₱400,000}{₱1,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CM\ Ratio=40\%}
\]

Interpretation:

Forty percent of sales revenue remains after variable costs to cover fixed costs and contribute to profit.

**Gross Margin vs. Contribution Margin**

Do not confuse these two.

| Gross Margin | Contribution Margin |
|---|---|
| Revenue less COGS | Revenue less variable costs |
| Used to assess gross profitability | Used for cost-volume-profit analysis |
| COGS includes applicable manufacturing costs | Variable costs may include variable manufacturing and variable selling costs |

Their values can differ because their cost classifications and purposes differ.

## 7.5 Operating Profit

**Formula:**

\[
Operating\ Profit=Gross\ Profit-Operating\ Expenses
\]

Example:

- Revenue = ₱1,000,000.
- COGS = ₱650,000.
- Operating expenses = ₱200,000.

Step 1: Gross profit.

\[
₱1,000,000-₱650,000=₱350,000
\]

Step 2: Operating profit.

\[
₱350,000-₱200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Profit=₱150,000}
\]

Operating profit measures the profit generated from normal business operations before financing costs and income taxes, subject to the company's accounting presentation.

### Operating Margin

\[
Operating\ Margin=\frac{Operating\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

\[
=\frac{₱150,000}{₱1,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Margin=15\%}
\]

Interpretation: For every ₱100 in revenue, the company generates ₱15 in operating profit.

## 7.6 Working Capital

**Formula:**

\[
Working\ Capital=Current\ Assets-Current\ Liabilities
\]

Example:

| Current assets | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | ₱300,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | ₱400,000 |
| Inventory | ₱500,000 |
| **Total Current Assets** | **₱1,200,000** |

| Current liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts Payable | ₱350,000 |
| Short-term Loans | ₱250,000 |
| Accrued Expenses | ₱100,000 |
| **Total Current Liabilities** | **₱700,000** |

Step 1:

\[
WC=₱1,200,000-₱700,000
\]

Step 2:

\[
\boxed{Working\ Capital=₱500,000}
\]

Interpretation:

The company has ₱500,000 of positive net working capital.

However, positive working capital does not automatically guarantee sufficient cash. A substantial portion may be tied up in inventory or receivables.

### Quick comparison of the formulas

| Financial measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Selling Price × Sales Quantity |
| Total Cost | Fixed Cost + Total Variable Cost |
| Gross Profit | Revenue − COGS |
| Gross Margin | Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| Contribution Margin | Revenue − Variable Costs |
| Operating Profit | Gross Profit − Operating Expenses |
| Working Capital | Current Assets − Current Liabilities |

### Accounting profit vs. cash flow

Let's reinforce this with a comparison.

| Transaction | Profit impact | Cash impact |
|---|---|---|
| Credit sale | Revenue and potentially profit increase | No immediate collection |
| Purchase of raw materials for inventory | Usually no immediate expense for unused materials | Cash decreases if paid |
| Payment of accounts payable | No new expense merely from paying an existing payable | Cash decreases |
| Depreciation | Reduces accounting profit | No immediate cash payment |
| Loan proceeds | Not revenue or profit | Cash increases |

This is a very important distinction for manufacturing S&OP.

A plan may show positive operating profit while requiring substantial cash investment to support inventory and receivables.

# 8. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's combine everything into one realistic manufacturing scenario.

This is where the concepts become useful for actual S&OP work.

## 8.1 Business Scenario: Manufacturing Company

Imagine that you are an S&OP Analyst supporting a company that manufactures industrial electronic components.

The company is preparing its operational and financial plan for December.

The Demand Planning team provides the following forecast:

**December Demand Forecast**

| Measure | Plan |
|---|---:|
| Expected customer sales volume | 10,000 units |
| Selling price | ₱200/unit |
| Expected revenue | ₱2,000,000 |
| Beginning finished goods | 1,000 units |
| Planned production | 11,000 units |
| Expected ending finished goods | 2,000 units |

First, let's understand why production is 11,000 units even though forecasted sales are only 10,000 units.

The company wants to increase its finished goods inventory from 1,000 to 2,000 units.

Inventory equation:

\[
Ending\ Inventory=Beginning\ Inventory+Production-Sales
\]

\[
=1,000+11,000-10,000
\]

\[
\boxed{2,000\ units}
\]

Operationally, the plan is consistent.

But what happens financially?

## 8.2 Production Cost Breakdown

The Supply Planning team provides the following production cost assumptions.

| Cost component | Cost per unit |
|---|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱70 |
| Direct labor | ₱20 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱10 |
| **Total manufacturing cost** | **₱100** |

The planned production volume is 11,000 units.

Let's calculate the manufacturing costs.

**A. Direct Material Cost**

\[
11,000\times ₱70
\]

\[
=₱770,000
\]

**B. Direct Labor Cost**

\[
11,000\times ₱20
\]

\[
=₱220,000
\]

**C. Manufacturing Overhead**

\[
11,000\times ₱10
\]

\[
=₱110,000
\]

**D. Total Production Cost**

\[
₱770,000+₱220,000+₱110,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Total\ Production\ Cost=₱1,100,000}
\]

**Planned manufacturing expenditure**

| Cost component | Amount | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱770,000 | 70% |
| Direct labor | ₱220,000 | 20% |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱110,000 | 10% |
| **Total** | **₱1,100,000** | **100%** |

## 8.3 Calculating COGS and Ending Inventory

Important: The company produced 11,000 units, but it only expects to sell 10,000 units.

Therefore, the full ₱1.1 million production cost should not automatically be recognized as COGS.

Let's calculate.

Beginning finished goods inventory:

\[
1,000\times ₱100=₱100,000
\]

Cost of goods manufactured:

\[
11,000\times ₱100=₱1,100,000
\]

Ending finished goods inventory:

\[
2,000\times ₱100=₱200,000
\]

Now:

\[
COGS=Beginning\ FG+COGM-Ending\ FG
\]

\[
=₱100,000+₱1,100,000-₱200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{COGS=₱1,000,000}
\]

This means:
- ₱1 million is recognized as the cost of products sold.
- ₱200,000 remains in finished goods inventory.

The difference is important because inventory is an asset until the related product cost is recognized as an expense through COGS.

For this example, assume there are no inventory write-downs, abnormal production losses, or other cost adjustments.

## 8.4 Calculating Revenue and Profitability

Expected sales:

10,000 units.

Selling price:

₱200/unit.

Revenue:

\[
10,000\times ₱200=₱2,000,000
\]

Gross profit:

\[
₱2,000,000-₱1,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Gross\ Profit=₱1,000,000}
\]

Gross margin:

\[
\frac{₱1,000,000}{₱2,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Gross\ Margin=50\%}
\]

Now assume that the company has the following operating expenses:

| Operating expense | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sales and marketing | ₱100,000 |
| Administration | ₱80,000 |
| Office and IT expenses | ₱50,000 |
| Other operating expenses | ₱70,000 |
| **Total OPEX** | **₱300,000** |

Operating profit:

\[
₱1,000,000-₱300,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Profit=₱700,000}
\]

Operating margin:

\[
\frac{₱700,000}{₱2,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Margin=35\%}
\]

## 8.5 Consolidated Financial Plan

Let's consolidate the results.

| Financial indicator | Planned result |
|---|---:|
| Sales volume | 10,000 units |
| Production volume | 11,000 units |
| Revenue | ₱2,000,000 |
| COGS | ₱1,000,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱1,000,000 |
| Gross Margin | 50% |
| Operating Expenses | ₱300,000 |
| **Operating Profit** | **₱700,000** |
| **Operating Margin** | **35%** |

**December financial outlook**

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Expected operating profit | ₱700,000 |
| Revenue | ₱2,000,000 |
| Gross profit | ₱1,000,000 |
| Operating expenses | ₱300,000 |
| Operating margin | 35% |

Illustrative forecast, not actual company financial results. Excludes financing costs and income taxes.

## 8.6 Inventory Investment and Working Capital

Let's examine the inventory implications.

The company ends December with:

- Raw materials inventory: ₱400,000
- Work-in-process inventory: ₱100,000
- Finished goods inventory: ₱200,000

Total inventory investment:

\[
400,000+100,000+200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱700,000}
\]

This is the amount of financial resources currently tied up in the three inventory categories.

Now assume:

| Current assets | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | ₱300,000 |
| Accounts receivable | ₱500,000 |
| Inventory | ₱700,000 |
| **Total Current Assets** | **₱1,500,000** |

| Current liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts payable | ₱600,000 |
| Accrued expenses | ₱100,000 |
| **Total Current Liabilities** | **₱700,000** |

Working capital:

\[
₱1,500,000-₱700,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Working\ Capital=₱800,000}
\]

The company has ₱800,000 in positive working capital.

However, notice that ₱700,000 is tied up in inventory.

This is why an S&OP Analyst must look beyond production volume and profitability.

## 8.7 Evaluating an Alternative Production Plan

Now let's introduce a decision-making situation.

Suppose Operations proposes increasing production from 11,000 to 13,000 units.

Why?

The production team wants to build additional inventory to protect against a possible increase in customer demand.

Assume:
- Forecasted sales remain at 10,000 units.
- Beginning inventory remains at 1,000 units.
- Unit manufacturing cost remains at ₱100.
- Selling price remains at ₱200.

Let's compare the alternatives.

| Indicator | Plan A | Plan B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Production | 11,000 | 13,000 |
| Sales | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Ending inventory | 2,000 | 4,000 |
| Revenue | ₱2,000,000 | ₱2,000,000 |
| Production cost | ₱1,100,000 | ₱1,300,000 |
| Ending FG value | ₱200,000 | ₱400,000 |

What happens?

1. Revenue remains unchanged because sales remain at 10,000 units.
2. Production expenditure increases by ₱200,000.
3. Finished goods inventory increases by ₱200,000.
4. More financial resources become tied up in inventory.
5. There is no immediate increase in revenue or operating profit from producing the additional units.

**Impact of additional production**

| Measure | Impact |
|---|---:|
| Additional manufacturing cost associated with 2,000 more units | +₱200,000 |
| Additional ending inventory | ₱200,000 |
| Additional sales revenue | ₱0 |
| Immediate operating profit increase | ₱0 |

This assumes the extra production does not create additional fixed costs, and all additional units are successfully produced at the same unit cost.

### Is Plan B necessarily wrong?

No.

Suppose next month the company expects a major customer order and has a long supplier lead time.

Producing additional inventory now may prevent:
- Future stockouts.
- Lost sales.
- Expedited procurement.
- Customer delivery delays.

Therefore, the decision should consider the expected benefit against the additional inventory investment and associated risks.

**This is where financial analysis and operational judgment must work together.**

# 9. Financial Perspective in S&OP

Let's now look at how an S&OP Analyst actually uses these concepts in business meetings.

## 9.1 Role of an S&OP Analyst

An S&OP Analyst does not necessarily prepare the company's official financial statements.

Instead, the analyst helps translate operational information into financial insights that support business planning.

Typical responsibilities include:

| Area | Analyst's responsibility |
|---|---|
| Demand | Translate forecast volume into expected revenue |
| Supply | Estimate manufacturing costs and resource requirements |
| Inventory | Evaluate inventory investment and working capital |
| Procurement | Analyze material spending and supplier-related financial impacts |
| Capacity | Evaluate overtime, additional shifts, and capacity investments |
| Profitability | Identify changes in gross profit and operating profit |
| Reconciliation | Identify gaps between operational plans and financial targets |
| Reporting | Present financial implications and decision-support analysis |

### Example of an S&OP Analyst's observation

Suppose you discover:

- Sales forecast increased by 15%.
- Production requirements increased by 20%.
- Material spending increased by 25%.
- Finished goods inventory increased by 30%.

An ordinary operational report might simply say:

"Production increased to support higher demand."

But a financially oriented S&OP analysis asks:

- Why did production increase faster than demand?
- Why did material spending increase faster than production?
- Is the increase caused by material price changes, scrap, or inventory buildup?
- Is the additional inventory justified?
- Will the company still achieve its gross margin target?

This is the difference between reporting operational numbers and providing business analysis.

## 9.2 How Finance and Operations Collaborate

Finance and Operations have different responsibilities, but they need the same assumptions and planning information.

| Department | Primary contribution |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer demand, pricing, and sales opportunities |
| Demand Planning | Consolidated demand forecast |
| Operations | Production capacity, labor, and manufacturing feasibility |
| Supply Chain | Materials, procurement, logistics, and supply availability |
| Inventory Management | Inventory targets and stock requirements |
| Finance | Revenue, costs, profitability, budgets, and cash requirements |
| S&OP Leadership | Cross-functional alignment and business decisions |

### Practical collaboration

Imagine that Operations proposes adding a weekend shift.

Operations explains:

"We need an additional shift to meet the forecasted demand."

Finance evaluates:

- Additional labor expenses.
- Additional utilities and overhead.
- Expected incremental revenue.
- Expected incremental contribution.
- Impact on operating profit.

Together, they determine whether the additional shift makes business sense.

The best decision is not always the lowest-cost option.

Sometimes, spending more is justified because it protects revenue, improves customer service, or prevents a larger future expense.

## 9.3 Financial Integration in Pre-S&OP

During Pre-S&OP, the company reviews the proposed demand and supply plans and identifies gaps.

Financial evaluation helps answer:

- Does the demand plan support the revenue target?
- Does the supply plan support the required sales volume?
- Are manufacturing costs within expectations?
- Is the inventory plan financially acceptable?
- Are there significant differences between the latest forecast and budget?
- What alternatives can improve the overall financial outcome?

Finance can help quantify the financial consequences of each alternative.

For example:

| Alternative | Operational consequence | Financial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Add overtime | Increase production capacity | Higher labor cost |
| Outsource production | Increase available supply | Higher external manufacturing cost |
| Increase inventory | Improve product availability | Higher working capital |
| Prioritize high-margin products | Change production mix | Potentially improve profitability |
| Reduce production | Lower inventory buildup | Potentially reduce revenue if demand is missed |

The goal is to prepare realistic alternatives before management makes decisions.

## 9.4 Financial Integration in Executive S&OP

Executive S&OP focuses on management decisions and business alignment.

At this level, the discussion becomes more strategic.

Management may ask:

1. Are we expected to achieve our revenue targets?
2. Are we on track to meet profitability objectives?
3. Is our inventory investment increasing too much?
4. Do we need additional production capacity?
5. Are we spending too much on overtime or expedited procurement?
6. Which products or customers should receive priority?
7. What financial risks require management intervention?

The S&OP Analyst supports these discussions by presenting accurate information, identifying gaps, and quantifying the impact of alternatives.

## 9.5 Why Companies Need One Integrated Operational and Financial Plan

Imagine that Operations is planning to produce 100,000 units while Finance is budgeting for only 80,000 units.

Sales is expecting demand for 90,000 units.

Inventory is already above target.

Procurement is preparing to purchase materials for 110,000 units.

Each department might have its own justification.

But without integration, the company may experience:
- Excess procurement.
- Unnecessary production.
- Increased inventory.
- Higher storage costs.
- Poor cash utilization.
- Misaligned revenue and profitability expectations.

An integrated S&OP plan ensures that the departments work from a common set of assumptions.

**The ultimate objective is not simply to produce more, sell more, or spend less. It is to make coordinated decisions that support the company's overall business objectives.**

# 10. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate the most important concepts from Topic 7.1.

## 10.1 Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Financial Planning | Translates business activities into financial expectations and resource requirements |
| Operational Planning | Determines what the company needs and can execute |
| Financial Planning | Determines the financial consequences of executing those plans |
| Revenue | Value generated from sales |
| COGS | Cost of products sold |
| Gross Profit | Revenue less COGS |
| Operating Expenses | Costs of running the business outside COGS |
| Operating Profit | Gross profit less operating expenses |
| Contribution Margin | Revenue less variable costs |
| Working Capital | Current assets less current liabilities |
| Cash Flow | Actual movement of cash into and out of the business |

## 10.2 Five Important Lessons for an S&OP Analyst

**1. Production volume is not the same as sales volume.**

A company can produce 100,000 units but sell only 80,000 units. The remaining units become inventory, subject to applicable inventory valuation rules.

**2. Higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit.**

Revenue may increase while material costs, labor expenses, and other costs increase faster.

**3. Higher profit does not automatically mean higher cash.**

Credit sales and inventory investments can consume cash even when accounting profit is positive.

**4. Inventory is both an operational resource and a financial investment.**

Inventory protects customer service but consumes working capital.

**5. Operational and financial plans must be aligned.**

A successful S&OP plan must be operationally achievable and financially sustainable.

## 10.3 The Complete Financial Integration Concept

**How operational decisions translate into financial outcomes**

| Operational area | Planning inputs | Financial result |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Forecast quantity, selling price, product mix | Revenue |
| Supply and Production | Materials, labor, capacity, production volume | Manufacturing costs |
| Inventory | Raw materials, WIP, finished goods | Inventory investment |
| Business Operations | Administration, selling, distribution, support | Operating expenses |

**Integrated financial outcome:** Revenue • Profitability • Working Capital • Cash Flow
