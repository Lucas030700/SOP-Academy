# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What is Revenue and Sales Planning?

Imagine na may manufacturing company na gumagawa ng tatlong products:

| Product | Selling Price |
|---|---:|
| Product A | ₱100 |
| Product B | ₱200 |
| Product C | ₱500 |

May forecast ang Sales Department na makakabenta sila ng:

- 1,000 units of Product A
- 500 units of Product B
- 200 units of Product C

Hindi sapat na malaman lang ang expected sales quantity.

Kailangan din nating malaman kung magkano ang magiging revenue kapag naibenta ang mga products na ito.

For example:

| Product | Forecast Quantity | Selling Price | Expected Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | ₱100 | ₱100,000 |
| B | 500 | ₱200 | ₱100,000 |
| C | 200 | ₱500 | ₱100,000 |
| **Total** | **1,700** | | **₱300,000** |

Although magkakaiba ang selling prices at quantities, pare-pareho ang revenue contribution ng bawat product.

Ito ang basic idea ng Revenue Planning.

**Revenue Planning** is the process of estimating how much money a company expects to generate from selling its products or services over a specific period.

Pero hindi lang ito simpleng multiplication. Kailangan din nating maintindihan kung saan nanggagaling ang sales, ano ang expected changes sa selling prices, at paano naaapektuhan ng product mix ang total revenue.

## 1.2 Why is Revenue Planning Important in S&OP?

Sa S&OP, hindi lang dapat tanungin:

- How many units will we sell?
- How many units should we produce?
- Do we have enough inventory?

Kailangan din nating itanong:

- How much revenue are we expecting?
- Are we meeting our revenue targets?
- Which products generate the most revenue?
- Are we selling more low-value or high-value products?
- Will our production constraints affect expected revenue?

Halimbawa:

May capacity ang factory na gumawa ng 10,000 units per month.

Option A:
- Produce 10,000 units of Product A at ₱100 each.

Option B:
- Produce 10,000 units of Product B at ₱300 each.

Same production quantity, pero magkaiba ang potential revenue.

Kaya mahalaga ang connection ng commercial decisions at operational decisions.

**Revenue planning helps the company determine whether its demand and supply plans can support its financial objectives.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Understand Sales, Demand, and Revenue

Let's use a simple example.

Imagine may bakery ka.

Every month, may customers na bumibili ng bread.

May tatlong bagay kang kailangang maintindihan:

1. Ilang customers ang posibleng bumili?
2. Ilang bread ang inaasahang mabebenta?
3. Magkano ang perang matatanggap mo mula sa sales?

Magkakaugnay ang mga ito pero hindi sila pare-pareho.

### A. Demand Planning

Ito ang estimation kung gaano karaming products ang kakailanganin ng market.

Example:

Expected market demand = 5,000 pieces of bread.

Ang focus ay quantity.

### B. Sales Planning

Ito naman ang planning kung paano makakamit ng Sales Department ang expected sales.

Example:

- Customer A: 2,000 pieces
- Customer B: 1,500 pieces
- Customer C: 1,000 pieces
- Other customers: 500 pieces

Total expected sales = 5,000 pieces.

Ang focus ay sales targets, customers, orders, selling activities, at commercial commitments.

### C. Revenue Planning

Dito naman natin kino-convert ang expected sales into monetary value.

Assume:

Selling price = ₱50 per piece.

Expected revenue:

5,000 × ₱50 = ₱250,000.

Ang focus ay financial value.

## 2.2 The Relationship Between the Three

**DEMAND PLANNING**

5,000 units

*How much does the market need?*

↓

**SALES PLANNING**

5,000 units

*How will we achieve the sales target?*

↓

**REVENUE PLANNING**

₱250,000

*How much revenue will we generate?*

Important clarification: These are connected planning activities, not necessarily a strictly one-way process. Sales information can also influence demand forecasts, and financial targets can influence commercial planning.

# 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the professional terminology.

## 3.1 What is Revenue?

**Revenue** is the monetary value generated by a company from its normal business activities, primarily from selling goods or providing services.

For a manufacturing company, revenue generally comes from selling finished goods to customers.

Example:

A manufacturing company sells 2,000 units of an electronic component at ₱150 per unit.

Revenue = ₱300,000.

Important distinction:

Revenue is NOT the same as profit.

| Financial Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Money generated from sales. |
| Cost | Resources consumed to produce and sell products. |
| Gross Profit | Revenue minus Cost of Goods Sold (COGS). |
| Operating Expenses | Expenses incurred to operate the business. |
| Net Profit | Remaining profit after applicable expenses, interest, and taxes. |

Example:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱1,000,000 |
| Cost of Goods Sold | ₱600,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱400,000 |
| Operating Expenses | ₱250,000 |
| Operating Profit | ₱150,000 |

Kahit ₱1 million ang revenue, hindi ibig sabihin na ₱1 million din ang kinita ng company as profit.

Ito ang dahilan kung bakit separate nating pag-aaralan ang Cost Fundamentals sa Topic 7.3.

## 3.2 Sales Planning vs. Demand Planning vs. Revenue Planning

Ito ang isang distinction na kailangan mong ma-master bilang S&OP analyst.

| Dimension | Demand Planning | Sales Planning | Revenue Planning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Market demand | Sales achievement | Financial value |
| Main question | How much will customers need? | How much can we sell? | How much money will we generate? |
| Typical data | Historical demand, forecast, trends | Orders, accounts, targets | Quantity, price, mix |
| Main output | Demand forecast | Sales plan | Revenue forecast |
| Unit | Units | Units / orders / sales value | Currency |

### Important distinction: Demand is not always equal to Sales.

Example:

Expected customer demand = 10,000 units.

But the Sales Department has confirmed orders for only 7,000 units.

The remaining 3,000 units may represent expected future orders, uncommitted demand, or a forecasted opportunity.

Hindi natin dapat basta ituring ang buong 10,000 units as confirmed sales.

Likewise, even if the Sales Department forecasts 10,000 units, the Operations team may only have capacity to produce 8,000 units.

This creates a demand-supply gap that needs to be resolved during S&OP.

## 3.3 Understanding the Revenue Equation

There are four important elements:

1. Sales quantity
2. Selling price
3. Product mix
4. Customer mix

The first two determine basic revenue. The other two explain how the composition of sales changes the overall result.

### A. Sales Quantity

The number of units sold during a particular period.

Example: 5,000 units per month.

### B. Selling Price

The amount charged to customers for each unit.

Example: ₱200 per unit.

### C. Product Mix

The proportion of total sales represented by different products.

Example:

| Product | Sales Quantity | Share of Total Units |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 700 | 70% |
| B | 200 | 20% |
| C | 100 | 10% |
| **Total** | **1,000** | **100%** |

### D. Customer Mix

The distribution of sales among different customers or customer groups.

Example:

| Customer | Sales Quantity | Selling Price |
|---|---:|---:|
| Customer A | 500 | ₱100 |
| Customer B | 300 | ₱120 |
| Customer C | 200 | ₱150 |

Even if the same product is sold, different customers may have different negotiated prices, contract terms, or discounts.

Therefore, customer mix can influence total revenue.

# 4. Detailed Discussion

## 4.1 Sales Planning Components

A reliable sales plan considers multiple information sources.

| Component | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Historical sales | Previous sales performance | Last year's monthly sales |
| Sales forecast | Expected future sales | 12,000 units next month |
| Customer orders | Confirmed customer requirements | 8,000 units ordered |
| Selling prices | Expected transaction prices | ₱250/unit |
| Product mix | Distribution of sales across products | 60% Product A, 40% Product B |
| Customer mix | Distribution of sales across accounts | Customer A contributes 50% |
| Promotions | Activities that increase demand | 10% promotional discount |
| Seasonality | Recurring demand patterns | Higher demand during peak season |
| New products | Products being introduced | Newly launched component |
| Discontinued products | Products being phased out | End-of-life product |

Let's examine some important components.

### Historical Sales Data

Historical sales data tells us what happened in previous periods.

Example:

| Month | Actual Sales |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 8,500 |
| March | 9,000 |
| April | 9,500 |

We can observe an increasing trend.

However, historical sales should not automatically become the future forecast.

Why?

Because sales may have been affected by:

- Stockouts
- Production interruptions
- Customer cancellations
- Promotions
- Seasonal demand
- Price changes

For example, if January sales were only 8,000 units because the factory could not produce enough, actual sales may underestimate true customer demand.

This connects directly to Module 3: Demand Planning.

### Sales Forecasts and Customer Orders

These are two different types of information.

**Customer Orders:** Actual orders already received from customers.

**Sales Forecast:** Expected future sales based on commercial expectations and available information.

Example:

| Information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Confirmed customer orders | 8,000 |
| Additional expected orders | 2,000 |
| Total sales forecast | 10,000 |

The 2,000 additional units are not yet confirmed orders.

A company should maintain visibility over the difference between committed and forecasted business.

### Selling Prices and Price Changes

Selling prices are not necessarily constant.

Possible reasons for price changes:

- Annual contract negotiations
- Raw material market conditions
- Customer discounts
- Inflation
- Competitive pressure
- Product redesign or specification changes

Example:

Previous selling price = ₱200.

New selling price = ₱220.

Price increase:

\[
\frac{220-200}{200}\times100=10\%
\]

Even if sales volume remains unchanged, revenue increases by 10%.

### Promotions and Discounts

Promotions may increase sales volume while reducing average selling price.

Example:

Normal price = ₱100.

Promotional price = ₱90.

Sales increase from 1,000 units to 1,300 units.

| Scenario | Quantity | Price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Normal | 1,000 | ₱100 | ₱100,000 |
| Promotional | 1,300 | ₱90 | ₱117,000 |

Revenue increased by ₱17,000 despite the lower selling price.

But remember: higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit.

Additional production and selling costs will be discussed in Topic 7.3.

### New Products and Discontinued Products

New products create a forecasting challenge because they may not have sufficient historical sales data.

Companies may use:

- Similar product performance
- Customer commitments
- Market research
- Sales pipeline
- Product launch targets

For discontinued products, the company must consider:

- Remaining customer demand
- Last-time-buy orders
- Remaining inventory
- Replacement products
- Revenue transition to successor products

A product discontinuation can reduce revenue from one product while increasing revenue from its replacement.

## 4.2 Revenue Forecasting Methods

Revenue forecasting means estimating the monetary value of future sales using available commercial and operational information.

There are several approaches.

### Method 1: Sales Volume × Selling Price

Ito ang most straightforward method.

Example:

- Forecast quantity: 5,000 units
- Expected selling price: ₱250/unit

\[
Revenue=5,000\times250
\]

\[
\boxed{Revenue=₱1,250,000}
\]

Best used when:
- Expected quantities are available.
- Selling prices are relatively stable.
- Product-level forecasts are available.

### Method 2: Historical Revenue Trends

Sometimes, companies use previous revenue performance as a starting point.

Example:

| Year | Revenue |
|---|---:|
| 2023 | ₱10,000,000 |
| 2024 | ₱11,000,000 |
| 2025 | ₱12,100,000 |

Observed annual growth is approximately 10%.

If the company assumes the same growth for 2026:

\[
12,100,000\times1.10
\]

\[
=₱13,310,000
\]

However, this method has limitations.

Historical revenue may have increased because of price increases rather than higher sales volume.

It may also reflect unusual events, such as one-time customer orders.

Therefore, historical revenue trends should be combined with volume and price information whenever possible.

### Method 3: Product-Level Revenue Forecasting

Instead of forecasting total company revenue immediately, calculate revenue for every product.

Example:

| Product | Forecast Quantity | Price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10,000 | ₱100 | ₱1,000,000 |
| B | 5,000 | ₱200 | ₱1,000,000 |
| C | 2,000 | ₱500 | ₱1,000,000 |
| **Total** | **17,000** | | **₱3,000,000** |

Advantages:

- Identifies which products generate revenue.
- Helps prioritize production.
- Makes product-level variance analysis possible.
- Supports product portfolio decisions.

### Method 4: Customer-Level Revenue Forecasting

Companies may also calculate expected revenue by customer.

Example:

| Customer | Forecast Quantity | Selling Price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Customer A | 5,000 | ₱100 | ₱500,000 |
| Customer B | 3,000 | ₱120 | ₱360,000 |
| Customer C | 2,000 | ₱150 | ₱300,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | | **₱1,160,000** |

This is useful because customers may have different negotiated prices.

For example, Customer A may receive a lower price because of a long-term contract or large purchase volume.

A decline in sales from Customer C may have a larger revenue impact per unit than a similar decline from Customer A.

### Method 5: Price-Volume-Mix Analysis

This is particularly important for professional S&OP analysts.

Price-Volume-Mix analysis, commonly called PVM, helps explain why revenue changed between two periods.

It separates the impact of:

- Price changes
- Total sales volume changes
- Product mix changes

Example:

Last month:
- 1,000 units sold
- Average selling price = ₱100
- Revenue = ₱100,000

This month:
- 1,200 units sold
- Average selling price = ₱110
- Revenue = ₱132,000

Revenue increased by ₱32,000.

But what caused the increase?

- Higher sales volume?
- Higher selling price?
- A combination of both?

PVM analysis helps answer this question.

We will calculate it in detail later.

## 4.3 Budget vs. Forecast vs. Actual Revenue

These three terms are frequently used in corporate financial reporting.

They are related but serve different purposes.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---:|
| Budget | Financial target or approved plan | ₱10M |
| Forecast | Latest estimate of expected performance | ₱9M |
| Actual | Revenue already realized and recorded | ₱8.5M |

### Budget

The original financial target established for a particular period.

Example:

The company targets ₱10 million revenue for November.

### Forecast

The latest estimate based on current business conditions.

Example:

During the month, Sales identifies that one major customer will reduce orders.

The updated forecast becomes ₱9 million.

### Actual

The revenue recognized from completed sales transactions according to the company's accounting policies.

Example:

At month-end, recorded revenue is ₱8.5 million.

### Why Are All Three Important?

Because they answer different management questions.

| Comparison | Business Question |
|---|---|
| Forecast vs. Budget | Are we still expected to achieve our target? |
| Actual vs. Budget | Did we achieve our original target? |
| Actual vs. Forecast | How accurate was our latest forecast? |

An S&OP analyst should understand all three comparisons.

For example, if actual revenue is below budget but matches the latest forecast, it may indicate that the company successfully identified and communicated a business risk before month-end.

That is valuable planning visibility, even if the original target was missed.

# 5. Formulas and Calculations

Now let's move from understanding the concepts to actually calculating revenue.

For all examples, we will use Philippine pesos (₱) and units as the measurement.

## 5.1 Revenue = Sales Quantity × Selling Price

**Formula**

\[
R=Q\times P
\]

Where:

- \(R\) = Revenue (₱)
- \(Q\) = Sales quantity (units)
- \(P\) = Selling price (₱/unit)

Example:

A company sells 2,500 units at ₱180 each.

Step 1: Identify the quantity.

\[
Q=2,500\ units
\]

Step 2: Identify the selling price.

\[
P=₱180/unit
\]

Step 3: Multiply.

\[
R=2,500\times180
\]

**Answer**

\[
\boxed{R=₱450,000}
\]

Interpretation:

The company generates ₱450,000 in revenue from these sales.

Important assumption: We are using the actual transaction price per unit, excluding separately accounted taxes and other amounts that are not recognized as revenue.

## 5.2 Total Revenue Across Multiple Products

When a company sells multiple products, calculate revenue separately for each product, then add the results.

Formula:

\[
R_{Total}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Q_i\times P_i)
\]

Where:

- \(Q_i\) = Sales quantity of product \(i\)
- \(P_i\) = Selling price of product \(i\)
- \(n\) = Number of products

Example:

| Product | Quantity | Price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | ₱100 | ₱100,000 |
| B | 800 | ₱150 | ₱120,000 |
| C | 500 | ₱200 | ₱100,000 |
| **Total** | **2,300** | | **₱320,000** |

Calculation:

\[
R_A=1,000\times100=100,000
\]

\[
R_B=800\times150=120,000
\]

\[
R_C=500\times200=100,000
\]

\[
R_{Total}=100,000+120,000+100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{R_{Total}=₱320,000}
\]

Notice that total revenue cannot be calculated accurately by multiplying total quantity by just any one product's selling price.

Different products have different prices.

## 5.3 Average Selling Price (ASP)

Average Selling Price tells us the average revenue generated per unit sold.

Formula:

\[
ASP=\frac{Total\ Revenue}{Total\ Units\ Sold}
\]

Example:

| Product | Quantity | Price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | ₱100 | ₱100,000 |
| B | 500 | ₱200 | ₱100,000 |
| **Total** | **1,500** | | **₱200,000** |

\[
ASP=\frac{200,000}{1,500}
\]

\[
\boxed{ASP=₱133.33/unit}
\]

Important:

ASP is a weighted average selling price, not a simple average of product prices.

Incorrect calculation:

\[
\frac{100+200}{2}=₱150
\]

Correct calculation:

\[
\frac{200,000}{1,500}=₱133.33
\]

Why?

Because Product A has twice as many units sold as Product B.

Its price therefore has greater influence on the average.

## 5.4 Revenue Growth Percentage

This measures how much revenue increased or decreased compared with a previous period.

Formula:

\[
Growth\%=
\frac{Current\ Revenue-Previous\ Revenue}
{Previous\ Revenue}\times100
\]

Example:

Previous month revenue = ₱500,000.

Current month revenue = ₱600,000.

\[
Growth\%=
\frac{600,000-500,000}{500,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Growth=20\%}
\]

Interpretation:

Revenue increased by 20% compared with the previous month.

If the result is negative, it indicates a revenue decline.

## 5.5 Sales Volume Variance

Sales Volume Variance measures the revenue impact of selling a different quantity than planned, using a specified reference price.

For a single product:

\[
SVV=(AQ-BQ)\times BP
\]

Where:

- \(AQ\) = Actual quantity sold (units)
- \(BQ\) = Budget quantity (units)
- \(BP\) = Budget selling price (₱/unit)

Example:

| Item | Value |
|---|---:|
| Budget quantity | 1,000 units |
| Actual quantity | 1,200 units |
| Budget price | ₱100/unit |

\[
SVV=(1,200-1,000)\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{SVV=+₱20,000}
\]

Interpretation:

The company sold 200 additional units, generating a favorable revenue impact of ₱20,000 at the budget price.

If actual quantity were only 800 units:

\[
SVV=(800-1,000)\times100
\]

\[
=-₱20,000
\]

This would be an unfavorable volume variance.

## 5.6 Price Variance

Price Variance measures the revenue impact of selling at a different price than budgeted.

Formula:

\[
PV=(AP-BP)\times AQ
\]

Where:

- \(AP\) = Actual selling price (₱/unit)
- \(BP\) = Budget selling price (₱/unit)
- \(AQ\) = Actual quantity sold (units)

Example:

| Item | Value |
|---|---:|
| Budget price | ₱100 |
| Actual price | ₱110 |
| Actual quantity | 1,200 units |

\[
PV=(110-100)\times1,200
\]

\[
\boxed{PV=+₱12,000}
\]

Interpretation:

The company sold at a ₱10 higher price per unit, creating a favorable revenue impact of ₱12,000.

If actual selling price were ₱90:

\[
PV=(90-100)\times1,200
\]

\[
=-₱12,000
\]

This is an unfavorable price variance.

## 5.7 Product Mix Impact

This is slightly more advanced, so let's establish the concept first.

Imagine that a company manufactures two products.

| Product | Selling Price |
|---|---:|
| A | ₱100 |
| B | ₱300 |

The company originally planned to sell:

- 80 units of A
- 20 units of B

Total = 100 units.

Revenue:

\[
(80\times100)+(20\times300)
\]

\[
=₱14,000
\]

But actual sales became:

- 50 units of A
- 50 units of B

Total = 100 units.

Actual revenue:

\[
(50\times100)+(50\times300)
\]

\[
=₱20,000
\]

Notice something interesting.

**The company sold exactly the same total quantity, but revenue increased by ₱6,000.**

Why?

Because customers purchased more of the higher-priced product.

That is the product mix effect.

For a formal mix calculation, we can hold total actual volume constant and compare actual product proportions with budgeted proportions.

Formula:

\[
Mix\ Impact=
\sum_i(AQ_i-Expected\ AQ_i)\times BP_i
\]

Where:

\[
Expected\ AQ_i=
Total\ Actual\ Quantity\times Budget\ Mix_i
\]

Let's calculate:

Budget mix:

- Product A = 80%
- Product B = 20%

Actual total volume = 100 units.

Expected quantities at budget mix:

- A = 100 × 80% = 80
- B = 100 × 20% = 20

Actual quantities:

- A = 50
- B = 50

| Product | Expected Quantity | Actual Quantity | Difference | Budget Price | Mix Impact |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 80 | 50 | -30 | ₱100 | -₱3,000 |
| B | 20 | 50 | +30 | ₱300 | +₱9,000 |
| **Total** | **100** | **100** | **0** | | **+₱6,000** |

**Favorable mix**

\[
\boxed{Mix\ Impact=+₱6,000}
\]

The company generated additional revenue because the actual product mix shifted toward the higher-priced product.

However, remember:

Higher-priced products are not automatically more profitable. Their production costs may also be higher. We will investigate that in Topic 7.3.

## 5.8 Budget vs. Actual Revenue Variance

This measures the difference between actual revenue and budgeted revenue.

Formula:

\[
Revenue\ Variance=Actual\ Revenue-Budget\ Revenue
\]

Example:

Budget revenue = ₱1,000,000.

Actual revenue = ₱900,000.

\[
Variance=900,000-1,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Variance=-₱100,000}
\]

Percentage variance:

\[
Variance\%=
\frac{Actual-Budget}{Budget}\times100
\]

\[
=\frac{-100,000}{1,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Variance=-10\%}
\]

Interpretation:

The company generated ₱100,000 less revenue than budgeted, equivalent to a 10% unfavorable variance.

## 5.9 How to Avoid Double-Counting in Price, Volume, and Mix Analysis

This is an important professional concept.

Suppose revenue increased from ₱100,000 to ₱120,000.

We want to explain the ₱20,000 increase.

If we calculate volume variance, price variance, and mix variance using inconsistent formulas, we may accidentally count the same revenue impact more than once.

For a proper reconciliation, we need to establish a consistent calculation sequence.

### Recommended decomposition

For a multi-product portfolio:

**Step 1: Calculate budget revenue**

\[
R_B=\sum_i BQ_i\times BP_i
\]

**Step 2: Calculate volume effect**

\[
Volume=
\sum_i(AQ_i-BQ_i)\times BP_i
\]

This is the total quantity effect valued at budget prices. It includes both overall volume and changes in product composition.

**Step 3: Calculate price effect**

\[
Price=
\sum_i(AP_i-BP_i)\times AQ_i
\]

**Step 4: Calculate actual revenue**

\[
R_A=\sum_i AQ_i\times AP_i
\]

Then:

\[
Actual\ Revenue-Budget\ Revenue
=
Volume+Price
\]

If you want to separate total volume from product mix, further decompose the volume effect:

\[
Volume\ Effect=Total\ Volume\ Effect+Mix\ Effect
\]

Using the budget-mix approach described earlier, the total volume effect is:

\[
Total\ Volume\ Effect=
(AQ_{Total}-BQ_{Total})\times Budget\ Weighted\ ASP
\]

The mix effect is the remaining difference between the product-level volume effect and the total volume effect.

**Key principle:** Once you have decomposed the volume effect into total volume and mix, do not add the original combined volume effect again. That would double-count the volume component.

Also, different companies may use different variance conventions and sequencing methods. Always confirm the company's finance reporting methodology before comparing PVM results.

# 6. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's put everything together using a realistic manufacturing scenario.

Imagine you are an S&OP analyst for an electronic components manufacturer.

The company manufactures three products:

- Product A: Standard electronic component
- Product B: High-capacity electronic component
- Product C: Premium electronic component

The Sales and Finance teams have prepared the November revenue budget.

However, actual November sales have already been recorded.

Your task is to determine why actual revenue differs from budget.

## 6.1 Budget vs. Actual Sales Data

*Illustrative manufacturing scenario*

| Product | Budget Qty | Actual Qty | Budget Price | Actual Price |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10,000 | 12,000 | ₱100 | ₱105 |
| B | 6,000 | 5,000 | ₱200 | ₱195 |
| C | 2,000 | 3,000 | ₱400 | ₱420 |

Assumptions:

- Quantities represent units sold during November.
- Prices represent net selling prices per unit.
- All amounts are in Philippine pesos.
- There are no additional adjustments for taxes, returns, or other accounting items in this simplified example.

## 6.2 Calculate Budget Revenue

Product A:

\[
10,000\times100=₱1,000,000
\]

Product B:

\[
6,000\times200=₱1,200,000
\]

Product C:

\[
2,000\times400=₱800,000
\]

Total budget revenue:

\[
R_B=1,000,000+1,200,000+800,000
\]

\[
\boxed{R_B=₱3,000,000}
\]

## 6.3 Calculate Actual Revenue

Product A:

\[
12,000\times105=₱1,260,000
\]

Product B:

\[
5,000\times195=₱975,000
\]

Product C:

\[
3,000\times420=₱1,260,000
\]

Total actual revenue:

\[
R_A=1,260,000+975,000+1,260,000
\]

\[
\boxed{R_A=₱3,495,000}
\]

## 6.4 Calculate Revenue Variance

\[
Variance=3,495,000-3,000,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Variance=+₱495,000}
\]

Percentage variance:

\[
\frac{495,000}{3,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{+16.5\%}
\]

### November Revenue Performance

| Metric | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Budget revenue | ₱3.00M |
| Actual revenue | ₱3.495M |
| Revenue variance | +₱495K |
| Performance vs. budget | +16.5% |

## 6.5 Identify the Main Revenue Drivers

Hindi sapat na malaman natin na tumaas ang revenue.

Kailangan nating malaman kung bakit.

Let's separate volume, price, and mix.

### A. Product-Level Volume Effect

Using budget prices:

\[
Volume\ Effect=(AQ-BQ)\times BP
\]

| Product | Quantity Change | Budget Price | Volume Effect |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | +2,000 | ₱100 | +₱200,000 |
| B | -1,000 | ₱200 | -₱200,000 |
| C | +1,000 | ₱400 | +₱400,000 |
| **Total** | **+2,000** | | **+₱400,000** |

Interpretation:

- Product A contributed an additional ₱200,000.
- Product B reduced revenue by ₱200,000.
- Product C contributed an additional ₱400,000.

Overall, higher sales volume at budget prices contributed ₱400,000.

### B. Price Effect

\[
Price\ Effect=(AP-BP)\times AQ
\]

| Product | Price Change | Actual Qty | Price Effect |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | +₱5 | 12,000 | +₱60,000 |
| B | -₱5 | 5,000 | -₱25,000 |
| C | +₱20 | 3,000 | +₱60,000 |
| **Total** | | | **+₱95,000** |

Interpretation:

Price changes contributed an additional ₱95,000.

### C. Reconcile the Results

\[
Volume+Price=Total\ Revenue\ Variance
\]

\[
400,000+95,000=495,000
\]

The revenue variance is fully explained.

Notice that we have not double-counted mix. Our volume effect currently includes both total volume and product mix changes.

### D. Separate Total Volume and Product Mix

Now let's calculate the more detailed explanation.

Budget total quantity:

\[
10,000+6,000+2,000=18,000
\]

Actual total quantity:

\[
12,000+5,000+3,000=20,000
\]

Budget weighted average selling price:

\[
ASP_B=\frac{3,000,000}{18,000}
\]

\[
=₱166.67
\]

Total volume effect:

\[
(20,000-18,000)\times166.67
\]

\[
\approx+₱333,333
\]

Product mix effect:

\[
400,000-333,333
\]

\[
\approx+₱66,667
\]

Therefore:

| Revenue Driver | Impact |
|---|---:|
| Total volume effect | +₱333,333 |
| Product mix effect | +₱66,667 |
| Price effect | +₱95,000 |
| **Total variance** | **+₱495,000** |

**Revenue Variance Breakdown**

| Driver | Revenue Impact |
|---|---:|
| Volume | +₱333,333 |
| Mix | +₱66,667 |
| Price | +₱95,000 |

This is a more complete explanation of revenue performance.

## 6.6 Business Interpretation

Let's analyze what these results tell us.

**Finding 1: Total sales volume increased.**

Actual sales reached 20,000 units compared with the budget of 18,000 units.

This is a positive commercial development.

**Finding 2: Product C performed better than expected.**

Product C sales increased from 2,000 to 3,000 units.

Since Product C has the highest selling price, this also improved the product mix.

**Finding 3: Product B underperformed.**

Product B sales decreased by 1,000 units, and its selling price also declined.

This requires investigation.

Possible reasons:

- Customer demand declined.
- A major customer shifted to another supplier.
- Production capacity was allocated to other products.
- The company offered discounts to retain customers.

These are possible explanations, not established causes. The analyst needs supporting evidence.

**Finding 4: Selling prices generally improved.**

Products A and C achieved higher selling prices, offsetting the decline in Product B's price.

Overall, price changes contributed ₱95,000.

## 6.7 Operational Implications for Production and Supply Planning

Dito pumapasok ang tunay na S&OP analysis.

Revenue is not just a Finance Department metric.

It affects what the factory needs to produce and what Procurement needs to purchase.

| Finding | Potential Operational Impact | Recommended Investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Product A sales increased | Higher production requirement | Check capacity and raw material availability |
| Product B sales decreased | Possible excess inventory | Review production schedule and customer demand |
| Product C sales increased | Higher premium product demand | Check bottleneck capacity and critical materials |
| Higher selling prices | Higher revenue per unit | Validate customer contracts and realized prices |
| Favorable product mix | Greater demand for Product C | Review production allocation and supply readiness |

### Example: Production Allocation Problem

Suppose the factory has only enough capacity to produce 18,000 units next month.

But the updated sales forecast requires:

- Product A: 12,000 units
- Product B: 5,000 units
- Product C: 3,000 units

Total demand = 20,000 units.

There is a 2,000-unit capacity gap.

The S&OP team must decide:

1. Should additional capacity be arranged?
2. Should production be reallocated toward Product C?
3. Can some Product B orders be rescheduled?
4. Is subcontracting possible?
5. Can inventory cover the shortfall?

But we must be careful.

We cannot automatically prioritize Product C just because it has the highest selling price.

Its production requirements, material availability, manufacturing time, and cost structure must also be considered.

**This is where revenue planning starts connecting to cost and capacity planning.**

# 7. Revenue Planning in S&OP

This section explains how an S&OP analyst actually uses revenue information in the business planning cycle.

## 7.1 Translating Demand Forecasts into Financial Expectations

The basic process is:

1. **Demand Forecast:** Estimate expected product demand by month, product, and customer.
2. **Sales Validation:** Review customer orders, sales opportunities, commercial commitments, and expected demand.
3. **Price Validation:** Apply expected selling prices, contract rates, discounts, and promotions.
4. **Revenue Forecast:** Calculate projected revenue by product, customer, and period.
5. **Supply Alignment:** Validate whether production capacity, inventory, and materials can support the sales forecast.
6. **Financial Review:** Evaluate the revenue forecast against budget and financial targets.
7. **Management Decision:** Resolve revenue risks and approve the agreed business plan.

The important point:

A demand forecast becomes a financial expectation only after it is translated using appropriate commercial assumptions.

## 7.2 Roles of Sales, Finance, and Operations

Revenue planning requires cross-functional collaboration.

| Department | Primary Responsibility | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Commercial expectations | Customer orders, opportunities, selling prices |
| Demand Planning | Demand forecast | Expected quantities and demand assumptions |
| Finance | Financial validation | Revenue targets, budget, financial reconciliation |
| Operations | Supply feasibility | Production capacity, inventory, manufacturing constraints |
| Procurement | Material availability | Raw materials, supplier capacity, lead times |
| S&OP Analyst | Integration and analysis | Reconcile plans, identify gaps, explain variances |

The S&OP analyst is not expected to independently decide every commercial or production issue.

Instead, the analyst consolidates information, identifies inconsistencies, quantifies business impacts, and prepares decision-support analysis.

## 7.3 How Revenue Forecasts Influence Operational Decisions

Consider three possible situations.

**Situation A: Revenue forecast is increasing.**

Possible implications:

- More production may be required.
- Additional raw materials may need to be purchased.
- Supplier lead times become important.
- Production capacity may need adjustment.
- Finished goods inventory targets may increase.

**Situation B: Revenue forecast is decreasing.**

Possible implications:

- Production schedules may need adjustment.
- Excess inventory risk may increase.
- Procurement orders may need to be reviewed.
- Capacity may become underutilized.
- The company may need to investigate declining customer demand.

**Situation C: Revenue forecast is increasing, but product mix is changing.**

Example:

Total sales volume remains at 10,000 units, but demand shifts from standard products to premium products.

Possible implications:

- Different raw materials may be required.
- Some production lines may experience higher loading.
- Equipment changeovers may increase.
- Specific production processes may become bottlenecks.
- Existing inventory may no longer match customer requirements.

This is why an S&OP plan should not focus on total units alone.

Product-level detail matters.

## 7.4 Revenue Risks During Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where cross-functional teams review the plans and identify gaps before management decision-making.

As an analyst, you should communicate:

- Revenue forecast versus budget.
- Significant product-level variances.
- Customer demand changes.
- Selling price risks.
- Unconfirmed sales opportunities.
- Capacity-related revenue risks.
- Inventory-related revenue risks.

Example:

| Indicator | Result |
|---|---:|
| Monthly revenue budget | ₱5.0M |
| Latest revenue forecast | ₱4.5M |
| Revenue gap | -₱500K |
| Forecast variance | -10% |

A useful analyst observation:

"The latest November revenue forecast is ₱4.5 million, which is ₱500,000 or 10% below budget. The primary contributors are lower-than-expected Product B orders and reduced selling prices for one major customer. The team should validate whether additional customer demand can be secured and review the corresponding production allocation."

Notice the structure:

1. What happened?
2. How much is the impact?
3. What caused it?
4. What needs to be investigated or decided?

## 7.5 Revenue Risks During Executive S&OP

Executive S&OP focuses on major business decisions that require management alignment.

At this stage, avoid overwhelming management with hundreds of product-level data points.

Instead, present the most significant business issues.

Example:

**Executive issue: Potential revenue shortfall**

- Revenue budget: ₱5 million
- Latest forecast: ₱4.5 million
- Gap: ₱500,000
- Main risk: Lower demand for Product B
- Operational constraint: Limited available capacity for Product C
- Potential response: Evaluate additional capacity and commercial opportunities for Product C.

Management may need to decide:

- Whether to approve additional production capacity.
- Whether to prioritize certain customer orders.
- Whether to revise the financial forecast.
- Whether to pursue additional sales opportunities.
- Whether to accept the revenue shortfall.

An important distinction:

**Executive S&OP is not simply about reporting revenue. It is about presenting the financial implications of business choices and obtaining decisions.**

# 8. Key Takeaways

Before moving forward, let's consolidate the concepts you should remember.

## 8.1 Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What You Should Remember |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Financial value generated from selling products or services. |
| Sales Planning | Planning how the company will achieve its sales objectives. |
| Demand Planning | Estimating how much customers will require. |
| Revenue Planning | Converting expected sales into financial value. |
| Selling Price | Amount charged per unit. |
| Product Mix | Distribution of sales among different products. |
| Customer Mix | Distribution of sales among different customers. |
| ASP | Average revenue per unit sold. |
| PVM | Analysis of price, volume, and mix effects. |
| Revenue Variance | Difference between actual and planned revenue. |

## 8.2 Important Formulas

Keep these formulas as your initial reference.

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | \(Q\times P\) |
| Total Revenue | \(\sum(Q_i\times P_i)\) |
| Average Selling Price | \(\frac{Total\ Revenue}{Total\ Quantity}\) |
| Revenue Growth | \(\frac{Current-Previous}{Previous}\times100\) |
| Sales Volume Variance | \((AQ-BQ)\times BP\) |
| Price Variance | \((AP-BP)\times AQ\) |
| Mix Impact | \(\sum(AQ_i-ExpectedAQ_i)\times BP_i\) |
| Revenue Variance | Actual Revenue − Budget Revenue |

## 8.3 Five Important Lessons for an S&OP Analyst

1. Higher sales volume does not always mean higher revenue. Product prices and product mix matter.

2. Higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit. Costs must also be evaluated.

3. Sales forecasts and customer orders are not necessarily the same. Distinguish confirmed commitments from expected opportunities.

4. Revenue performance must be analyzed at product and customer levels. Total revenue alone may hide important problems.

5. Revenue planning must be aligned with operational feasibility. A company cannot reliably generate its projected revenue if it cannot produce and deliver the required products.
