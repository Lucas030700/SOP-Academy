# Module 7 – Financial Management
## Topic 7.5: Inventory Financial Impact

**Main learning objective:** Understand how inventory decisions affect a company's money, financial position, cash flow, operating costs, and profitability.

Sa topic na ito, pag-uusapan natin ang isang importanteng reality sa manufacturing:

**Inventory is not just a supply chain resource. It is also money invested in the business.**

Puwedeng mataas ang production output, kumpleto ang warehouse, at walang material shortage. Pero financially, hindi ibig sabihin na healthy ang company.

Bakit? Dahil maaaring malaking halaga ng pera ang nakatengga sa raw materials, WIP, at finished goods na hindi pa nabebenta.

Ito ang financial side ng inventory management.

### 1.2 Why is this topic important?

Imagine two manufacturing companies:

| Financial indicator | Company A | Company B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Annual revenue | ₱100M | ₱100M |
| Inventory value | ₱30M | ₱10M |
| Annual profit | ₱15M | ₱15M |

- Available cash for daily operations.
- Ability to purchase additional materials.
- Ability to pay suppliers.
- Financing requirements.
- Exposure to obsolete and slow-moving inventory.
- Overall financial efficiency.

**Important distinction:** Lower inventory does not automatically mean higher profit. The company must still maintain enough inventory to support customer demand and production continuity.

### 1.3 Connection to S&OP

Inventory financial impact is important because S&OP connects three major perspectives:

- **Demand:** How much inventory do customers require?
- **Supply:** How much inventory must Operations and Procurement maintain?
- **Finance:** How much money is required to support the inventory plan?

The objective is not simply to minimize inventory.

The objective is to maintain the right inventory level while balancing customer service, operational stability, and financial performance.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Think of inventory as money stored in physical form

Let's use a simple example.

May manufacturing company na gumagawa ng electronic components.

Bumili sila ng raw materials worth ₱500,000.

Instead of keeping the ₱500,000 as cash, ginamit nila ito para bumili ng:

- Ceramic powder
- Plasticizers
- Metal materials
- Packaging materials

Now, instead of cash, the company has physical materials worth ₱500,000.

That money hasn't disappeared. It has been converted into inventory.

### How money moves through manufacturing

1. **Cash used to purchase materials:** ₱500,000.
2. **Raw Materials (RM):** ₱500,000.
3. **Work in Process (WIP):** ₱650,000 after adding labor and manufacturing overhead.
4. **Finished Goods (FG):** ₱800,000 after further manufacturing costs.
5. **Product sold and delivered:** The cost is transferred to Cost of Goods Sold, and revenue is recognized.
6. **Cash collection:** Cash is received according to customer payment terms.

Notice something important:

The value of inventory increases as the company adds labor and manufacturing overhead during production.

Hindi ibig sabihin na profit na agad ang increase in value.

It simply means that additional manufacturing costs have been accumulated into the product.

### 2.2 Inventory is not the same as cash

Let's say the company has:

- Cash: ₱2M
- Raw materials: ₱5M
- WIP: ₱3M
- Finished goods: ₱4M

Total inventory = ₱12M.

Although the company has ₱12M worth of inventory, hindi ibig sabihin na may ₱12M silang available cash.

To convert inventory into cash, the company generally needs to:
1. Complete production if necessary.
2. Sell the finished product.
3. Deliver the product.
4. Collect payment from the customer.

This is why inventory management is closely connected to cash flow.

**Key concept:** Inventory represents money invested in products and materials that have not yet completed the sales-to-cash cycle.

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 Inventory as a financial asset

In accounting, inventory is generally classified as a **current asset**.

An asset is an economic resource controlled by a company that is expected to provide future economic benefits.

Inventory qualifies because the company expects to:
- Use raw materials in manufacturing.
- Convert WIP into finished products.
- Sell finished goods to customers.

Under standard financial reporting principles, inventory is generally measured at the lower of:

- Cost
- Net Realizable Value (NRV)

**Net Realizable Value** is the estimated selling price less the estimated costs necessary to complete and sell the product.

For example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Manufacturing cost of product | ₱1,000 |
| Expected selling price | ₱1,200 |
| Remaining selling and completion costs | ₱100 |
| Net Realizable Value | ₱1,100 |

The inventory remains valued at ₱1,000 because cost is lower than NRV.

But suppose the expected selling price decreases to ₱950.

NRV becomes:

\[
₱950-₱100=₱850
\]

The inventory must be written down from ₱1,000 to ₱850, assuming these are the relevant estimates.

This accounting treatment prevents companies from reporting inventory at an amount greater than what they expect to recover from it.

## 3.2 Inventory quantity versus inventory value

These two measurements answer different questions.

| Measurement | What it tells us | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory quantity | How many units do we have? | 10,000 kg |
| Inventory unit cost | How much does each unit cost? | ₱200/kg |
| Inventory value | What is the total financial value? | ₱2,000,000 |

Formula:

\[
\text{Inventory Value}=\text{Inventory Quantity}\times\text{Unit Cost}
\]

Example:

\[
10,000\text{ kg}\times ₱200/\text{kg}=₱2,000,000
\]

Important: Two warehouses may have the same physical quantity but different inventory values because of different acquisition costs, production costs, or valuation methods.

## 3.3 Three major inventory classifications

Manufacturing companies generally classify inventory into three major categories.

| Inventory type | Meaning | Financial treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Raw Materials (RM) | Materials not yet processed | Current asset |
| Work in Process (WIP) | Materials currently undergoing production | Current asset |
| Finished Goods (FG) | Completed products awaiting sale | Current asset |

Let's distinguish their financial roles.

**Raw Materials**

The company has purchased materials but has not yet consumed them in manufacturing.

Example: 1,000 kg of ceramic powder.

**Work in Process**

The company has already consumed materials and added manufacturing costs, but production is not yet complete.

Example: Ceramic material already mixed, processed, and undergoing subsequent manufacturing operations.

**Finished Goods**

Production is complete, and the product is ready for sale.

Example: Completed electronic components waiting for shipment.

The key point is that all three can represent inventory assets, even though they are at different manufacturing stages.

## 3.4 How inventory costs move from RM to WIP to FG

Ito ang isa sa pinakamahalagang concepts sa manufacturing accounting.

Kapag bumili ang company ng raw materials, hindi agad buong purchase cost ang kinikilala bilang manufacturing expense.

Initially, it becomes an inventory asset.

As materials move through production, their costs are transferred between inventory accounts.

### Inventory cost flow

1. **Raw Materials Inventory:** Material purchase cost. Example: ₱100,000.
2. **Work in Process Inventory:** Raw materials + Direct Labor + Manufacturing Overhead. Example: ₱100,000 + ₱30,000 + ₱20,000 = ₱150,000.
3. **Finished Goods Inventory:** Completed manufacturing cost of ₱150,000.
4. **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS):** When the product is sold, the ₱150,000 cost is transferred from inventory to expense.

| Manufacturing activity | Financial treatment |
|---|---|
| Purchase raw materials | Increase RM inventory |
| Issue materials to production | RM decreases, WIP increases |
| Add direct labor | WIP increases |
| Apply manufacturing overhead | WIP increases |
| Complete production | WIP decreases, FG increases |
| Sell finished goods | FG decreases, COGS increases |

**Important distinction:**

Producing a product does not automatically create an expense equal to its manufacturing cost.

The cost generally remains in inventory until the product is sold, at which point it becomes Cost of Goods Sold.

There are exceptions and adjustments, such as abnormal production losses, which may be expensed immediately.

This concept will become important when we discuss financial reconciliation in Topic 7.6.

# 4. Detailed Discussion

## 4.1 Inventory Valuation

Inventory valuation answers this question:

**At what monetary amount should the company record its inventory in financial statements?**

Hindi sapat na alam lang natin na may 10,000 units sa warehouse. Kailangan din nating malaman kung magkano ang accounting value ng 10,000 units na iyon.

Three important approaches are:

### A. FIFO – First-In, First-Out

FIFO assumes that the earliest purchased or produced inventory costs are assigned to the units sold or consumed first.

In simple terms:

*Unang pumasok na inventory cost, iyon ang unang ginagamit sa costing.*

Example:

A company purchased the following raw materials:

| Purchase | Quantity | Unit Cost | Total Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 100 kg | ₱100 | ₱10,000 |
| February | 100 kg | ₱120 | ₱12,000 |
| March | 100 kg | ₱140 | ₱14,000 |
| **Total** | **300 kg** | | **₱36,000** |

Assume the company consumes 150 kg.

Under FIFO:

- First 100 kg = ₱100/kg
- Next 50 kg = ₱120/kg

Calculation:

\[
(100\times100)+(50\times120)=₱16,000
\]

**FIFO consumption cost: ₱16,000**

Remaining inventory:

- 50 kg at ₱120 = ₱6,000
- 100 kg at ₱140 = ₱14,000

Total ending inventory:

\[
₱6,000+₱14,000=\boxed{₱20,000}
\]

**When is FIFO useful?**

- When materials have identifiable purchase batches.
- When older inventory is physically consumed first.
- When management wants a costing method that reflects older costs in consumption and newer costs in ending inventory.

Remember: FIFO is an accounting cost-flow assumption. It does not always mean that the actual physical materials were issued in that exact sequence.

### B. Weighted Average Cost

Weighted Average Cost combines the costs of available inventory and calculates one average unit cost.

Instead of assigning separate purchase costs to every batch, we calculate an average.

Using the same example:

| Purchase | Quantity | Unit Cost | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 100 kg | ₱100 | ₱10,000 |
| February | 100 kg | ₱120 | ₱12,000 |
| March | 100 kg | ₱140 | ₱14,000 |
| **Total** | **300 kg** | | **₱36,000** |

Formula:

\[
\text{Weighted Average Cost}=\frac{\text{Total Inventory Cost}}{\text{Total Inventory Quantity}}
\]

Substitute:

\[
=\frac{₱36,000}{300}=₱120/\text{kg}
\]

If the company consumes 150 kg:

\[
150\times ₱120=₱18,000
\]

Remaining inventory:

\[
150\times ₱120=₱18,000
\]

Compare the results:

| Measurement | FIFO | Weighted Average |
|---|---:|---:|
| Consumption cost | ₱16,000 | ₱18,000 |
| Ending inventory | ₱20,000 | ₱18,000 |
| Total | ₱36,000 | ₱36,000 |

Notice that total inventory cost is unchanged. Ang nagbabago ay kung paano hinahati ang cost between consumed inventory and remaining inventory.

**Why does this matter financially?**

When material prices increase, FIFO generally produces:
- Lower cost assigned to consumption.
- Higher ending inventory value.
- Higher gross profit, assuming selling prices and other costs remain unchanged.

Weighted average generally smooths out price fluctuations.

The actual impact depends on the price movement and inventory transactions.

### C. Standard Costing

Standard costing uses predetermined costs to value materials or products based on established cost assumptions.

For example:

A company establishes the following standard manufacturing cost:

| Cost component | Standard cost/unit |
|---|---:|
| Raw materials | ₱100 |
| Direct labor | ₱30 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱20 |
| **Standard manufacturing cost** | **₱150** |

Suppose actual production cost becomes ₱160 per unit.

The difference is:

\[
₱160-₱150=₱10
\]

This ₱10 difference is called a **cost variance**.

For 5,000 units:

\[
5,000\times ₱10=₱50,000
\]

The company has a ₱50,000 unfavorable manufacturing cost variance, assuming the actual cost is above the standard and the quantities are comparable.

Why use standard costing?

- Easier cost planning.
- Faster inventory valuation.
- Cost variance monitoring.
- Production cost control.
- Financial forecasting.

Under IAS 2, standard costing may be used as a technique to approximate cost when it reasonably reflects actual cost. Standards should be reviewed regularly and adjusted when necessary.

**Remember the distinction:**

FIFO and weighted average are cost formulas. Standard costing is a costing technique based on predetermined standards.

## 4.2 Inventory and Working Capital

Now let's introduce one of the most important financial concepts in inventory management.

### What is Working Capital?

Working Capital measures the difference between a company's current assets and current liabilities.

In simple terms:

Ito ang financial resources available to support the company's short-term operations after considering its short-term obligations.

Formula:

\[
\text{Working Capital}=\text{Current Assets}-\text{Current Liabilities}
\]

**Current Assets** are resources expected to be realized, sold, or consumed within the normal operating cycle or generally within 12 months.

Examples:
- Cash
- Accounts Receivable (AR)
- Inventory
- Short-term investments
- Other current assets

**Current Liabilities** are obligations expected to be settled within the normal operating cycle or generally within 12 months.

Examples:
- Accounts Payable (AP)
- Short-term loans
- Accrued expenses
- Other short-term obligations

### How does inventory affect working capital?

Let's examine a company:

| Current Assets | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | ₱2,000,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | ₱3,000,000 |
| Inventory | ₱5,000,000 |
| Other Current Assets | ₱500,000 |
| **Total Current Assets** | **₱10,500,000** |

| Current Liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts Payable | ₱3,000,000 |
| Short-term Loan | ₱1,000,000 |
| Accrued Expenses | ₱500,000 |
| **Total Current Liabilities** | **₱4,500,000** |

Working Capital:

\[
₱10.5M-₱4.5M=₱6,000,000
\]

### Relationship between Inventory, AR, and AP

These three accounts are closely connected.

| Account | Business meaning | Financial implication |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | Materials and products not yet sold | Money invested in stock |
| Accounts Receivable | Products already sold but not yet collected | Money owed by customers |
| Accounts Payable | Materials or services purchased but not yet paid | Money owed to suppliers |

Let's illustrate.

1. The company purchases ₱1M of materials on credit.
2. Inventory increases by ₱1M.
3. Accounts Payable increases by ₱1M.
4. The company manufactures products.
5. The company sells the products on credit.
6. Inventory decreases, COGS is recognized, and Accounts Receivable increases.
7. The customer eventually pays.
8. Accounts Receivable decreases and cash increases.

This is why inventory management cannot be analyzed independently from supplier and customer payment terms.

### Excess inventory ties up company funds

Suppose the company has ₱10M in inventory, but its operational requirement only justifies ₱6M.

The excess is:

\[
₱10M-₱6M=₱4M
\]

That ₱4M represents excess inventory investment relative to the target.

The company may have purchased materials too early, produced more than demand requires, or maintained unnecessarily high safety stocks.

The money could potentially support:
- Other production requirements.
- Supplier payments.
- Equipment maintenance.
- Business expansion.
- Debt reduction.

### An important financial nuance

May isang distinction na gusto kong tandaan mo.

**Reducing inventory does not automatically increase total accounting working capital.**

For example, if ₱1M of inventory is sold at its carrying value and converted into ₱1M cash:

| Current asset | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventory | ₱1M | ₱0 |
| Cash | ₱0 | ₱1M |
| Total | ₱1M | ₱1M |

Total current assets remain unchanged.

But the company's liquidity improves because cash is more immediately available than inventory.

If the company instead reduces inventory by avoiding future purchases, it can prevent cash outflows. If it uses the resulting cash to settle current liabilities, both current assets and current liabilities decrease.

For operational analysis, companies also use **Operating Working Capital**:

\[
\text{OWC}=\text{Inventory}+\text{AR}-\text{AP}
\]

This is particularly useful in S&OP because it focuses on funds tied up in the operating cycle.

Reducing excess inventory can directly reduce OWC, assuming other factors remain constant.

## 4.3 Inventory Carrying and Financial Costs

Inventory is not free to maintain.

Kahit paid na ang materials, may continuing costs habang naka-store ang inventory.

These are commonly called **Inventory Carrying Costs** or **Holding Costs**.

Let's discuss the major categories.

### A. Storage and Warehousing Costs

These are costs associated with maintaining inventory storage facilities.

Examples:
- Warehouse rental.
- Building maintenance.
- Electricity.
- Temperature and humidity control.
- Storage racks.
- Warehouse equipment.

Manufacturing example:

A company needs special temperature-controlled storage for certain raw materials.

Higher inventory quantities may require additional storage space and resources.

### B. Insurance Costs

Inventory may require insurance coverage against:
- Fire.
- Theft.
- Flood.
- Accidental damage.
- Other covered risks.

Higher inventory values may increase insurance requirements or premiums, depending on the insurance arrangement.

### C. Handling Costs

These involve the physical movement and management of inventory.

Examples:
- Forklift operation.
- Labor for receiving materials.
- Material transfers.
- Repacking.
- Cycle counting.
- Loading and unloading.

More inventory can mean more handling activity, although the relationship is not always directly proportional.

### D. Administrative Costs

These are costs associated with managing inventory information and processes.

Examples:
- Inventory monitoring.
- Warehouse administration.
- Stock reconciliation.
- Documentation.
- Inventory management systems.

### E. Obsolescence and Deterioration

This is particularly important in manufacturing.

Inventory may lose value because of:
- Changes in product design.
- Engineering changes.
- Expired shelf life.
- Material deterioration.
- Customer demand changes.
- Technology changes.
- Damaged packaging.

Example:

A company purchased specialized raw materials for a product model that was discontinued.

Even if the materials are physically present, the company may no longer have a practical use for them.

This creates an obsolescence risk.

### F. Financing Cost

When a company purchases inventory using borrowed money, it may incur interest expense.

Example:

The company borrows ₱5M to finance additional raw materials.

At an illustrative annual borrowing rate of 8%:

\[
₱5M\times8\%=₱400,000
\]

The company incurs ₱400,000 annual interest expense if the entire ₱5M remains outstanding for a full year at that rate.

### G. Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost is the potential benefit the company gives up by choosing one use of money over another.

Example:

The company has ₱5M invested in excess inventory.

That money could potentially have been used to:
- Purchase more productive equipment.
- Reduce expensive debt.
- Fund a new product.
- Support another business opportunity.

If the company could have earned a 10% annual return from an alternative investment, the illustrative opportunity cost would be:

\[
₱5M\times10\%=₱500,000
\]

This is an economic cost, not automatically an accounting expense.

### Which costs are accounting expenses?

Important distinction:

| Cost | Accounting treatment | Economic consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Warehouse rental | Generally an expense | Yes |
| Warehouse electricity | Generally an expense | Yes |
| Insurance | Generally an expense | Yes |
| Handling labor | Expense or included in eligible inventory cost, depending on activity | Yes |
| Obsolescence write-down | Expense when recognized | Yes |
| Interest on borrowing | Generally a finance expense | Yes |
| Opportunity cost | Not normally recorded as an accounting expense | Yes |
| Existing warehouse capacity used | Not necessarily an incremental accounting expense | Yes |

One important accounting nuance: certain normal production-related storage and handling costs may be included in inventory cost under applicable accounting rules. Storage costs are generally expensed unless they are necessary in the production process before a further production stage.

### 4.4 Inventory Write-Downs and Write-Offs

These two terms are related, but they are not identical.

**Inventory Write-Down**

A write-down happens when inventory's recorded value must be reduced because its Net Realizable Value is lower than its cost.

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Original inventory cost | ₱100,000 |
| Net Realizable Value | ₱70,000 |
| Required write-down | ₱30,000 |

Formula:

\[
\text{Write-down}=\text{Cost}-\text{NRV}
\]

\[
=₱100,000-₱70,000=₱30,000
\]

Financial impact:
- Inventory asset decreases by ₱30,000.
- Expense increases by ₱30,000.
- Profit decreases by ₱30,000 before tax effects.

**Inventory Write-Off**

A write-off generally occurs when inventory has no remaining recoverable value or is no longer recognized as an asset.

Example:

A batch of raw materials worth ₱50,000 becomes completely unusable due to contamination.

Assuming no recovery value:

\[
\text{Write-off}=₱50,000
\]

Financial impact:
- Inventory decreases by ₱50,000.
- Expense or loss increases by ₱50,000.
- Profit decreases by ₱50,000 before tax effects.

**Remember:**

- Write-down = Reduce inventory value.
- Write-off = Remove inventory value from the books.

Both can negatively affect profitability, even though the original cash payment may have occurred months earlier.

# 5. Formulas and Calculations

Now let's build the mathematical foundation.

## 5.1 Inventory Value

Formula:

\[
\boxed{IV=Q\times UC}
\]

Where:

| Variable | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| IV | Inventory Value | ₱ |
| Q | Inventory Quantity | Units, kg, liters, etc. |
| UC | Unit Cost | ₱/unit, ₱/kg, etc. |

Example:

- Inventory quantity = 2,500 kg
- Unit cost = ₱180/kg

\[
IV=2,500\times180=₱450,000
\]

Interpretation:

The company has ₱450,000 invested in this raw material inventory at its recorded unit cost.

It is not necessarily an expense yet.

## 5.2 Average Inventory Value

Average inventory is useful because inventory changes throughout the year.

A simple formula is:

\[
\text{Average Inventory}=\frac{\text{Beginning Inventory}+\text{Ending Inventory}}{2}
\]

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning inventory | ₱5M |
| Ending inventory | ₱7M |

\[
\frac{₱5M+₱7M}{2}=₱6M
\]

The company's average inventory value is ₱6M.

**Limitation:** This two-point average can be misleading when inventory fluctuates significantly throughout the year. A monthly or daily average may provide a more representative figure.

## 5.3 Inventory Carrying Cost

A common planning formula is:

\[
\text{Annual Carrying Cost}=\text{Average Inventory Value}\times\text{Carrying Cost Rate}
\]

Where:

- Average Inventory Value = average monetary value of stock.
- Carrying Cost Rate = annual percentage representing the cost of holding inventory.

Example:

- Average inventory = ₱6M
- Annual carrying cost rate = 20%

\[
₱6,000,000\times0.20=₱1,200,000/year
\]

Monthly equivalent:

\[
\frac{₱1,200,000}{12}=₱100,000/month
\]

Important: The 20% is an illustrative planning assumption, not a universal industry standard.

Also, carrying cost rate may combine accounting costs and economic costs. Avoid adding financing cost twice if interest is already included in the rate.

## 5.4 Working Capital

Formula:

\[
\boxed{WC=CA-CL}
\]

Where:

- WC = Working Capital (₱)
- CA = Current Assets (₱)
- CL = Current Liabilities (₱)

Example:

- Current Assets = ₱12M
- Current Liabilities = ₱7M

\[
WC=₱12M-₱7M=₱5M
\]

Interpretation:

The company has ₱5M in net current assets after accounting for its current liabilities.

This does not mean the company has ₱5M in available cash. Current assets include inventory and receivables, which must still be converted into cash.

## 5.5 Inventory Days / Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)

DIO estimates how many days, on average, inventory remains in the business before being sold or consumed through the cost of goods sold.

Formula:

\[
\boxed{DIO=\frac{\text{Average Inventory}}{\text{COGS}}\times365}
\]

Where:

- Average Inventory = average inventory value (₱).
- COGS = annual Cost of Goods Sold (₱).
- 365 = assumed days in a year.

Example:

- Average inventory = ₱6M
- Annual COGS = ₱30M

\[
DIO=\frac{6M}{30M}\times365=73\text{ days}
\]

Interpretation:

On average, the company holds inventory equivalent to approximately 73 days of COGS.

**Important:** For manufacturing companies, DIO is a financial-level metric. Operational inventory days may also be calculated separately for RM, WIP, and FG using their appropriate consumption or throughput measures. Do not automatically divide every inventory category by total COGS.

## 5.6 Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

The Cash Conversion Cycle measures how long company cash is tied up in the operating cycle.

It combines three components:

1. DIO = Days Inventory Outstanding
2. DSO = Days Sales Outstanding
3. DPO = Days Payable Outstanding

Formula:

\[
\boxed{CCC=DIO+DSO-DPO}
\]

Definitions:

| Metric | Meaning |
|---|---|
| DIO | Average days inventory is held |
| DSO | Average days customers take to pay |
| DPO | Average days the company takes to pay suppliers |

Example:

- DIO = 73 days
- DSO = 45 days
- DPO = 50 days

\[
CCC=73+45-50=68\text{ days}
\]

Interpretation:

The company has approximately 68 days between paying for its operating inputs and recovering cash from customers, based on these simplified metrics.

A shorter CCC generally indicates more efficient working capital management, although supplier relationships and customer service must also be considered.

## 5.7 Financial Impact of Inventory Reduction

Let's say a company reduces its average inventory from ₱10M to ₱7M.

Inventory reduction:

\[
₱10M-₱7M=₱3M
\]

Assuming a carrying cost rate of 20%:

Before reduction:

\[
₱10M\times20\%=₱2M
\]

After reduction:

\[
₱7M\times20\%=₱1.4M
\]

Annual carrying cost savings:

\[
₱2M-₱1.4M=₱600,000/year
\]

The company also reduces its inventory investment by ₱3M.

But take note of two separate effects:

| Financial effect | Result |
|---|---:|
| Inventory investment reduction | ₱3,000,000 |
| Estimated annual carrying cost savings | ₱600,000 |

The ₱3M is not automatically profit. It represents reduced capital tied up in inventory.

The ₱600,000 is estimated annual cost savings, but only the portion that actually reduces expenses or relevant economic costs should be treated as an accounting profit improvement.

## 5.8 Write-Down and Write-Off Calculations

**Write-down formula:**

\[
WD=\max(0,\text{Cost}-\text{NRV})
\]

Example:

- Inventory cost = ₱250,000
- NRV = ₱190,000

\[
WD=₱250,000-₱190,000=₱60,000
\]

**Write-off formula:**

\[
WO=\text{Carrying Value}-\text{Recoverable Value}
\]

Example:

- Inventory carrying value = ₱80,000
- Recoverable value = ₱10,000

\[
WO=₱80,000-₱10,000=₱70,000
\]

The inventory asset decreases by the recognized amount, with the corresponding expense or loss recognized according to the applicable accounting treatment.

If the recoverable value is zero, the entire carrying amount is written off.

# 6. Practical Manufacturing Application

Let's apply everything to a realistic manufacturing company.

For this example, imagine an electronic components manufacturer similar to the type of manufacturing environment you have encountered.

The company manufactures electronic components using ceramic raw materials and several production processes.

We will compare two situations:

- **Scenario A:** High Inventory
- **Scenario B:** Optimized Inventory

Our objective is to determine how inventory management affects the company's financial performance.

## 6.1 Manufacturing Inventory Profile

Assume the company maintains three types of inventory.

### Scenario A: High Inventory

| Inventory | Quantity | Unit Cost | Inventory Value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RM | 15,000 kg | ₱200/kg | ₱3,000,000 |
| WIP | 5,000 units | ₱350/unit | ₱1,750,000 |
| FG | 4,000 units | ₱500/unit | ₱2,000,000 |
| **Total** | | | **₱6,750,000** |

Let's understand the calculations.

**Raw Materials:**

\[
15,000\times ₱200=₱3,000,000
\]

**Work in Process:**

\[
5,000\times ₱350=₱1,750,000
\]

**Finished Goods:**

\[
4,000\times ₱500=₱2,000,000
\]

Total:

\[
3M+1.75M+2M=₱6,750,000
\]

This means the company has ₱6.75M invested in inventory.

Notice that the unit cost of WIP and FG is higher because additional manufacturing costs have been accumulated.

## 6.2 Scenario B: Optimized Inventory

After reviewing its demand forecast, production plan, supplier lead times, and safety stock requirements, the company identifies opportunities to reduce excess inventory.

The new inventory target becomes:

| Inventory | Quantity | Unit Cost | Inventory Value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RM | 10,000 kg | ₱200/kg | ₱2,000,000 |
| WIP | 4,000 units | ₱350/unit | ₱1,400,000 |
| FG | 3,000 units | ₱500/unit | ₱1,500,000 |
| **Total** | | | **₱4,900,000** |

Total inventory value:

\[
2M+1.4M+1.5M=₱4,900,000
\]

## 6.3 Compare Inventory Investment

| Inventory | High Inventory | Optimized Inventory | Reduction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RM | ₱3.00M | ₱2.00M | ₱1.00M |
| WIP | ₱1.75M | ₱1.40M | ₱0.35M |
| FG | ₱2.00M | ₱1.50M | ₱0.50M |
| **Total** | **₱6.75M** | **₱4.90M** | **₱1.85M** |

Inventory reduction percentage:

\[
\frac{6.75M-4.90M}{6.75M}\times100=27.41\%
\]

**Inventory investment reduction: ₱1.85M, or 27.41%.**

Operationally, the company has reduced the amount of inventory maintained in the manufacturing system.

Financially, it has reduced its inventory investment by ₱1.85M.

But we still need to examine the carrying cost.

## 6.4 Calculate Inventory Carrying Costs

Assume the company uses an annual inventory carrying cost rate of 20%.

This is an illustrative planning assumption.

### High Inventory

\[
₱6,750,000\times20\%=₱1,350,000/year
\]

Monthly equivalent:

\[
\frac{₱1,350,000}{12}=₱112,500/month
\]

### Optimized Inventory

\[
₱4,900,000\times20\%=₱980,000/year
\]

Monthly equivalent:

\[
\frac{₱980,000}{12}=₱81,666.67/month
\]

### Carrying Cost Savings

\[
₱1,350,000-₱980,000=₱370,000/year
\]

| | High | Optimized |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventory value | ₱6.75M | ₱4.90M |
| Carrying rate | 20% | 20% |
| Annual carrying cost | ₱1.35M | ₱980K |

**Estimated annual carrying cost reduction: ₱370,000.**

Business interpretation:

The company has reduced:
- Capital invested in inventory by ₱1.85M.
- Estimated annual inventory carrying costs by ₱370,000.

However, the actual accounting expense reduction depends on which components of the 20% carrying rate are avoidable.

For example, reducing inventory may not immediately reduce warehouse rental if the company still occupies the same warehouse.

## 6.5 Working Capital Impact

Let's examine the company's financial position before and after inventory optimization.

Assume the following:

| Current Assets | High Inventory |
|---|---:|
| Cash | ₱2,000,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | ₱3,000,000 |
| Inventory | ₱6,750,000 |
| Other Current Assets | ₱250,000 |
| **Total Current Assets** | **₱12,000,000** |

| Current Liabilities | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts Payable | ₱4,000,000 |
| Short-term Debt | ₱1,000,000 |
| Accrued Expenses | ₱1,000,000 |
| **Total Current Liabilities** | **₱6,000,000** |

Working Capital:

\[
12M-6M=₱6M
\]

Now assume the company reduces inventory by ₱1.85M through better procurement planning and by selling or consuming excess inventory, while keeping the released funds as cash.

The revised current assets become:

| Current Assets | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cash | ₱2.00M | ₱3.85M |
| Accounts Receivable | ₱3.00M | ₱3.00M |
| Inventory | ₱6.75M | ₱4.90M |
| Other Current Assets | ₱0.25M | ₱0.25M |
| **Total** | **₱12.00M** | **₱12.00M** |

Working Capital remains:

\[
12M-6M=₱6M
\]

This is an important lesson.

The company has improved the availability of cash without necessarily increasing total working capital.

Now let's examine Operating Working Capital.

\[
OWC=Inventory+AR-AP
\]

Before optimization:

\[
6.75M+3M-4M=₱5.75M
\]

After optimization:

\[
4.90M+3M-4M=₱3.90M
\]

Reduction:

\[
5.75M-3.90M=₱1.85M
\]

The company has reduced its operating working capital requirement by ₱1.85M.

**This is why inventory reduction is financially important even when total accounting working capital does not change.**

## 6.6 Calculate Inventory Days

Assume the company's annual COGS is ₱24M.

For simplicity, use the respective inventory values as representative average inventory balances.

High inventory:

\[
DIO=\frac{6.75M}{24M}\times365=102.66\text{ days}
\]

Optimized inventory:

\[
DIO=\frac{4.90M}{24M}\times365=74.48\text{ days}
\]

Comparison:

| KPI | High Inventory | Optimized |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventory Value | ₱6.75M | ₱4.90M |
| Annual COGS | ₱24M | ₱24M |
| DIO | 102.66 days | 74.48 days |

The company has reduced its inventory days by approximately 28.18 days.

This suggests improved inventory efficiency, provided that the company can still satisfy demand and maintain production continuity.

## 6.7 What if Inventory Reduction Causes Stockouts?

Dito pumapasok ang importanteng balance between Operations and Finance.

Suppose the company reduces FG inventory too aggressively.

Before optimization:
- FG inventory = 4,000 units.
- Customer demand = 3,000 units.
- Customer orders are fulfilled.

After optimization:
- FG inventory = 1,000 units.
- Customer demand unexpectedly increases to 2,000 units.
- Only 1,000 units are immediately available.

The company now faces a potential shortage of 1,000 units.

Possible consequences:
- Delayed customer deliveries.
- Lost sales.
- Expedited production.
- Additional transportation costs.
- Customer dissatisfaction.
- Potential penalties.

So even if inventory investment decreases, the business may suffer greater financial losses elsewhere.

**The lesson: Inventory optimization must consider demand variability, replenishment lead time, production capacity, and required customer service levels.**

The lowest inventory is not necessarily the best inventory.

# 7. Inventory Financial Impact in S&OP

Now let's connect the concepts to actual S&OP activities.

This section is especially important because it explains how an S&OP analyst uses inventory financial information to support management decisions.

## 7.1 How Production and Procurement Decisions Affect Inventory Investment

Every production and procurement decision can change the company's inventory value.

| Business decision | Inventory impact | Financial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase more raw materials | RM increases | More capital invested |
| Increase production ahead of demand | WIP and FG may increase | Higher inventory investment |
| Produce in large batches | Higher average inventory | Higher carrying costs |
| Reduce production | Potential FG reduction | Lower inventory investment |
| Delay unnecessary procurement | RM accumulation avoided | Cash outflow potentially avoided |
| Improve production yield | Less material consumed per good unit | Potentially lower material cost per good unit |
| Reduce production lead time | Less WIP accumulation | Lower WIP investment |

Let's examine an important manufacturing situation.

**Example: Procurement build-ahead**

A supplier informs the company that a particular raw material will experience a price increase next month.

Procurement proposes buying six months of material requirements immediately.

At first glance, this seems beneficial because the company can secure the current price.

However, the company must evaluate:

- How much additional inventory will be purchased?
- How much cash is required?
- Is warehouse capacity sufficient?
- What is the material's shelf life?
- Will the product design change?
- How much financing cost will be incurred?
- Does the purchase create excess inventory?

The decision should not be based on material price alone.

Finance and Operations must evaluate the total financial impact.

## 7.2 How Inventory Build-Ahead Affects Cash Requirements

Inventory build-ahead means purchasing or producing inventory earlier than the immediate requirement.

This is sometimes necessary when:
- Demand is expected to increase.
- Production capacity will be limited in future periods.
- Suppliers have long lead times.
- Seasonal demand is approaching.
- A planned shutdown is approaching.

But build-ahead requires additional working capital.

Example:

The company plans to produce 10,000 additional units.

Assume manufacturing cost is ₱500/unit.

\[
10,000\times ₱500=₱5,000,000
\]

The company needs to finance ₱5M of additional production cost.

If the products are manufactured three months before they are sold, the company must carry that inventory during the waiting period.

At a 20% annual carrying rate, the illustrative three-month carrying cost is:

\[
₱5M\times20\%\times\frac{3}{12}=₱250,000
\]

The company must evaluate whether the benefit of build-ahead exceeds the additional inventory and financing costs.

## 7.3 How Inventory Reduction Affects Working Capital and Customer Service

Inventory reduction can create several financial opportunities:

1. Lower inventory investment.
2. Lower exposure to obsolescence.
3. Lower storage and handling requirements.
4. Reduced financing requirements.
5. Improved cash availability.
6. Lower operating working capital.

However, the company must also evaluate its service-level targets.

For example:

| Inventory strategy | Potential benefit | Potential risk |
|---|---|---|
| High safety stock | Better protection against demand variability | Higher holding costs |
| Low safety stock | Lower inventory investment | Greater stockout exposure |
| Frequent procurement | Lower average RM inventory | Higher ordering and transportation costs |
| Large production batches | Potentially lower setup cost per unit | Higher WIP and FG inventory |

The best strategy depends on the company's supply chain characteristics.

## 7.4 How Finance and Operations Evaluate Inventory Targets

Finance and Operations often look at the same inventory from different perspectives.

| Operations perspective | Finance perspective |
|---|---|
| Is there enough material? | How much capital is invested? |
| Can production continue? | What are the inventory carrying costs? |
| Can we meet customer demand? | How does inventory affect working capital? |
| Are supplier lead times reliable? | What is the financing requirement? |
| Is safety stock sufficient? | Is the inventory level financially justified? |
| Are we producing efficiently? | Are we generating sufficient returns from invested capital? |

The objective is to find a balanced inventory target.

For example:

Operations may recommend maintaining ₱8M of inventory to protect production continuity.

Finance may recommend reducing inventory to ₱6M to improve working capital.

The S&OP analyst should not simply choose one side.

Instead, investigate:

- Which materials contribute most to the ₱8M?
- Which items are critical?
- Which materials have long lead times?
- Which inventory is slow-moving?
- Which products have uncertain demand?
- Can production scheduling be improved?
- Can supplier delivery frequency be increased?
- What customer service level is required?

Then present a financially and operationally supported recommendation.

## 7.5 How an S&OP Analyst Communicates Inventory Financial Risks and Opportunities

An S&OP analyst should translate inventory data into business implications.

For example, instead of reporting:

> Inventory increased from ₱5M to ₱7M.

A more useful S&OP analysis would be:

> Total inventory increased by ₱2M, primarily driven by higher raw material purchases and finished goods build-ahead. The increase represents additional capital tied up in inventory and may increase carrying costs. Based on the current demand forecast and production schedule, selected inventory categories may exceed planned requirements. Operations and Procurement should review replenishment timing and production quantities to identify opportunities to reduce excess inventory without compromising customer service.

Notice the difference.

The first statement reports a number.

The second explains:
- What changed.
- Why it changed.
- What the financial impact could be.
- What action should be considered.

### 7.6 Practical S&OP Inventory Financial Dashboard

As an S&OP analyst, these are useful indicators to monitor:

| KPI | What it measures | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Total Inventory Value | Total financial value of inventory | Measures inventory investment |
| RM Value | Raw material investment | Monitors procurement exposure |
| WIP Value | Production-stage investment | Identifies production bottlenecks |
| FG Value | Finished goods investment | Monitors unsold products |
| Inventory Days | Inventory relative to COGS | Measures inventory efficiency |
| Inventory Carrying Cost | Cost of maintaining inventory | Measures holding-cost exposure |
| Excess Inventory Value | Inventory above target or requirement | Identifies reduction opportunities |
| Obsolete Inventory Value | Inventory with reduced or no recoverable value | Measures potential financial losses |
| Inventory Turns | How frequently inventory is consumed or sold relative to average inventory | Measures inventory utilization |
| Operating Working Capital | Inventory + AR − AP | Measures capital tied up in operations |

These metrics can be integrated into monthly S&OP reporting.

### 7.7 A Practical Example of an S&OP Recommendation

Imagine that during the monthly S&OP cycle, you identify the following:

| Indicator | Current | Target |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total inventory | ₱12M | ₱9M |
| Inventory days | 90 days | 70 days |
| Carrying cost rate | 20% | 20% |
| Customer service level | 98% | 98% |

Potential inventory investment reduction:

\[
₱12M-₱9M=₱3M
\]

Estimated annual carrying cost reduction:

\[
₱3M\times20\%=₱600,000
\]

However, the company wants to maintain its 98% customer service level.

Your responsibility as an analyst is to investigate whether the ₱3M reduction is achievable without creating unacceptable supply risks.

You might recommend:

1. Reduce slow-moving finished goods.
2. Review materials with excessive coverage.
3. Align procurement schedules with actual demand.
4. Review WIP accumulation and production bottlenecks.
5. Maintain appropriate safety stocks for critical materials.
6. Monitor customer service and stockout performance after implementation.

This is the kind of analysis that connects Operations, Supply Chain, and Finance.

# 8. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate the most important concepts from this lesson.

## 8.1 Concepts You Must Understand

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Inventory as an asset | Inventory represents resources with expected future economic benefits. |
| Inventory value | Quantity multiplied by unit cost. |
| RM | Purchased materials awaiting production. |
| WIP | Materials and manufacturing costs accumulated during production. |
| FG | Completed products awaiting sale. |
| FIFO | Earlier inventory costs are assigned to consumption first. |
| Weighted Average | Available inventory costs are averaged. |
| Standard Costing | Uses predetermined costs for planning and cost control. |
| Working Capital | Current Assets minus Current Liabilities. |
| Operating Working Capital | Inventory + AR − AP. |
| Carrying Cost | Cost or economic burden of maintaining inventory. |
| DIO | Measures average inventory days relative to COGS. |
| CCC | Measures the time between cash outflow for operations and cash recovery. |
| Write-down | Reduces inventory's recorded value. |
| Write-off | Removes inventory value that is no longer recoverable. |

## 8.2 The Most Important Financial Distinctions

These distinctions are especially important for professional S&OP work.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inventory Value | Monetary value of inventory currently recorded as an asset. |
| Inventory Expense | Cost recognized in the income statement, such as COGS or an inventory write-down. |
| Cash Flow | Actual movement of money into and out of the company. |
| Inventory Investment | Capital committed to maintaining inventory. |
| Carrying Cost | Financial and economic burden of holding inventory. |
| Working Capital | Difference between current assets and current liabilities. |

Remember these three statements:

1. **Inventory value is not the same as inventory expense.** Unsold inventory generally remains an asset.
2. **Inventory reduction is not automatically profit.** It may release capital and reduce certain holding costs, but the financial effects must be distinguished.
3. **Lower inventory is not automatically better.** Inventory must still support production continuity and customer service.

## 8.3 The Big Picture: How Inventory Affects Financial Performance

**Inventory Management Decisions** (procurement, production, safety stock, replenishment) influence:

- **Inventory Investment:** RM, WIP, FG value and working capital.
- **Holding Costs:** Storage, insurance, financing, obsolescence.
- **Cash Flow:** Purchasing, payment timing, collection.
- **Customer Service:** Availability, shortages, delivery performance.

These combine to affect overall business performance: **Profitability + Liquidity + Operational Stability.**
