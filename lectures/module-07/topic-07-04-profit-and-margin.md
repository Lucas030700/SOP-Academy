# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 What is Profit?

Ang profit ay ang perang natitira sa company pagkatapos ibawas ang applicable costs and expenses mula sa revenue.

Simple example:

May manufacturing business na nagbebenta ng isang machine component.

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Total sales | ₱100,000 |
| Manufacturing and other applicable costs | ₱70,000 |
| Remaining amount | ₱30,000 |

Therefore:

\[
Profit = ₱100,000-₱70,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Profit=₱30,000}
\]

Ibig sabihin, kumita ang business ng ₱30,000 based on the costs included in this calculation.

Pero importanteng tandaan: hindi ibig sabihin na lahat ng ₱30,000 ay automatically available cash ng company. May iba pang expenses, taxes, receivables, and cash obligations na maaaring makaapekto sa actual financial position.

## 2.2 Revenue vs Cost vs Profit vs Cash Flow

Magkakaugnay ang apat na concepts na ito, pero hindi sila interchangeable.

| Concept | Simple explanation | Example |
|---|---|---:|
| Revenue | Pera/value earned from selling products or services | ₱100,000 |
| Cost | Resources consumed to produce or deliver products | ₱70,000 |
| Profit | Financial gain after deducting applicable costs and expenses | ₱30,000 |
| Cash Flow | Actual movement of money entering and leaving the business | Depends on collections and payments |

### Let's use a practical scenario.

Nag-manufacture ang company ng 1,000 units worth ₱100 each.

Total sales revenue:

\[
1,000\times ₱100=₱100,000
\]

The company incurred ₱70,000 in applicable costs.

Accounting-wise, may ₱30,000 profit.

However, hindi pa nagbabayad ang customer. Meaning:

- Revenue may already be recognized, depending on the accounting rules and delivery terms.
- Profit may already be recorded.
- Cash may not yet have been received.

Ito ang difference between profitability and cash flow.

**Important principle: A profitable company can still experience cash flow problems.**

For example, maaaring kumikita ang company sa financial statements pero nahihirapan itong magbayad ng suppliers dahil delayed ang customer collections.

## 2.3 Why Increasing Sales Does Not Always Increase Profit

Ito ang isa sa pinakamahalagang concepts sa topic na ito.

Imagine na nagbebenta ang company ng product for ₱100 per unit.

| Item | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Selling price | ₱100 | ₱100 |
| Production cost per unit | ₱70 | ₱85 |
| Sales volume | 1,000 | 1,500 |
| Revenue | ₱100,000 | ₱150,000 |
| Total production cost | ₱70,000 | ₱127,500 |
| Gross profit | ₱30,000 | ₱22,500 |

Notice something?

**Revenue increased, but profit decreased.**

Bakit?

Because the additional production volume required more expensive resources, perhaps due to:

- Overtime labor
- Expedited raw materials
- Higher subcontracting costs
- Manufacturing inefficiencies
- Additional production losses

Kaya hindi automatic na kapag dumami ang orders, mas magiging profitable ang company.

**The objective is not simply to maximize sales volume. It is to maximize profitable sales while maintaining operational feasibility.**

# 3. Technical Explanation

Ngayon, let's introduce the formal financial concepts used by Finance, Sales, Operations, and S&OP teams.

## 3.1 Three Major Types of Profit

Hindi iisa ang meaning ng profit sa financial reporting. May iba't ibang levels dahil may iba't ibang costs na kailangang ibawas.

Ang three major types are:

1. Gross Profit
2. Operating Profit
3. Net Profit

### The Profit Waterfall

Illustrative example only:

| Profit calculation | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱16,000,000 |
| Less: Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) | (₱10,600,000) |
| **Gross Profit** | **₱5,400,000** |
| Less: Operating Expenses | (₱2,000,000) |
| **Operating Profit** | **₱3,400,000** |
| Less: Interest and other non-operating expenses | (₱400,000) |
| **Profit Before Tax** | **₱3,000,000** |
| Less: Income Tax | (₱750,000) |
| **Net Profit** | **₱2,250,000** |

*Illustrative example only. Amounts are assumed for learning purposes.*

### A. Gross Profit

Gross Profit measures how much money remains after deducting the Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) from Revenue.

COGS generally includes the costs assigned to products sold, such as:

- Direct materials
- Direct labor
- Manufacturing overhead allocated to production

It answers:

**"After manufacturing or acquiring the products sold, how much remains from sales?"**

For a manufacturing company, this is important because it helps evaluate production economics.

For example, if raw material prices increase, gross profit may decrease even if sales revenue remains unchanged.

### B. Operating Profit

Operating Profit measures how much profit remains after deducting operating expenses from Gross Profit.

Operating expenses may include:

- Selling expenses
- Marketing expenses
- Administrative salaries
- Office expenses
- Other operating expenses

It answers:

**"How profitable is the company's normal business operation?"**

A company may have high gross profit but relatively low operating profit because its operating expenses are high.

### C. Net Profit

Net Profit is the profit remaining after accounting for all applicable expenses, including:

- COGS
- Operating expenses
- Interest expenses
- Other non-operating expenses
- Income taxes

It answers:

**"After all recognized expenses and taxes, how much profit does the company retain?"**

This is commonly referred to as the company's bottom line.

### Important distinction

| Profit type | Costs deducted | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Gross Profit | COGS | Product and production profitability |
| Operating Profit | COGS + operating expenses | Operating business profitability |
| Net Profit | All applicable expenses and taxes | Overall accounting profitability |

# 4. Understanding Profit Margins

## 4.1 What is Profit Margin?

Ang profit ay amount ng kinita ng company.

Ang profit margin naman ay percentage ng revenue na naging profit.

Example:

- Revenue = ₱100,000
- Profit = ₱20,000

Meaning, out of every ₱100 in sales, ₱20 remains as profit.

\[
Profit\ Margin=\frac{20,000}{100,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{20\%}
\]

Mas madaling gamitin ang margin kapag nagko-compare ng different products, customers, business units, or periods na magkakaiba ang sales values.

## 4.2 The Four Important Profitability Margins

### A. Gross Profit Margin (GPM)

Measures the percentage of revenue remaining after deducting COGS.

\[
GPM=\frac{Gross\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱500,000 |
| COGS | ₱350,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱150,000 |

\[
GPM=\frac{150,000}{500,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{GPM=30\%}
\]

**Interpretation:** Every ₱100 of revenue generates ₱30 gross profit before operating expenses and other deductions.

Management uses this to evaluate:

- Selling price versus manufacturing cost
- Raw material cost changes
- Production efficiency
- Product pricing
- Product profitability

### B. Operating Profit Margin (OPM)

Measures the percentage of revenue remaining after operating expenses.

\[
OPM=\frac{Operating\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱500,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱150,000 |
| Operating Expenses | ₱80,000 |
| Operating Profit | ₱70,000 |

\[
OPM=\frac{70,000}{500,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{OPM=14\%}
\]

**Interpretation:** Every ₱100 in revenue generates ₱14 operating profit.

Management uses this to evaluate whether the business is operating efficiently after considering its operating expenses.

### C. Net Profit Margin (NPM)

Measures the percentage of revenue that remains as net profit.

\[
NPM=\frac{Net\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱500,000 |
| Net Profit | ₱35,000 |

\[
NPM=\frac{35,000}{500,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{NPM=7\%}
\]

**Interpretation:** Every ₱100 in revenue produces ₱7 in net profit.

Management uses this to assess overall profitability after all included expenses and taxes.

### D. Contribution Margin (CM)

Ito ang particularly important sa production planning and S&OP.

Contribution Margin measures how much revenue remains after deducting variable costs.

\[
Contribution\ Margin=Revenue-Variable\ Costs
\]

Unlike Gross Profit, Contribution Margin focuses on the relationship between sales and variable costs.

Variable costs change with production or sales volume.

Examples:

- Variable raw material consumption
- Variable production labor, where applicable
- Variable packaging
- Variable production utilities
- Sales commissions, when applicable

Bakit importante ito?

Because contribution margin tells management how much money is available to cover fixed costs and eventually generate profit.

For example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Selling price per unit | ₱1,000 |
| Variable cost per unit | ₱600 |
| Contribution Margin per unit | ₱400 |

Every unit sold contributes ₱400 toward covering fixed costs and generating profit.

**Important:** Contribution Margin is not the same as Gross Profit. The cost classifications and accounting treatment may differ. Gross Profit uses COGS, while Contribution Margin uses variable costs.

## 4.3 What Does Each Metric Tell Management?

| Metric | Main management question |
|---|---|
| Gross Profit Margin | Are our products profitable after production costs? |
| Operating Profit Margin | Are we managing the business efficiently? |
| Net Profit Margin | How much of our sales ultimately becomes profit? |
| Contribution Margin | How much does each sale contribute toward fixed costs and profit? |

## 4.4 Margin vs Markup

Ito ang common source of confusion, especially when dealing with pricing.

Both measure profit relative to a different base.

- Margin compares profit against selling price.
- Markup compares profit against cost.

Let's assume:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Product cost | ₱80 |
| Selling price | ₱100 |
| Profit | ₱20 |

**Margin:**

\[
Margin=\frac{20}{100}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{20\%}
\]

**Markup:**

\[
Markup=\frac{20}{80}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{25\%}
\]

Same ₱20 profit, different percentages because the denominators are different.

Why does this matter?

Suppose Sales tells Finance:

"We are applying a 25% markup to our products."

That does not mean the company has a 25% profit margin.

A 25% markup produces a 20% margin under this simple pricing example.

# 5. Factors Affecting Profitability

Profitability is influenced by both commercial and operational decisions.

Let's examine the factors from an S&OP perspective.

| Factor | How it affects profitability | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sales volume | Higher volume may increase total profit if additional sales generate positive contribution | More units sold |
| Selling price | Higher selling price generally increases margin if volume and costs remain unchanged | Price increase |
| Product mix | Different products have different margins and contribution levels | More high-margin products |
| Customer mix | Customers have different pricing, service, and delivery requirements | Special customer discounts |
| Raw material cost | Higher material prices increase production costs | Copper or resin price increase |
| Labor cost | Higher labor costs can reduce profit | Overtime payments |
| Manufacturing efficiency | Lower yield and higher scrap increase cost per good unit | Defective production |
| Capacity utilization | Poor utilization can increase fixed cost per unit | Underutilized production line |
| Inventory holding costs | Carrying excess inventory creates additional expenses | Storage and obsolescence |
| Overtime | Additional labor premiums may increase production costs | Weekend production |
| Subcontracting | External processing may be more expensive | Outsourced production |
| Expedited procurement | Emergency purchases and premium freight increase costs | Air freight instead of sea freight |
| Discounts | Lower selling prices reduce revenue per unit | Promotional pricing |
| Returns | Returned products may create lost revenue and additional costs | Customer rejects |
| Production losses | Scrap, rework, and yield losses consume resources without generating equivalent saleable output | Material wastage |

### 5.1 Capacity Utilization and Profitability

Let's say a manufacturing line has a monthly capacity of 10,000 units.

Scenario A:

- Actual production: 5,000 units
- Fixed manufacturing costs: ₱500,000

Scenario B:

- Actual production: 10,000 units
- Fixed manufacturing costs: ₱500,000

Fixed manufacturing cost per unit:

Scenario A:

\[
\frac{500,000}{5,000}=₱100/unit
\]

Scenario B:

\[
\frac{500,000}{10,000}=₱50/unit
\]

Fixed manufacturing cost per unit decreases as production volume increases, assuming the same fixed cost and relevant capacity range.

This is an example of fixed-cost absorption.

However, higher utilization is not automatically better.

If the company exceeds practical capacity, it may need overtime, subcontracting, or additional equipment.

These can increase costs and potentially reduce profitability.

### 5.2 Manufacturing Efficiency and Yield

Let's use a simple example.

A production process consumes raw materials worth ₱100,000.

Scenario A:
- Input: 1,000 units
- Good output: 950 units
- Scrap: 50 units

Scenario B:
- Input: 1,000 units
- Good output: 900 units
- Scrap: 100 units

Assuming all material cost is consumed by the production run:

Scenario A material cost per good unit:

\[
\frac{100,000}{950}=₱105.26
\]

Scenario B material cost per good unit:

\[
\frac{100,000}{900}=₱111.11
\]

The second scenario has a higher material cost per good unit because fewer saleable units were produced from the same material investment.

This is why manufacturing yield and profitability are connected.

For an S&OP analyst, a capacity plan that assumes 100% yield when actual performance is lower can overestimate available saleable output and underestimate the resources required.

# 6. Profitability Analysis

Hindi lang overall company profitability ang dapat tingnan. Sometimes, kailangan nating i-break down ang profitability by product, customer, or product mix.

## 6.1 Product-Level Profitability

This determines how profitable an individual product is.

Example:

| Product | Revenue | COGS | Gross Profit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | ₱500,000 | ₱400,000 | ₱100,000 |
| B | ₱300,000 | ₱180,000 | ₱120,000 |

Product A generates more revenue.

However, Product B generates more gross profit.

This tells us that higher revenue does not necessarily mean higher product profitability.

## 6.2 Customer-Level Profitability

Companies may also evaluate profitability per customer.

Why?

Because customers may have different commercial and operational requirements.

Consider:

| Item | Customer X | Customer Y |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱1,000,000 | ₱800,000 |
| COGS | ₱700,000 | ₱500,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱300,000 | ₱300,000 |
| Special delivery costs | ₱80,000 | ₱20,000 |
| Additional service costs | ₱40,000 | ₱10,000 |
| Remaining contribution after listed costs | ₱180,000 | ₱270,000 |

Although Customer X generates higher revenue, Customer Y generates a better result after the additional customer-related costs shown.

This is why customer profitability analysis may include:

- Freight and delivery costs
- Special packaging
- Discounts and rebates
- Returns and warranty costs
- Special handling
- Customer-specific service requirements

Not all companies allocate these costs in the same way, so Finance must define a consistent methodology.

## 6.3 Product Mix and Contribution

Product mix refers to the combination or proportion of different products sold by a company.

Let's assume:

- Product A has a contribution margin of ₱400 per unit.
- Product B has a contribution margin of ₱700 per unit.

If the company sells more Product B, it may generate more contribution per unit sold, assuming the same relevant conditions.

But we must also consider:

- Customer demand
- Production capacity
- Material availability
- Bottleneck resources
- Delivery commitments
- Product-specific constraints

A product with a higher contribution margin may not always be the best choice if it consumes significantly more constrained production capacity.

We will return to this when evaluating S&OP alternatives.

## 6.4 High-Revenue vs High-Margin Products

Remember these distinctions:

| Product characteristic | What it means |
|---|---|
| High revenue | Generates a large amount of sales |
| High gross profit | Generates a large amount of gross profit |
| High gross margin | Retains a higher percentage of sales after COGS |
| High contribution | Provides a larger amount toward fixed costs and profit |
| High contribution margin ratio | Retains a higher percentage of sales after variable costs |

These are not interchangeable.

A product may have:

- High revenue but low margin.
- Low revenue but high margin.
- High gross profit but low contribution after customer-specific expenses.
- High contribution per unit but low contribution per constrained machine hour.

A professional S&OP analyst should understand which measure is relevant to the decision being evaluated.

## 6.5 Favorable and Unfavorable Profit Variances

A variance is the difference between actual performance and planned or budgeted performance.

Example:

| Metric | Budget | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱10M | ₱11M |
| COGS | ₱7M | ₱8.5M |
| Gross Profit | ₱3M | ₱2.5M |

Revenue variance:

\[
11M-10M=+₱1M
\]

COGS variance:

\[
8.5M-7M=+₱1.5M
\]

Gross profit variance:

\[
2.5M-3M=-₱500,000
\]

Although revenue exceeded budget, gross profit was below budget.

This is an unfavorable gross profit variance.

Possible explanations include:

- Higher raw material prices
- Increased overtime
- Lower production yield
- Unfavorable product mix
- Lower selling prices
- Higher manufacturing overhead

**Important:** A favorable revenue variance does not automatically mean a favorable profit variance. Likewise, a cost variance must be interpreted based on the budgeted and actual cost, volume, and accounting assumptions.

# 7. Formulas and Calculations

Ito na ang mathematical part. We'll build from the simplest profit calculation toward break-even analysis.

## 7.1 Gross Profit

**Formula:**

\[
GP=Revenue-COGS
\]

Where:

- GP = Gross Profit (₱)
- Revenue = Recognized sales revenue (₱)
- COGS = Cost of Goods Sold (₱)

Example:

- Revenue = ₱2,000,000
- COGS = ₱1,300,000

Step 1:

\[
GP=2,000,000-1,300,000
\]

Step 2:

\[
\boxed{GP=₱700,000}
\]

Interpretation: The company generated ₱700,000 gross profit before operating expenses and other applicable deductions.

## 7.2 Gross Profit Margin

**Formula:**

\[
GPM=\frac{GP}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Using the previous example:

\[
GPM=\frac{700,000}{2,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{GPM=35\%}
\]

Interpretation: Every ₱100 in revenue generates ₱35 gross profit.

## 7.3 Operating Profit and Operating Profit Margin

**Formula:**

\[
Operating\ Profit=GP-Operating\ Expenses
\]

Assume:

- Gross Profit = ₱700,000
- Operating Expenses = ₱250,000

\[
Operating\ Profit=700,000-250,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Profit=₱450,000}
\]

Operating Profit Margin:

\[
OPM=\frac{Operating\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

\[
OPM=\frac{450,000}{2,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{OPM=22.5\%}
\]

## 7.4 Net Profit and Net Profit Margin

For this simplified example, assume:

- Operating Profit = ₱450,000
- Interest and other non-operating expenses = ₱50,000
- Income tax = ₱100,000

Net Profit:

\[
Net\ Profit=450,000-50,000-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Net\ Profit=₱300,000}
\]

Net Profit Margin:

\[
NPM=\frac{Net\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

\[
NPM=\frac{300,000}{2,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{NPM=15\%}
\]

## 7.5 Contribution Margin per Unit

**Formula:**

\[
CM/unit=Selling\ Price-Variable\ Cost/unit
\]

Where:

- CM/unit = Contribution Margin per unit (₱/unit)
- Selling Price = Selling price per unit (₱/unit)
- Variable Cost/unit = Variable cost per unit (₱/unit)

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Selling price | ₱1,500/unit |
| Variable manufacturing cost | ₱800/unit |
| Other variable selling costs | ₱100/unit |
| Total variable cost | ₱900/unit |

\[
CM/unit=1,500-900
\]

\[
\boxed{CM/unit=₱600}
\]

Interpretation: Each unit sold contributes ₱600 toward covering fixed costs and generating profit.

## 7.6 Contribution Margin Ratio

**Formula:**

\[
CM\ Ratio=\frac{Total\ CM}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Alternatively:

\[
CM\ Ratio=\frac{CM/unit}{Selling\ Price/unit}\times100
\]

Using our previous example:

\[
CM\ Ratio=\frac{600}{1,500}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CM\ Ratio=40\%}
\]

Interpretation: Every ₱100 in sales generates ₱40 contribution after variable costs.

## 7.7 Markup vs Margin

Let's use the same example:

- Product cost = ₱800
- Selling price = ₱1,000
- Profit = ₱200

Margin:

\[
Margin=\frac{200}{1,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{20\%}
\]

Markup:

\[
Markup=\frac{200}{800}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{25\%}
\]

**Alternative formulas when calculating a selling price:**

If you know the cost and desired margin:

\[
Selling\ Price=\frac{Cost}{1-Desired\ Margin}
\]

If you know the cost and desired markup:

\[
Selling\ Price=Cost\times(1+Markup)
\]

Example: Product cost is ₱800, and the target margin is 20%.

\[
Selling\ Price=\frac{800}{1-0.20}
\]

\[
\boxed{Selling\ Price=₱1,000}
\]

Notice that a 20% target margin requires a 25% markup on the original cost.

## 7.8 Break-Even Analysis

Now let's introduce a very important business concept.

**Break-even point** is the sales volume at which Total Revenue equals Total Costs.

At break-even:

\[
Profit=0
\]

Meaning, the company is neither earning profit nor experiencing a loss.

### A. Break-Even Quantity

**Formula:**

\[
BEQ=\frac{Fixed\ Costs}{CM/unit}
\]

Where:

- BEQ = Break-even quantity (units)
- Fixed Costs = Total relevant fixed costs (₱)
- CM/unit = Contribution margin per unit (₱/unit)

Example:

| Description | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Fixed costs | ₱1,200,000 |
| Selling price | ₱1,000/unit |
| Variable cost | ₱600/unit |
| Contribution margin | ₱400/unit |

Step 1: Calculate contribution margin.

\[
CM=1,000-600=₱400
\]

Step 2: Calculate break-even quantity.

\[
BEQ=\frac{1,200,000}{400}
\]

\[
\boxed{BEQ=3,000\ units}
\]

Interpretation:

The company must sell at least 3,000 units to cover its relevant fixed and variable costs.

- Below 3,000 units = Loss
- At 3,000 units = Break-even
- Above 3,000 units = Profit, assuming the model's assumptions hold

### B. Break-Even Revenue

**Formula:**

\[
BE\ Revenue=\frac{Fixed\ Costs}{CM\ Ratio}
\]

Using the same example:

\[
CM\ Ratio=\frac{400}{1,000}=40\%
\]

\[
BE\ Revenue=\frac{1,200,000}{0.40}
\]

\[
\boxed{BE\ Revenue=₱3,000,000}
\]

The company needs ₱3 million in sales revenue to break even.

### Break-Even Assumptions

This calculation assumes:

1. Selling price per unit remains constant.
2. Variable cost per unit remains constant.
3. Fixed costs remain constant within the relevant operating range.
4. All produced units considered in the calculation are sold.
5. For multiple products, the sales mix remains stable if using a weighted-average contribution margin.

If these assumptions change, the break-even point also changes.

### 7.9 Formula Summary

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Gross Profit | Revenue − COGS |
| Gross Profit Margin | GP ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| Operating Profit | GP − Operating Expenses |
| Operating Profit Margin | Operating Profit ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| Net Profit | Total Revenue − All applicable expenses |
| Net Profit Margin | Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| Contribution Margin/unit | Selling Price − Variable Cost/unit |
| Contribution Margin Ratio | Total CM ÷ Revenue × 100 |
| Markup | Profit ÷ Cost × 100 |
| Margin | Profit ÷ Selling Price × 100 |
| Break-even Quantity | Fixed Costs ÷ CM/unit |
| Break-even Revenue | Fixed Costs ÷ CM Ratio |

**Important accounting reminder:** Always use consistent definitions of revenue and costs. For example, don't compare revenue net of discounts against a COGS calculation that includes a different sales scope or period. Ensure that costs are assigned to the same products, quantities, accounting period, and unit of measurement. Otherwise, the resulting margin can be misleading.

# 8. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's put everything together using a realistic manufacturing scenario.

## 8.1 Business Scenario: Manufacturing Company

Imagine a company manufacturing three electronic components.

The company sells Products A, B, and C.

Each product has a different selling price, manufacturing cost, and demand volume.

The monthly sales plan is:

| Parameter | Product A | Product B | Product C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Selling price/unit | ₱1,000 | ₱1,500 | ₱2,000 |
| COGS/unit | ₱700 | ₱900 | ₱1,400 |
| Monthly sales volume | 10,000 | 4,000 | 2,000 |

Our objective is to determine which product generates the highest revenue and gross profit, and which has the highest gross margin.

## 8.2 Step 1: Calculate Revenue

Formula:

\[
Revenue=Selling\ Price\times Sales\ Volume
\]

Product A:

\[
1,000\times10,000=₱10,000,000
\]

Product B:

\[
1,500\times4,000=₱6,000,000
\]

Product C:

\[
2,000\times2,000=₱4,000,000
\]

## 8.3 Step 2: Calculate COGS

Formula:

\[
COGS=COGS/unit\times Units\ Sold
\]

Product A:

\[
700\times10,000=₱7,000,000
\]

Product B:

\[
900\times4,000=₱3,600,000
\]

Product C:

\[
1,400\times2,000=₱2,800,000
\]

## 8.4 Step 3: Calculate Gross Profit

Formula:

\[
GP=Revenue-COGS
\]

Product A:

\[
10M-7M=₱3M
\]

Product B:

\[
6M-3.6M=₱2.4M
\]

Product C:

\[
4M-2.8M=₱1.2M
\]

## 8.5 Step 4: Calculate Gross Profit Margin

Formula:

\[
GPM=\frac{GP}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Product A:

\[
\frac{3M}{10M}\times100=30\%
\]

Product B:

\[
\frac{2.4M}{6M}\times100=40\%
\]

Product C:

\[
\frac{1.2M}{4M}\times100=30\%
\]

## 8.6 Consolidated Product Profitability

**MONTHLY FINANCIAL PERFORMANCE**

| Metric | Product A | Product B | Product C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Units sold | 10,000 | 4,000 | 2,000 |
| Revenue | ₱10M | ₱6M | ₱4M |
| COGS | ₱7M | ₱3.6M | ₱2.8M |
| Gross Profit | ₱3M | ₱2.4M | ₱1.2M |
| Gross Margin | 30% | 40% | 30% |

### What do the results tell us?

**Product A: Highest revenue and total gross profit**

- Generates ₱10 million revenue.
- Generates ₱3 million gross profit.
- Gross margin is 30%.

Product A is the company's largest revenue contributor and gross profit contributor.

**Product B: Highest gross margin**

- Generates ₱6 million revenue.
- Generates ₱2.4 million gross profit.
- Gross margin is 40%.

Product B generates a higher percentage of gross profit per peso of sales.

**Product C: Lowest total gross profit**

- Generates ₱4 million revenue.
- Generates ₱1.2 million gross profit.
- Gross margin is 30%.

Product C has the same margin as Product A but generates less total gross profit because of its lower sales volume.

### Important observation

The highest-margin product is not necessarily the product generating the highest total profit.

Likewise, the highest-volume product is not necessarily the product with the highest profitability percentage.

This is why S&OP must consider both volume and margin.

## 8.7 Product Mix Analysis

Now let's make the scenario more interesting.

Suppose Sales wants to increase Product B's monthly volume by 2,000 units while reducing Product A's volume by 2,000 units.

Assume the company has sufficient capacity and demand for this change.

Original mix:

- Product A = 10,000 units
- Product B = 4,000 units
- Product C = 2,000 units

New mix:

- Product A = 8,000 units
- Product B = 6,000 units
- Product C = 2,000 units

Let's calculate the new results.

| Metric | Original | New mix |
|---|---:|---:|
| Product A revenue | ₱10M | ₱8M |
| Product B revenue | ₱6M | ₱9M |
| Product C revenue | ₱4M | ₱4M |
| **Total Revenue** | **₱20M** | **₱21M** |
| Product A gross profit | ₱3M | ₱2.4M |
| Product B gross profit | ₱2.4M | ₱3.6M |
| Product C gross profit | ₱1.2M | ₱1.2M |
| **Total Gross Profit** | **₱6.6M** | **₱7.2M** |

Gross profit improvement:

\[
7.2M-6.6M=₱600,000
\]

Gross profit increase percentage:

\[
\frac{600,000}{6,600,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{9.09\%}
\]

Original gross margin:

\[
\frac{6.6M}{20M}\times100=33\%
\]

New gross margin:

\[
\frac{7.2M}{21M}\times100=34.29\%
\]

The new product mix generated:

- ₱1 million additional revenue.
- ₱600,000 additional gross profit.
- Approximately 1.29 percentage points higher gross margin.

### Business interpretation

This is a good example of how product mix decisions can improve profitability without simply increasing total production volume.

However, before recommending this change, an S&OP analyst must validate:

1. Is there sufficient customer demand for Product B?
2. Does Product B require more machine hours?
3. Are raw materials available?
4. Will the change affect delivery commitments for Product A?
5. Does Product B require additional labor or specialized equipment?

A theoretically better product mix may not be operationally feasible.

# 9. Profitability in S&OP Decisions

This is where the topic becomes particularly important for your actual S&OP work.

An S&OP analyst must connect commercial opportunities with operational capabilities and financial outcomes.

## 9.1 How Production and Demand Decisions Influence Profit

Consider the following situations:

| S&OP decision | Possible profitability impact |
|---|---|
| Increase demand forecast | More potential revenue and contribution, but requires supply feasibility |
| Prioritize high-margin products | Potentially improves total profit |
| Increase production volume | May improve fixed-cost absorption |
| Run overtime | May increase output but also labor costs |
| Use subcontracting | May protect sales but introduce additional costs |
| Expedite raw materials | May prevent lost sales but increase procurement and freight costs |
| Reduce production yield losses | Reduces cost per good unit |
| Build excess inventory | May increase holding costs and obsolescence risk |
| Accept low-margin orders | May increase revenue but dilute overall margins |

The important principle is that every major demand or supply decision has financial consequences.

## 9.2 How an S&OP Analyst Evaluates Supply Alternatives

Let's use another manufacturing situation.

The company receives additional demand for 5,000 units.

However, normal production capacity is insufficient.

Management has three options.

**Supply alternatives**

**Option A: Normal Production**

Produce within regular working hours. However, available capacity only supports 3,000 additional units.

**Option B: Overtime Production**

Use overtime to produce the remaining units. Higher labor costs but greater control over production.

**Option C: Subcontracting**

Outsource the remaining units. Potentially faster, but external manufacturing costs are higher.

Let's quantify the decision.

Assumptions:

- Additional customer demand = 5,000 units
- Selling price = ₱1,000/unit
- Normal production variable cost = ₱600/unit
- Overtime production variable cost = ₱750/unit
- Subcontracting cost = ₱850/unit
- Normal capacity available = 3,000 units

Assume the 3,000 regular units cost ₱600 each.

| Supply source | Units | Cost/unit | Total cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Normal production | 3,000 | ₱600 | ₱1,800,000 |
| Overtime | 2,000 | ₱750 | ₱1,500,000 |
| **Total** | **5,000** | | **₱3,300,000** |

Revenue:

\[
5,000\times1,000=₱5,000,000
\]

Contribution before other relevant costs:

\[
5,000,000-3,300,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱1,700,000}
\]

Now compare the remaining 2,000 units under subcontracting.

| Metric | Overtime | Subcontracting |
|---|---:|---:|
| Regular production cost | ₱1.8M | ₱1.8M |
| Remaining 2,000 units cost | ₱1.5M | ₱1.7M |
| Total cost | ₱3.3M | ₱3.5M |
| Total revenue | ₱5M | ₱5M |
| Contribution before other costs | ₱1.7M | ₱1.5M |

In this scenario, overtime is financially preferable by ₱200,000.

But what if overtime causes:

- Higher defect rates?
- Equipment downtime?
- Worker fatigue?
- Delayed production of other high-margin products?

Then the financial comparison must include those potential consequences.

**The lowest unit cost is not necessarily the best overall business decision.**

## 9.3 The Role of Finance, Sales, and Operations

Profitability is not owned by one department.

It requires collaboration.

| Department | Main responsibility | Profitability contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Demand, pricing, customer requirements | Protect selling price and profitable demand |
| Operations | Production, capacity, yield, efficiency | Control manufacturing costs and resource utilization |
| Supply Chain | Materials, suppliers, inventory, logistics | Control procurement and supply costs |
| Finance | Cost validation, margins, budgets, financial reporting | Validate financial impact and profitability |
| S&OP | Cross-functional alignment and decision support | Balance demand, supply, and financial objectives |

The S&OP analyst acts as a bridge between these functions.

Hindi sapat na sabihin:

"Operations can produce the required volume."

You also need to understand:

- What will it cost?
- What revenue will it generate?
- What profit will it contribute?
- What other products might be displaced?
- What risks are associated with the proposed plan?

## 9.4 Presenting Profitability Risks During Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where cross-functional teams reconcile the demand, supply, and financial plans.

An S&OP analyst may present a financial comparison like this:

| Financial metric | Current plan | Proposed plan |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱20M | ₱21M |
| COGS | ₱13.4M | ₱13.8M |
| Gross Profit | ₱6.6M | ₱7.2M |
| Gross Margin | 33% | 34.29% |

Then identify the main risks:

- Additional raw material requirements.
- Capacity constraints.
- Potential overtime costs.
- Higher procurement lead times.
- Risk of excess inventory if demand does not materialize.

The objective is to provide management with a complete picture rather than simply reporting the higher revenue.

## 9.5 Presenting Profitability During Executive S&OP

Executive S&OP focuses on decisions requiring management alignment or approval.

A good financial presentation should answer:

1. What is the current profitability outlook?
2. What is the gap against the financial target?
3. What is causing the gap?
4. What alternatives are available?
5. What are the financial consequences of each alternative?
6. What decision or approval is needed?

For example:

**Executive recommendation:**

"Based on the proposed demand mix, increasing Product B allocation is expected to improve monthly gross profit by ₱600,000 and gross margin from 33% to 34.29%. However, the proposal requires validation of production capacity and raw material availability before implementation."

That is a much stronger business recommendation than simply saying:

"Product B has a higher margin, so we should produce more Product B."

The first statement connects financial improvement with operational feasibility.

# 10. Business Perspective: How an S&OP Analyst Thinks

Let's summarize how you should approach profitability when analyzing a business situation.

A professional S&OP analyst should not look at only one financial metric.

Consider these questions:

| Business situation | What should you investigate? |
|---|---|
| Revenue increased but profit decreased | Did costs increase faster than revenue? |
| Gross margin decreased | Did raw material prices, yield, or product mix change? |
| Sales volume increased | Did additional production require overtime or subcontracting? |
| Product B has higher margin | Is there sufficient demand and production capacity? |
| Customer requests a discount | Will the additional volume compensate for the lower selling price? |
| Inventory is increasing | Is the company producing ahead of demand and incurring carrying costs? |
| Actual profit is below budget | Is the problem volume, price, cost, or product mix? |

## 10.1 The Four Main Drivers of Profitability

A useful way to organize your analysis is:

1. **Volume:** How many units are we selling?
2. **Price:** How much revenue are we generating per unit?
3. **Cost:** How much does it cost to produce and deliver each unit?
4. **Mix:** Which products and customers contribute to our total profitability?

These four drivers are closely connected to demand planning, supply planning, and financial planning.

When actual profitability differs from the plan, you can investigate these drivers to identify the reason.

## 10.2 Profitability Analysis Framework

**S&OP Profitability Evaluation**

1. **Demand Assessment:** What products and quantities are customers expected to purchase?
2. **Revenue Assessment:** What selling prices and total revenue are expected?
3. **Cost Assessment:** What materials, labor, manufacturing, and logistics costs are required?
4. **Profitability Assessment:** What are the expected gross profit, contribution, and operating profit?
5. **Supply Feasibility:** Can Operations deliver the proposed demand within available capacity and resources?
6. **Financial Comparison:** Which supply alternative provides the best overall financial outcome?
7. **Management Recommendation:** What decision should management make, and what are the financial and operational risks?

# 11. Key Takeaways

Before moving to the exercises, make sure you understand these concepts.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Value generated from selling products or services |
| Cost | Resources consumed to generate revenue |
| Profit | Financial gain after applicable costs and expenses |
| Cash Flow | Actual movement of cash into and out of the business |
| Gross Profit | Revenue less COGS |
| Operating Profit | Gross profit less operating expenses |
| Net Profit | Profit after all applicable expenses and taxes |
| Gross Margin | Percentage of revenue remaining after COGS |
| Operating Margin | Percentage of revenue remaining after operating expenses |
| Net Margin | Percentage of revenue retained as net profit |
| Contribution Margin | Revenue less variable costs |
| Markup | Profit relative to cost |
| Margin | Profit relative to selling price |
| Break-even | Point where total revenue equals total costs |
| Product Mix | Combination of products sold |
| Profit Variance | Difference between actual and planned profit |

### Five principles to remember

1. Higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit.
2. Higher sales volume does not automatically mean better profitability.
3. High-margin products do not necessarily generate the highest total profit.
4. Production efficiency, capacity, yield, and product mix influence financial performance.
5. S&OP decisions should balance demand, supply feasibility, and financial objectives.

**The ultimate objective is profitable and sustainable business growth, not simply maximizing sales or production volume.**
