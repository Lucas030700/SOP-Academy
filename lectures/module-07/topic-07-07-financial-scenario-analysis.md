# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

### 1.1 What is Financial Scenario Analysis?

Imagine that you are working as an S&OP Analyst in a manufacturing company.

Your company currently expects to sell 10,000 units of Product A and 6,000 units of Product B next month.

However, several things could happen:

- Customer demand might increase by 20%.
- Customer demand might decrease by 15%.
- Raw material prices might increase by 10%.
- A production machine might become unavailable.
- The company might need overtime to meet additional demand.
- The company might need to build more inventory to protect against supply disruptions.

The question is:

**How will these different situations affect the company's financial performance?**

Hindi sapat na malaman lang natin kung kaya bang mag-produce ng additional units.

We also need to understand:

- How much additional revenue can we generate?
- How much additional production cost will we incur?
- Will our profit increase or decrease?
- How much additional inventory investment will be required?
- Will we have enough working capital?
- Is the additional business financially worth pursuing?

This is where Financial Scenario Analysis becomes important.

**Technical Definition:**

Financial Scenario Analysis is a systematic process of evaluating the financial consequences of different possible business conditions by changing operational and financial assumptions and comparing their resulting financial outcomes.

In simpler terms:

We create different possible business situations, calculate their financial consequences, and evaluate which situation produces the most appropriate business outcome.

### 1.2 Why is it important?

Companies operate in uncertain environments.

Hindi natin laging alam kung:

- Exactly how much customers will purchase.
- Whether suppliers will maintain their prices.
- Whether production capacity will remain available.
- Whether material shortages will occur.
- Whether the company will achieve its revenue targets.

Instead of preparing only one financial forecast, companies evaluate several possible outcomes.

This allows management to prepare decisions before problems actually happen.

For example:

| Business situation | Possible financial consequence |
|---|---|
| Demand increases | Higher revenue, but additional production costs |
| Demand decreases | Lower revenue and potential excess inventory |
| Material prices increase | Higher manufacturing costs and lower margins |
| Production capacity decreases | Potential lost sales and higher overtime costs |
| Supplier lead time increases | Higher safety stock and working capital |
| Production efficiency improves | Lower unit cost and potentially higher profitability |

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

### 2.1 Understanding scenarios through a simple example

Imagine that you own a small manufacturing business producing electric fans.

Your normal monthly business conditions are:

- Expected sales: 1,000 units
- Selling price: ₱2,000 per unit
- Manufacturing cost: ₱1,200 per unit
- Monthly operating expenses: ₱300,000

Your expected revenue is:

\[
1,000 \times ₱2,000 = ₱2,000,000
\]

Your manufacturing cost for sold units is:

\[
1,000 \times ₱1,200 = ₱1,200,000
\]

Your gross profit is:

\[
₱2,000,000-₱1,200,000=₱800,000
\]

Your operating profit is:

\[
₱800,000-₱300,000=₱500,000
\]

Now, what happens if the market changes?

Instead of 1,000 units, customers might purchase 1,300 units.

Alternatively, customers might purchase only 700 units.

You now have three possible situations:

### Scenario 1: Base Case

**1,000 units**

Expected demand, normal production conditions, and normal material prices.

### Scenario 2: Upside Case

**1,300 units**

Higher customer demand and additional production requirements.

### Scenario 3: Downside Case

**700 units**

Lower customer demand and possible excess inventory.

The important part is that each scenario produces a different financial result.

The company needs to understand these results before deciding how much to produce, how much inventory to maintain, and how to allocate its resources.

### 2.2 The most important principle

**Different operational decisions produce different financial consequences.**

For example:

- Increasing production does not automatically increase revenue.
- Increasing sales volume does not automatically increase profit.
- Lower production does not automatically mean lower total cost.
- Higher inventory does not automatically mean better customer service.
- Higher revenue does not automatically mean better financial performance.

This is one of the most important things to understand in financial scenario analysis.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

## 3.1 Financial Forecasting vs Financial Reconciliation vs Financial Scenario Analysis

These three concepts are related, but they serve different purposes.

| Concept | Main question | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Financial Forecasting | What financial results do we currently expect? | Estimate future financial performance |
| Financial Reconciliation | Does our operational plan align with our financial targets? | Identify and resolve financial gaps |
| Financial Scenario Analysis | What happens financially if business conditions change? | Evaluate alternative business situations |

Let's use a manufacturing example.

Suppose the company has an annual revenue target of ₱50 million.

Based on current demand and sales information, the company estimates that it will generate ₱46 million.

The forecast indicates a potential revenue shortfall of ₱4 million.

**Financial Forecasting**

Based on current demand and sales information, the company estimates that it will generate ₱46 million.

The forecast indicates a potential revenue shortfall of ₱4 million.

**Financial Reconciliation**

Finance and Operations investigate the ₱4 million gap.

Possible actions include:

- Increasing sales volume.
- Improving product mix.
- Increasing selling prices.
- Improving production capacity.
- Reducing operational costs.

They determine which adjustments can close the gap.

**Financial Scenario Analysis**

Management evaluates different alternatives:

- What if sales volume increases by 10%?
- What if material costs increase by 5%?
- What if production capacity decreases by 15%?
- What if the company introduces a new product?

Each alternative is evaluated financially.

**Remember the distinction:** Forecasting estimates. Reconciliation aligns. Scenario analysis evaluates alternatives.

## 3.2 Relationship Between Operational Scenarios and Financial Outcomes

This is the foundation of the entire topic.

Operational scenarios describe what might happen in the business.

Financial scenarios describe the financial consequences of those operational changes.

Consider this relationship:

**Operational Changes**  
Demand, production, procurement, capacity, inventory

↓  

**Financial Drivers**  
Sales volume, selling price, unit cost, overhead, inventory investment

↓  

**Financial Outcomes**  
Revenue, COGS, gross profit, operating profit, working capital, cash flow

↓  

**Management Decisions**  
Production adjustments, procurement actions, inventory strategies, resource allocation

For example:

An increase in demand may result in:

1. Higher sales volume.
2. Higher revenue.
3. Higher production requirements.
4. Additional material consumption.
5. Higher manufacturing costs.
6. Additional inventory investment.
7. Potentially higher profit, provided that the additional revenue exceeds the additional costs.

Notice that an operational change can affect several financial variables simultaneously.

That is why scenario analysis must consider the complete financial picture.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Financial Scenario Components

When developing financial scenarios, we need to understand the major financial components.

### A. Revenue and Sales Volume

Revenue represents the financial value of products sold.

\[
Revenue=Sales\ Quantity\times Selling\ Price
\]

Important variables:

- Sales volume: Number of units sold.
- Selling price: Amount charged per unit.
- Product mix: Distribution of sales across different products.

Example:

| Product | Sales volume | Selling price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Product A | 10,000 | ₱200 | ₱2,000,000 |
| Product B | 6,000 | ₱300 | ₱1,800,000 |
| **Total** | **16,000** | | **₱3,800,000** |

Even if total sales volume remains unchanged, revenue can change because of product mix.

For example, selling more of Product B instead of Product A increases average revenue per unit because Product B has a higher selling price.

However, higher revenue per unit does not necessarily mean higher profitability. We also need to consider the product's manufacturing cost.

### B. Selling Price and Product Mix

There are three important revenue drivers:

1. Volume: How many units are sold?
2. Price: How much is charged per unit?
3. Mix: Which products are sold?

Example:

A company sells two products.

- Product A: ₱200 selling price.
- Product B: ₱300 selling price.

If demand shifts from Product A toward Product B, total revenue may increase even without an increase in total units sold.

However, if Product B has significantly higher manufacturing costs, the resulting profit improvement might be smaller than expected.

This is why Finance and Sales should evaluate both revenue and margin.

### C. Production and Procurement Costs

Production costs change depending on the required production volume and manufacturing conditions.

Common cost components include:

| Cost component | Example |
|---|---|
| Direct materials | Raw materials, chemicals, components |
| Direct labor | Operators and production workers |
| Variable overhead | Utilities and consumables that vary with activity |
| Fixed manufacturing overhead | Factory depreciation and fixed facility costs |
| Procurement costs | Purchasing, transportation, and supplier-related costs |

Example:

If production increases from 10,000 to 12,000 units, material consumption normally increases.

If raw material prices also increase, total manufacturing costs can increase faster than production volume alone.

This is particularly important when evaluating upside scenarios.

### D. Labor, Overhead, and Operating Expenses

Additional demand may require:

- Overtime.
- Additional shifts.
- Temporary workers.
- Additional maintenance.
- Additional utilities.
- Additional logistics activities.

Not all costs increase at the same rate.

For example:

| Cost | Typical behavior |
|---|---|
| Raw material | Variable |
| Production consumables | Often variable |
| Regular factory rent | Fixed |
| Factory depreciation | Generally fixed |
| Overtime | Variable or step-variable |
| Additional production shift | Step-fixed |
| Sales commissions | Often variable |
| Administrative salaries | Generally fixed within a relevant range |

An important concept here is **step-fixed cost**.

For example, a factory can produce 10,000 units using one shift.

But producing 12,000 units may require a second shift.

The additional shift creates a sudden increase in labor and overhead costs.

This means costs do not always increase proportionally with production volume.

### E. Inventory Investment and Working Capital

Higher production may require additional inventory.

Inventory is a financial investment because company funds are tied up in:

- Raw materials.
- Work-in-process (WIP).
- Finished goods.

For example:

A company produces 10,000 units but sells only 8,000 units.

The remaining 2,000 units become finished goods inventory.

Important distinction:

**Production is not the same as sales.**

Unsold products do not automatically generate revenue.

They represent inventory investment until they are sold.

Working capital also changes because the company may need more funds to finance:

- Inventory.
- Accounts receivable.
- Supplier payments.
- Operational requirements.

### F. Profitability and Cash Flow

Finally, we evaluate whether the scenario improves financial performance.

Important measurements include:

| Financial metric | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Total sales value |
| Gross profit | Profit after cost of goods sold |
| Gross margin | Gross profit relative to revenue |
| Contribution margin | Revenue remaining after variable costs |
| Operating profit | Profit after operating expenses |
| Inventory value | Capital invested in inventory |
| Working capital | Short-term operating financial position |
| Cash flow | Actual movement of cash |

A scenario may improve profitability while creating additional cash requirements.

For example, a company may generate more profit because of increased sales, but it might need to purchase raw materials several months before collecting payment from customers.

This creates a timing difference between profitability and cash availability.

## 4.2 Developing Financial Scenarios

The three standard scenarios are:

### 1. Base Case

The Base Case represents the company's expected business conditions.

It usually uses:

- Current demand forecast.
- Expected selling prices.
- Approved production plans.
- Expected material costs.
- Normal capacity assumptions.
- Existing inventory policies.

This becomes the reference scenario against which other alternatives are compared.

### 2. Upside Case

The Upside Case represents a more favorable business condition.

Examples:

- Demand exceeds expectations.
- Additional customer orders are received.
- Product mix shifts toward higher-margin products.
- Production efficiency improves.
- Material availability improves.

However, an upside scenario may also create challenges.

For example, higher demand might require overtime, additional raw materials, or external subcontracting.

Therefore, upside does not automatically mean higher profit.

### 3. Downside Case

The Downside Case represents unfavorable business conditions.

Examples:

- Demand decreases.
- A major customer cancels orders.
- Material prices increase.
- A supplier experiences disruption.
- Production capacity becomes unavailable.
- Finished goods inventory accumulates.

The purpose is to understand the potential financial exposure and identify actions that can reduce the impact.

### Scenario Comparison

| Variable | Base | Upside | Downside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | Expected | Higher | Lower |
| Selling price | Normal | Normal or higher | Normal or lower |
| Material cost | Expected | May increase | May increase |
| Production | Normal | Higher | Adjusted downward |
| Inventory | Planned | Additional stock may be needed | Excess stock risk |
| Profit | Expected | Potentially higher | Potentially lower |
| Financial risk | Normal | Capacity and cost exposure | Demand and inventory exposure |

### 4.3 Understanding Scenario Assumptions

A scenario is only as reliable as its assumptions.

Suppose the upside scenario assumes:

- Demand increases by 20%.
- Selling price remains unchanged.
- Material cost increases by 5%.
- Production capacity can support the increase.

These are assumptions, not guaranteed outcomes.

The analyst should identify:

1. What is changing?
2. Why is it changing?
3. What information supports the assumption?
4. Which other variables will be affected?
5. What risks could invalidate the scenario?

A good financial scenario should be traceable to operational assumptions.

# 5. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

This section is particularly important because it converts the concepts into actual financial analysis.

We will build the formulas one by one before applying them to a manufacturing case.

## 5.1 Scenario Revenue

The basic formula is:

\[
Revenue=Q\times P
\]

Where:

- \(Q\) = Sales quantity (units).
- \(P\) = Selling price (₱/unit).

For multiple products:

\[
Total\ Revenue=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Q_i\times P_i)
\]

Example:

| Product | Sales quantity | Price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10,000 | ₱200 | ₱2,000,000 |
| B | 6,000 | ₱300 | ₱1,800,000 |
| **Total** | | | **₱3,800,000** |

Interpretation:

The company generates ₱3.8 million from the expected sales volume.

Important: Use sales quantity, not production quantity, when calculating sales revenue.

## 5.2 Variable and Fixed Costs

Total cost can be expressed as:

\[
Total\ Cost=Variable\ Cost+Fixed\ Cost
\]

Variable cost:

\[
VC=Q\times VCU
\]

Where:

- \(Q\) = Relevant production or sales quantity.
- \(VCU\) = Variable cost per unit.

Fixed cost:

\[
FC=Constant\ Cost
\]

within the relevant operating range and time period.

Example:

A factory produces 10,000 units.

- Variable cost per unit = ₱100.
- Monthly fixed manufacturing overhead = ₱400,000.

Variable cost:

\[
10,000\times ₱100=₱1,000,000
\]

Total manufacturing cost:

\[
₱1,000,000+₱400,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱1,400,000}
\]

Average manufacturing cost per unit:

\[
\frac{₱1,400,000}{10,000}=₱140
\]

Notice that average cost per unit includes both variable and fixed costs.

If production increases while fixed costs remain unchanged, average fixed cost per unit decreases.

However, this only holds within the relevant capacity range.

## 5.3 Contribution Margin

Contribution Margin measures how much revenue remains after deducting variable costs.

\[
CM=Revenue-Total\ Variable\ Costs
\]

Contribution Margin Ratio:

\[
CMR=\frac{CM}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Example:

- Revenue = ₱2,000,000.
- Variable costs = ₱1,200,000.

\[
CM=₱2,000,000-₱1,200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{CM=₱800,000}
\]

Contribution Margin Ratio:

\[
CMR=\frac{800,000}{2,000,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CMR=40\%}
\]

Interpretation:

For every ₱100 of revenue, ₱40 remains after variable costs to cover fixed costs and generate profit.

## 5.4 Gross Profit and Operating Profit

Gross Profit:

\[
GP=Revenue-COGS
\]

Gross Margin:

\[
GM=\frac{GP}{Revenue}\times100
\]

Operating Profit:

\[
OP=GP-OPEX
\]

Where:

- COGS = Cost of Goods Sold.
- OPEX = Operating Expenses.

Example:

| Component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱2,000,000 |
| COGS | ₱1,200,000 |
| Gross Profit | ₱800,000 |
| Operating Expenses | ₱300,000 |
| **Operating Profit** | **₱500,000** |

Gross Margin:

\[
\frac{800,000}{2,000,000}\times100=40\%
\]

Operating Margin:

\[
\frac{500,000}{2,000,000}\times100=25\%
\]

Important distinction:

Gross profit considers COGS, while operating profit also considers operating expenses.

## 5.5 Inventory Value and Carrying Cost

Inventory Value:

\[
Inventory\ Value=Inventory\ Quantity\times Unit\ Cost
\]

Example:

- Ending inventory = 2,000 units.
- Unit inventory cost = ₱150.

\[
2,000\times ₱150
\]

\[
\boxed{₱300,000}
\]

Inventory Carrying Cost:

\[
Annual\ Carrying\ Cost=Average\ Inventory\ Value\times Carrying\ Rate
\]

Example:

- Average inventory value = ₱300,000.
- Annual carrying rate = 20%.

\[
₱300,000\times20\%
\]

\[
\boxed{₱60,000/year}
\]

Carrying cost can include:

- Cost of capital.
- Storage.
- Insurance.
- Obsolescence.
- Inventory handling.
- Risk of deterioration.

The 20% rate is an illustrative assumption, not a universal industry standard.

For monthly analysis, if the inventory level is stable:

\[
Monthly\ Carrying\ Cost=
\frac{Annual\ Carrying\ Cost}{12}
\]

\[
=\frac{₱60,000}{12}=₱5,000
\]

## 5.6 Working Capital Impact

A simplified working capital formula is:

\[
Working\ Capital=Current\ Assets-Current\ Liabilities
\]

For operational analysis, we often examine:

\[
Operating\ NWC=AR+Inventory-AP
\]

Where:

- AR = Accounts Receivable.
- Inventory = Inventory value.
- AP = Accounts Payable.

Example:

| Component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Accounts Receivable | ₱500,000 |
| Inventory | ₱300,000 |
| Accounts Payable | ₱200,000 |
| **Operating NWC** | **₱600,000** |

Calculation:

\[
₱500,000+₱300,000-₱200,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱600,000}
\]

If inventory increases by ₱100,000 while everything else remains constant:

\[
New\ NWC=₱700,000
\]

The company now needs an additional ₱100,000 in operating working capital.

An increase in working capital generally represents additional cash tied up in operations.

## 5.7 Financial Variance Between Scenarios

We compare alternative scenarios using:

\[
Financial\ Variance=Scenario\ Value-Base\ Value
\]

Percentage Change:

\[
\%\ Change=
\frac{Scenario\ Value-Base\ Value}{Base\ Value}
\times100
\]

Example:

- Base operating profit = ₱500,000.
- Upside operating profit = ₱600,000.

Variance:

\[
₱600,000-₱500,000=₱100,000
\]

Percentage change:

\[
\frac{100,000}{500,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{20\%}
\]

The upside scenario generates 20% more operating profit.

## 5.8 ROI and Payback Period

Scenario analysis can also evaluate whether a proposed operational improvement is financially worthwhile.

Example:

A company invests ₱250,000 in an equipment improvement.

The improvement generates:

- Annual cost savings = ₱150,000.
- Annual additional operating costs = ₱0.
- Initial investment = ₱250,000.

Simple annual ROI:

\[
ROI=\frac{Annual\ Net\ Benefit}{Investment}\times100
\]

\[
ROI=\frac{150,000}{250,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{ROI=60\%}
\]

Payback Period:

\[
Payback=\frac{Initial\ Investment}{Annual\ Net\ Cash\ Savings}
\]

\[
=\frac{250,000}{150,000}
\]

\[
\boxed{1.67\ Years}
\]

Interpretation:

The company recovers its initial investment in approximately 1 year and 8 months.

Important limitations:

- Simple ROI does not consider the time value of money.
- Simple payback does not consider benefits after the payback period.
- For larger investments, companies may also evaluate NPV and IRR.

## 6.6 Step 4: Calculate COGS

This is an important accounting distinction.

We calculate the cost of units sold, not the cost of all units produced.

For this example:

\[
COGS=\sum[Sales\ Quantity\times Full\ Unit\ Cost]
\]

Where:

\[
Full\ Unit\ Cost=Variable\ Cost/Unit+OH/Unit
\]

Base Case:

Product A:

\[
10,000\times(110+20)=₱1,300,000
\]

Product B:

\[
6,000\times(180+30)=₱1,260,000
\]

Total COGS:

\[
\boxed{₱2,560,000}
\]

Comparison:

| Scenario | Variable cost of sold units | OH allocated to sold units | COGS |
|---|---:|---:|---:|

## 6.15 Avoiding Double-Counting Financial Impacts

This is an important technical consideration.

When preparing financial scenarios, the same financial impact can accidentally be counted more than once.

For example:

A company increases production by 1,000 units.

The additional production cost is ₱100,000.

However, some of those units remain unsold.

The company must not:

1. Record the full ₱100,000 production cost as current COGS.
2. Add the same ₱100,000 again as inventory investment.

That would double-count the production cost.

Instead:

\[
Production\ Cost=COGS+Ending\ Inventory
\]

For example:

- Total production cost = ₱100,000.
- Cost of units sold = ₱80,000.
- Ending inventory = ₱20,000.

Therefore:

\[
₱100,000=₱80,000+₱20,000
\]

Other common double-counting situations:

| Potential error | Correct approach |
|---|---|
| Counting production cost and COGS as separate total expenses | COGS is the portion of production cost associated with units sold |
| Including carrying cost in OPEX and adding it again separately | Include it only once in the financial model |
| Counting material cost inflation in both unit cost and a separate inflation adjustment | Apply inflation once |
| Counting sales revenue from produced but unsold units | Recognize revenue based on sales, not production |
| Counting overtime in both labor cost and a separate capacity adjustment | Identify the correct cost classification |
| Subtracting the full inventory increase from profit while also recording COGS | Separate income statement impact from cash and working capital impact |

**Golden Rule:** Every financial impact should have one clearly identified place in the financial model.

# 7. FINANCIAL SCENARIO EVALUATION

Now we move beyond calculations.

An S&OP Analyst should not simply prepare three tables and present them to management.

The analyst must interpret the results and identify which business drivers are creating the financial differences.

## 7.1 How to Compare Financial Alternatives

Let's return to our case study.

Which scenario is the best?

| Evaluation criteria | Best result |
|---|---|
| Highest revenue | Upside |
| Highest gross profit | Upside |
| Highest operating profit | Base |
| Highest contribution margin amount | Upside |
| Highest contribution margin ratio | Base |
| Lowest inventory investment | Base |
| Lowest operating NWC | Downside |

There is no single answer without understanding management's objectives and operational constraints.

For example:

If the company's priority is revenue growth, the upside scenario may be attractive.

If the priority is operating profitability, the Base Case currently performs better.

If the company is facing liquidity constraints, working capital requirements become particularly important.

The decision depends on the company's strategic objectives, financial targets, risks, and operational feasibility.

## 7.2 Identifying the Most Important Financial Drivers

Not every variable has the same financial impact.

Some variables have a significant effect on business performance.

Common financial drivers include:

- Sales volume.
- Selling price.
- Product mix.
- Material cost per unit.
- Labor cost per unit.
- Production efficiency.
- Fixed overhead.
- Inventory levels.
- Customer payment terms.
- Supplier payment terms.

Let's examine how these drivers affect performance.

| Driver changes | Potential financial effect |
|---|---|
| Selling price increases | Revenue and margin may improve |
| Sales volume increases | Revenue and contribution may increase |
| Material cost increases | Contribution and profit decrease |
| Product mix shifts to high-margin products | Profitability may improve |
| Production efficiency improves | Unit manufacturing cost may decrease |
| Inventory increases | Working capital and carrying cost may increase |
| Customer payment period increases | Accounts receivable and cash requirements may increase |
| Supplier payment period increases | Accounts payable may increase, reducing immediate working capital requirements |

An S&OP Analyst should identify which drivers are responsible for the largest changes in financial results.

## 7.3 Sensitivity Analysis

Scenario analysis evaluates different business situations.

Sensitivity analysis examines how changing a particular assumption affects the financial outcome.

For example:

What happens to operating profit if material cost increases by:

- 2%?
- 5%?
- 8%?
- 10%?

Instead of changing all assumptions simultaneously, we isolate one variable.

Example:

A product sells for ₱200.

Its variable cost is ₱110.

Its monthly sales volume is 10,000 units.

Contribution Margin:

\[
CM=(200-110)\times10,000
\]

\[
=₱900,000
\]

Now, material cost increases by 5%.

For simplicity, assume the entire variable cost increases by 5%.

New variable cost:

\[
₱110\times1.05=₱115.50
\]

New contribution margin:

\[
(200-115.50)\times10,000
\]

\[
=₱845,000
\]

Financial impact:

\[
₱845,000-₱900,000=-₱55,000
\]

A 5% increase in variable cost reduces monthly contribution margin by ₱55,000, assuming selling price and sales volume remain unchanged.

### Interactive Sensitivity Exercise

Try changing the material cost increase and observe its impact on contribution margin.

| Material cost increase | New contribution margin |
|---:|---:|
| 0% | ₱900,000 |
| 5% | ₱845,000 |
| 10% | ₱790,000 |
| 15% | ₱735,000 |
| 20% | ₱680,000 |

Assumes 10,000 units sold, ₱200 selling price, ₱110 initial variable cost per unit, and no changes in other costs.

This type of analysis helps management identify how sensitive profitability is to raw material price movements.

## 7.4 Risk and Opportunity Assessment

Financial scenario analysis should also identify potential business risks and opportunities.

| Scenario | Opportunity | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Base | Stable financial performance | Unexpected changes may create gaps |
| Upside | Higher sales and market share | Additional costs and capacity constraints |
| Downside | Opportunity to reduce production and protect cash | Lost sales, excess inventory, and lower profit |

The analyst should evaluate both the potential benefit and the exposure.

For example:

A customer requests 20% additional volume.

The company may accept the order, but the analyst should first investigate:

- Can production capacity support it?
- Are materials available?
- Will additional shifts be necessary?
- Will supplier prices change?
- Can the company deliver on time?
- Will the additional business generate sufficient margin?

## 7.5 Short-Term vs Long-Term Financial Consequences

Some decisions improve short-term financial results but create long-term disadvantages.

Example:

A company reduces inventory to release working capital.

Short-term benefits:

- Lower inventory investment.
- Lower carrying cost.
- Improved working capital.

Potential long-term risks:

- Increased stockout exposure.
- Production interruptions.
- Higher emergency procurement costs.
- Reduced customer service levels.

Another example:

A company accepts additional demand using overtime.

Short-term benefits:

- Higher production output.
- Additional revenue opportunity.
- Better customer fulfillment.

Long-term risks:

- Employee fatigue.
- Increased maintenance requirements.
- Higher operating costs.
- Potential quality issues.

An effective scenario analysis should evaluate both time horizons.

## 7.6 Why Highest Revenue Is Not Always the Best Scenario

Let's summarize our manufacturing case.

**Upside scenario revenue: ₱4.50M**

**Base scenario operating profit: ₱940K**

Upside revenue is higher by ₱700,000, but operating profit is lower by ₱11,000 compared with Base.

The additional volume does not generate enough incremental profit to offset the additional manufacturing and operating costs.

This is a critical professional insight:

**The objective of S&OP is not simply to maximize production or revenue. It is to develop a feasible business plan that supports the company's financial and strategic objectives.**

# 8. FINANCIAL SCENARIO ANALYSIS IN S&OP

Now let's connect everything to the actual S&OP process.

## 8.1 How an S&OP Analyst Prepares Financial Scenarios

An S&OP Analyst typically follows a structured process.

1. **Collect operational and financial data**

   Gather demand forecasts, sales plans, production capacity, inventory, procurement costs, and financial targets.

2. **Establish the Base Case**

   Create the expected business outlook using the latest approved assumptions.

3. **Identify major uncertainties**

   Determine which variables may change, such as demand, selling price, material cost, production capacity, and lead times.

4. **Develop alternative scenarios**

   Create Upside and Downside cases using clearly documented assumptions.

5. **Translate operational changes into financial impacts**

   Calculate revenue, costs, profitability, inventory investment, and working capital.

6. **Compare financial outcomes**

   Identify variances, risks, opportunities, and the most important financial drivers.

7. **Prepare management recommendations**

   Present alternatives and explain their financial and operational consequences.

## 8.2 Collaboration Between Finance and Operations

Financial scenario analysis is not exclusively a Finance activity.

Different departments contribute different information.

| Department | Main contribution |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer demand, sales volume, selling price |
| Demand Planning | Demand forecast and forecast assumptions |
| Supply Planning | Production requirements and capacity |
| Manufacturing | Labor, efficiency, output, overhead |
| Procurement | Material prices, supplier availability, lead times |
| Inventory Management | Inventory requirements and stock exposure |
| Finance | Cost validation, revenue, profit, working capital, financial targets |
| S&OP Analyst | Integration, scenario comparison, financial analysis, reporting |

The S&OP Analyst helps ensure that everyone is evaluating the same business situation.

For example:

Sales might propose a 20% increase in demand.

Supply Planning determines that only 15% additional production is feasible.

Procurement identifies a 5% material cost increase.

Finance calculates the resulting revenue and profitability.

The S&OP Analyst consolidates these inputs into a complete business scenario.

## 8.3 Presenting Scenarios During Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where functional teams review the proposed business plan and resolve major imbalances before executive review.

A financial scenario presentation should contain:

1. Current Base Case.
2. Upside and Downside scenarios.
3. Revenue comparison.
4. Cost and profitability comparison.
5. Inventory and working capital impact.
6. Operational constraints.
7. Risks and opportunities.
8. Proposed actions.

Example:

| Metric | Base | Upside | Downside |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱3.80M | ₱4.50M | ₱2.95M |
| Operating profit | ₱940K | ₱929K | ₱475K |
| Inventory | ₱170K | ₱181.5K | ₱187.5K |
| Working capital | ₱275K | ₱287K | ₱231K |

The analyst should explain not just the numbers, but why they changed.

For example:

"The Upside Case generates 18.42% higher revenue compared with Base. However, operating profit decreases by 1.17% because additional manufacturing costs and operating expenses offset the revenue improvement."

That is much more useful than simply showing a spreadsheet.

## 8.4 Presenting Scenarios During Executive S&OP

Executive S&OP focuses on management decisions.

Executives generally need answers to the following:

- Are we going to achieve our financial targets?
- Which scenario is financially attractive?
- What risks could prevent us from achieving the plan?
- What resources or investments are required?
- What decisions need executive approval?

The analyst should present the financial implications clearly.

For example:

**Management Recommendation**

Based on the illustrative case study, the Base Case produces the highest operating profit at ₱940,000.

The Upside Case increases revenue by 18.42%, but operating profit decreases by 1.17% because additional production and material costs offset the revenue improvement.

Before accepting the additional demand, management should evaluate:

- Whether selling prices can be improved.
- Whether material costs can be negotiated.
- Whether production efficiency can be improved.
- Whether additional capacity can be obtained without excessive overhead.

The Downside Case presents a significant profitability risk, with operating profit decreasing by 49.47% compared with Base.

Management should consider reducing production in line with demand and reviewing procurement commitments to avoid excess inventory.

This demonstrates how financial analysis supports actual business decisions.

## 8.5 How Management Selects the Appropriate Scenario

Management should not select a scenario based solely on its revenue or profit.

The selected business plan must consider:

| Evaluation area | Management consideration |
|---|---|
| Financial performance | Does it support revenue and profit targets? |
| Operational feasibility | Can production realistically deliver the plan? |
| Supply availability | Are materials available when needed? |
| Inventory | Are inventory levels appropriate? |
| Customer service | Can customer demand be fulfilled? |
| Working capital | Can the company finance the plan? |
| Risk | What happens if assumptions change? |
| Strategic alignment | Does the plan support long-term business objectives? |

Ultimately, management selects a plan that balances financial performance, operational capability, customer requirements, and business risk.

# 9. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Let's consolidate the most important lessons from Topic 7.7.

### 9.1 Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Financial Scenario Analysis | Evaluates financial consequences of different possible business conditions |
| Base Case | Represents expected business conditions |
| Upside Case | Represents favorable business conditions |
| Downside Case | Represents unfavorable business conditions |
| Sensitivity Analysis | Measures the impact of changing individual assumptions |
| Financial Driver | A variable that significantly influences financial performance |
| Financial Variance | Difference between financial results of two scenarios |

### 9.2 Important Formulas

| Financial metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Sales Quantity × Selling Price |
| Variable Cost | Quantity × Variable Cost per Unit |
| Total Cost | Variable Cost + Fixed Cost |
| Contribution Margin | Revenue − Variable Costs |
| Gross Profit | Revenue − COGS |
| Operating Profit | Gross Profit − OPEX |
| Gross Margin | Gross Profit / Revenue × 100 |
| CM Ratio | Contribution Margin / Revenue × 100 |
| Inventory Value | Inventory Quantity × Unit Cost |
| Carrying Cost | Average Inventory Value × Carrying Rate |
| Operating NWC | AR + Inventory − AP |
| Financial Variance | Scenario Value − Base Value |
| Percentage Change | Variance / Base Value × 100 |
| ROI | Annual Net Benefit / Investment × 100 |
| Payback Period | Initial Investment / Annual Net Cash Savings |

### 9.3 Professional Insights You Should Remember

1. Production does not automatically generate revenue. Revenue is associated with sales.
2. Higher demand may require additional costs and working capital.
3. Higher revenue does not automatically mean higher profit.
4. Lower demand can create excess inventory if production is not adjusted.
5. Profitability and cash flow are different financial measurements.
6. Scenario assumptions must be realistic, documented, and traceable.
7. Avoid double-counting financial impacts.
8. Sensitivity analysis helps identify the most important financial drivers.
9. Finance and Operations must collaborate to create financially and operationally feasible scenarios.
10. The purpose of S&OP is to support a balanced business plan, not simply maximize one financial metric.
