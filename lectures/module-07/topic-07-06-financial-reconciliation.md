# Module 7 – Topic 7.6: Financial Reconciliation

**Level 2: Technical Application | Manufacturing & S&OP Application**

**Main objective:** Learn how to connect the operational plans of a company with its financial targets, identify financial gaps, understand their operational causes, and develop an integrated business plan.

## 1. Topic Overview

### 1.1 What is Financial Reconciliation?

Imagine that a manufacturing company has prepared its operational plan for the next three months.

The Operations team says:

- We will produce 100,000 units.
- We have enough production capacity.
- We have sufficient raw materials.
- We can meet the expected customer demand.
- We expect inventory to increase by the end of the quarter.

Everything looks good operationally.

However, when Finance evaluates the same plan, they discover:

- Projected revenue is below the sales budget.
- Material costs are higher than expected.
- Manufacturing expenses are increasing.
- Inventory requires more working capital.
- Projected operating profit is below the financial target.

Here's the problem:

**The operational plan is feasible, but the financial results are not aligned with the company's objectives.**

This is where Financial Reconciliation becomes important.

**Financial Reconciliation** is the process of translating operational plans into financial results, comparing those results against financial targets, identifying gaps, and determining what adjustments are necessary to establish an integrated business plan.

In simple terms:

Operations answers:

*Can we produce and deliver what the business needs?*

Finance answers:

*What will happen to the company's revenue, costs, cash requirements, and profitability if we execute that plan?*

Financial Reconciliation connects these two questions.

### 1.2 Why is it important in S&OP?

S&OP is not simply about producing enough products to satisfy customer demand.

A company also needs to ensure that its plans support its financial objectives.

For example, increasing production might improve product availability, but it can also:

- Increase raw material purchases.
- Increase labor and manufacturing expenses.
- Increase finished goods inventory.
- Increase storage and carrying costs.
- Tie up additional cash in inventory.

Therefore, an operational decision can create both financial opportunities and financial risks.

Financial Reconciliation helps management understand these consequences before committing to the plan.

### 1.3 Operational Reconciliation vs. Financial Reconciliation

These two processes are related, but they answer different questions.

| Area | Operational Reconciliation | Financial Reconciliation |
|---|---|---|
| Main focus | Operational feasibility | Financial feasibility |
| Demand | Can demand be fulfilled? | What revenue will demand generate? |
| Production | Can we produce the required quantity? | What will production cost? |
| Materials | Are materials available? | How much will procurement cost? |
| Capacity | Is capacity sufficient? | What are the financial consequences of capacity constraints? |
| Inventory | Do we have the right inventory? | How much working capital is tied up? |
| Output | Agreed operational plan | Financially evaluated business plan |

**Important distinction:**

Operational Reconciliation determines whether the plan can work.

Financial Reconciliation determines what the plan means financially and whether its financial outcomes support business objectives.

Both are necessary.

### 1.4 Relationship Between Operational and Financial Plans

Let's establish the relationship between the four major planning components.

**The S&OP to Finance connection**

1. **Demand Plan:** Expected customer demand and sales volume.
2. **Supply Plan:** Production, procurement, capacity and inventory requirements.
3. **Financial Translation:** Revenue, material costs, labor, overhead, inventory value and working capital.
4. **Financial Reconciliation:** Compare financial forecast against budget and identify gaps.
5. **Integrated Business Plan:** Agreed operational actions and financial expectations.

The important principle is that financial results must come from the same assumptions used in the operational plan.

For example, Finance should not assume that the company will sell 120,000 units if the agreed demand plan only supports 100,000 units, unless there is a clearly identified additional sales opportunity.

Likewise, Operations should not plan to produce 120,000 units without considering the resulting inventory and financial consequences.

## 2. Layman's Explanation

### 2.1 Think of a manufacturing company as a household

Imagine that you have a monthly household budget.

Your plan is:

- Monthly income: ₱50,000
- Food expenses: ₱10,000
- Rent: ₱8,000
- Transportation: ₱5,000
- Utilities: ₱3,000
- Savings target: ₱10,000

You have planned how to spend your money.

However, something changes:

- Your transportation expenses increase.
- Your electricity bill becomes higher.
- Your income decreases because of fewer working hours.

Your original budget is no longer aligned with your actual financial situation.

You need to reconcile your original plan with your updated financial expectations.

The same principle applies to S&OP.

| Household | Manufacturing Company |
|---|---|
| Expected income | Projected sales revenue |
| Grocery expenses | Raw material costs |
| Electricity bill | Manufacturing overhead |
| Transportation | Logistics and distribution costs |
| Savings | Profit or cash generation objectives |
| Money tied up in purchases | Working capital tied up in inventory |

Financial Reconciliation is essentially the company's process of checking whether its operating decisions will produce the financial results it expects.

### 2.2 An important example

Suppose your company has a monthly sales budget of:

₱10 million.

However, based on the latest demand forecast, expected revenue is only:

₱9 million.

There is a ₱1 million gap.

Now, the company needs to investigate:

- Are customers ordering fewer products?
- Did the average selling price decrease?
- Did the product mix change?
- Are production constraints preventing shipments?
- Are materials unavailable?
- Is the sales forecast inaccurate?

Notice that the ₱1 million gap is not automatically a Finance problem.

It may originate from Sales, Operations, Procurement, or the market itself.

**Financial Reconciliation identifies the financial gap, while operational analysis helps explain why that gap exists.**

This distinction is fundamental to becoming an effective S&OP analyst.

## 3. Technical Explanation

### 3.1 The four important planning terms

Before discussing financial calculations, we need to understand four interconnected planning concepts.

| Planning component | Meaning | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| Demand Plan | Expected customer requirements | Forecasted sales volume |
| Supply Plan | Resources and activities needed to fulfill demand | Production and procurement quantities |
| Financial Forecast | Expected financial results based on current assumptions | Revenue, expenses and profit |
| Budget | Approved financial targets for a defined period | Planned revenue, costs and profitability |

Let's examine how they work together.

**A. Demand Plan**

The demand plan estimates how many products customers are expected to purchase.

Example:

- Product A: 5,000 units
- Product B: 3,000 units
- Product C: 2,000 units

Total forecasted demand = 10,000 units.

**B. Supply Plan**

The supply plan determines how the company will fulfill the forecast.

Example:

- Required production: 10,000 units
- Beginning inventory: 1,000 units
- Planned ending inventory: 500 units

Required production:

\[
10,000+500-1,000=9,500
\]

The company needs to manufacture 9,500 units, assuming no other inventory adjustments or losses.

**C. Financial Forecast**

The financial forecast translates the operational assumptions into monetary values.

Example:

- Expected sales: 10,000 units
- Average selling price: ₱1,000/unit

Projected revenue:

\[
10,000\times ₱1,000=₱10,000,000
\]

If production costs are ₱600 per unit, the production plan also provides the basis for estimating manufacturing costs.

**D. Budget**

The budget establishes the company's approved financial expectations.

Example:

- Revenue budget: ₱11 million
- Manufacturing cost budget: ₱6 million
- Gross profit budget: ₱5 million

Finance can now compare the latest forecast against these targets.

### 3.2 Why operational plans must be translated into financial results

Operational quantities alone cannot fully explain business performance.

Consider these examples:

| Operational information | Financial meaning |
|---|---|
| 10,000 units sold | Revenue |
| 9,500 units produced | Manufacturing cost and inventory movement |
| 20,000 kg of raw materials purchased | Procurement expenditure and inventory |
| 500 additional finished goods | Increased inventory value |
| 10% overtime increase | Higher labor expense |
| Production downtime | Potential lost output and additional costs |

This is called **financial translation**.

The purpose is to convert operational measurements into financial measurements that management can use for decision-making.

One important point: production cost, procurement expenditure, and inventory valuation are not automatically the same financial expense. Accounting treatment and inventory movements determine when costs appear in the income statement.

## 4. Detailed Discussion

Now let's move from understanding the concept to how Financial Reconciliation actually works in a manufacturing business.

### 4.1 Connecting Operational Plans to Financial Plans

There are five major operational areas that need financial translation.

#### A. Sales Forecast → Projected Revenue

Sales forecast tells us how many units the company expects to sell.

Finance translates those quantities into expected revenue using selling prices.

\[
\text{Revenue}=\text{Sales Volume}\times\text{Selling Price}
\]

Example:

| Product | Forecast volume | Selling price | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | ₱1,000 | ₱5,000,000 |
| B | 3,000 | ₱800 | ₱2,400,000 |
| C | 2,000 | ₱600 | ₱1,200,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | | **₱8,600,000** |

The important consideration is that different products have different selling prices.

Therefore, a change in product mix can affect revenue even if total sales volume remains unchanged.

For example:

- Selling more Product A increases revenue because it has a higher selling price.
- Selling more Product C may increase total volume but generate less revenue per unit.

This is why S&OP analysts should not evaluate sales volume alone.

#### B. Production Plan → Manufacturing Costs

The production plan determines how many units the factory needs to manufacture.

Producing more units generally requires more:

- Raw materials
- Direct labor
- Machine operating hours
- Electricity
- Manufacturing overhead

A simplified manufacturing cost structure is:

\[
\text{Manufacturing Cost}=
\text{Material Cost}+\text{Labor Cost}+\text{Overhead}
\]

Example:

| Cost component | Cost per unit |
|---|---:|
| Direct materials | ₱400 |
| Direct labor | ₱100 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱100 |
| **Total** | **₱600** |

If production volume is 10,000 units:

\[
10,000\times ₱600=₱6,000,000
\]

The projected manufacturing cost is ₱6 million.

However, remember that manufacturing costs incurred are not necessarily equal to expenses recognized immediately in the income statement.

Some manufacturing costs become inventory assets and are recognized as Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) when the corresponding products are sold.

This distinction is important in financial reconciliation.

#### C. Procurement Plan → Material Costs

Procurement determines how much raw material the company needs to purchase.

For example:

- Production requirement: 10,000 units
- Material requirement: 2 kg per finished unit
- Material price: ₱200/kg

Required material quantity:

\[
10,000\times2=20,000\text{ kg}
\]

Projected material requirement cost:

\[
20,000\times ₱200=₱4,000,000
\]

But procurement spending can differ from material consumption.

For example:

The company might purchase 25,000 kg even though production only consumes 20,000 kg.

The remaining 5,000 kg becomes raw material inventory, subject to normal inventory movements and losses.

This means:

**Material purchases affect cash flow and inventory, while material consumption contributes to manufacturing cost.**

They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

#### D. Inventory Plan → Working Capital

Inventory represents money invested in materials, work-in-process (WIP), and finished goods.

Suppose:

- Beginning inventory value: ₱2 million
- Ending inventory value: ₱3 million

The company has increased inventory investment by:

\[
₱3M-₱2M=₱1M
\]

This means an additional ₱1 million is tied up in inventory.

The company may have sufficient products to serve customers, but it also has less cash available for other activities.

Inventory reconciliation therefore evaluates whether the planned inventory level is financially sustainable.

This connects directly to Topic 7.5: Inventory Financial Impact.

#### E. Capacity and Product Mix → Financial Performance

Capacity and product mix can significantly influence profitability.

Consider two products:

| | Product A | Product B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Selling price | ₱1,000 | ₱700 |
| Manufacturing cost | ₱600 | ₱500 |
| Gross profit per unit | ₱400 | ₱200 |

Suppose the factory has capacity for 10,000 units.

**Scenario 1:**

- 8,000 units of A
- 2,000 units of B

Gross profit:

\[
(8,000\times400)+(2,000\times200)
\]

\[
=₱3,600,000
\]

**Scenario 2:**

- 3,000 units of A
- 7,000 units of B

Gross profit:

\[
(3,000\times400)+(7,000\times200)
\]

\[
=₱2,600,000
\]

Both scenarios produce 10,000 units.

However, Scenario 1 generates ₱1 million more gross profit.

This illustrates why product mix is important in S&OP.

Capacity allocation should consider not only production volume but also the financial contribution of each product, subject to customer commitments, demand, constraints, and other business priorities.

### 4.2 Budget, Forecast, and Actual Reconciliation

These three terms are among the most important concepts in financial planning.

They may look similar, but they serve different purposes.

**1. Budget**

The budget is the approved financial target established for a particular period.

It represents what the company intends to achieve.

Example:

- Annual revenue target: ₱120 million
- Annual manufacturing cost budget: ₱72 million
- Gross profit target: ₱48 million

**2. Financial Forecast**

The financial forecast is the company's latest estimate of what it expects to achieve based on current information.

It incorporates updated assumptions, such as:

- Latest customer demand
- Updated selling prices
- Material cost changes
- Production constraints
- Updated labor expenses
- Inventory movements

A forecast can change several times throughout the year.

**3. Actual Results**

Actual results represent what the company has already achieved based on recorded transactions and financial reporting.

These are historical results, not estimates.

#### Budget vs. Forecast vs. Actual

Let's use a simplified monthly revenue example.

| Financial metric | Budget | Forecast | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱10M | ₱9.5M | ₱9.2M |
| Manufacturing cost | ₱6M | ₱5.9M | ₱6.1M |
| Gross profit | ₱4M | ₱3.6M | ₱3.1M |

What can we observe?

- The forecast already indicated that revenue would be below budget.
- Actual revenue was even lower than forecast.
- Manufacturing cost was higher than forecast.
- Gross profit deteriorated further.

This gives management two different questions:

1. Why is the latest forecast different from the approved budget?
2. Why did actual performance differ from the forecast?

These questions require different investigations.

#### Understanding Financial Variances

A variance is the difference between two financial values.

There are two common comparisons:

- Budget vs. Forecast: Are we still expecting to meet the financial target?
- Forecast vs. Actual: Did actual performance match our latest expectations?

For S&OP, Budget vs. Forecast is particularly important because it provides an early warning of potential financial gaps.

### 4.3 Favorable vs. Unfavorable Variances

Not all positive variances are good, and not all negative variances are bad.

The interpretation depends on the metric.

| Financial metric | Situation | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Actual higher than budget | Favorable |
| Revenue | Actual lower than budget | Unfavorable |
| Manufacturing cost | Actual higher than budget | Unfavorable |
| Manufacturing cost | Actual lower than budget | Favorable |
| Gross profit | Actual higher than budget | Favorable |
| Inventory value | Higher than planned | Depends on business context |
| Working capital requirement | Higher than planned | Usually unfavorable for cash availability |

**Important:** Higher inventory is not automatically bad.

If inventory increases because the company is preparing for a confirmed demand increase, it may be strategically justified.

However, if inventory increases because products are not selling, it may indicate excess stock and unnecessary working capital.

Financial reconciliation must consider the reason behind the variance, not just its numerical direction.

### 4.4 Financial Variance Analysis

Now we will examine the major types of financial variances and their possible operational causes.

#### A. Revenue Variance

Revenue variance measures the difference between expected revenue and the financial target.

Possible operational causes:

- Lower customer demand
- Reduced shipment volume
- Delayed production
- Material shortages
- Lower selling prices
- Changes in product mix
- Customer cancellations

Example:

Budget revenue = ₱10 million.

Forecast revenue = ₱9 million.

Revenue gap = -₱1 million.

The analyst should investigate whether the shortfall comes from lower sales volume, price reductions, or product mix changes.

#### B. Material Cost Variance

Material cost variance measures the difference between expected material costs and the budget.

Possible causes:

- Higher supplier prices
- Increased material consumption
- Higher scrap rates
- Production yield deterioration
- Material specification changes
- Emergency purchases
- Supplier substitution

Example:

Budget material cost = ₱4 million.

Forecast material cost = ₱4.5 million.

Unfavorable variance = ₱500,000.

Possible operational explanation:

The factory experienced higher material consumption because of increased production rejects.

This means the financial gap may originate from manufacturing yield rather than procurement price.

#### C. Labor Cost Variance

Labor costs may increase because of:

- Overtime
- Additional shifts
- Increased headcount
- Low labor productivity
- Additional inspection requirements
- Production schedule changes

Example:

Budget labor cost = ₱1 million.

Forecast labor cost = ₱1.2 million.

Unfavorable variance = ₱200,000.

Possible explanation:

Production requirements increased, forcing the factory to operate additional shifts.

However, the analyst should also check whether the additional production is generating enough revenue and profit to justify the extra labor cost.

#### D. Manufacturing Overhead Variance

Manufacturing overhead includes indirect production expenses.

Examples:

- Electricity
- Equipment maintenance
- Depreciation
- Factory utilities
- Indirect labor
- Equipment-related operating expenses

Possible causes of unfavorable overhead variance:

- Higher electricity rates
- Increased machine operating hours
- Equipment breakdowns
- Unplanned maintenance
- Low capacity utilization

For example, production volume may decrease while fixed manufacturing overhead remains relatively constant.

As a result, fixed overhead cost per unit increases.

This is one reason why production volume and capacity utilization affect unit economics.

#### E. Inventory and Working Capital Variance

Inventory variance evaluates the difference between planned and projected inventory value.

Possible causes:

- Production exceeding demand
- Lower-than-expected shipments
- Excess procurement
- Longer supplier lead times
- Safety stock increases
- Slow-moving products
- Obsolete materials

Example:

Budget ending inventory = ₱2 million.

Forecast ending inventory = ₱2.8 million.

Inventory value increased by ₱800,000 against plan.

The company may need additional working capital to support this inventory.

The analyst should determine whether the increase is necessary or whether production and procurement plans need adjustment.

### 4.5 Understanding the Four Main Drivers of Financial Variance

One of the most useful skills in financial reconciliation is identifying what actually caused a financial deviation.

A financial variance may be divided into four major drivers:

**The four financial drivers**

1. **Volume:** Did we sell or produce more or fewer units than planned?
2. **Price:** Did selling prices or purchasing prices change?
3. **Mix:** Did the proportion of high-value and low-value products change?
4. **Efficiency:** Did we consume more or fewer resources per unit than expected?

Let's examine each one.

| Driver | Example | Financial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | Sales decreased from 10,000 to 9,000 units | Lower revenue |
| Price | Selling price decreased from ₱1,000 to ₱950 | Lower revenue per unit |
| Mix | More low-margin products sold | Lower average profit |
| Efficiency | Material consumption increased per unit | Higher unit cost |

These drivers help the analyst move beyond simply reporting that revenue decreased or costs increased.

Instead, the analyst can explain the underlying business reason.

**Example of an effective variance explanation:**

Weak explanation:

"Manufacturing cost increased by 8%."

Better explanation:

"Manufacturing cost increased by 8%, primarily due to a 5% increase in material prices and a 3% increase in material consumption associated with lower production yield."

The second explanation is more useful because it identifies the financial impact and its operational drivers.

## 5. Formulas and Calculations

This is where we convert the concepts into actual financial reconciliation calculations.

Before starting, remember the following conventions:

- For revenue and profit, a higher result is generally favorable.
- For costs, a higher result is generally unfavorable.
- For inventory and working capital, the interpretation depends on the reason for the increase.

For consistency, we will use:

\[
\text{Variance}=\text{Forecast}-\text{Budget}
\]

A negative revenue variance means unfavorable performance.

A positive cost variance means unfavorable performance.

The same subtraction formula can therefore have different interpretations depending on the metric.

### 5.1 Revenue Variance

**Formula:**

\[
RV=FR-BR
\]

Where:

- RV = Revenue variance (₱)
- FR = Forecast revenue (₱)
- BR = Budget revenue (₱)

Example:

Budget revenue = ₱10,000,000.

Forecast revenue = ₱9,000,000.

Step 1: Calculate the difference.

\[
RV=9,000,000-10,000,000
\]

\[
RV=-₱1,000,000
\]

**₱1,000,000 Unfavorable**

Interpretation:

The company expects to generate ₱1 million less revenue than its approved target.

Possible causes include lower sales volume, reduced selling prices, unfavorable product mix, or shipment delays.

### 5.2 Cost Variance

**Formula:**

\[
CV=FC-BC
\]

Where:

- CV = Cost variance (₱)
- FC = Forecast cost (₱)
- BC = Budget cost (₱)

Example:

Budget manufacturing cost = ₱6,000,000.

Forecast manufacturing cost = ₱6,500,000.

\[
CV=6,500,000-6,000,000
\]

\[
CV=+₱500,000
\]

**₱500,000 Unfavorable**

Interpretation:

The company expects to spend ₱500,000 more than its budget.

Possible causes include higher material prices, increased production volume, overtime, lower yield, or higher overhead.

### 5.3 Gross Profit Variance

Gross profit represents revenue less Cost of Goods Sold.

\[
GP=Revenue-COGS
\]

Where:

- GP = Gross profit (₱)
- Revenue = Sales revenue (₱)
- COGS = Cost of Goods Sold (₱)

Budget:

- Revenue = ₱10,000,000
- COGS = ₱6,000,000

\[
GP_B=10,000,000-6,000,000
\]

\[
GP_B=₱4,000,000
\]

Forecast:

- Revenue = ₱9,000,000
- COGS = ₱6,500,000

\[
GP_F=9,000,000-6,500,000
\]

\[
GP_F=₱2,500,000
\]

Gross profit variance:

\[
GPV=GP_F-GP_B
\]

\[
GPV=2,500,000-4,000,000
\]

\[
GPV=-₱1,500,000
\]

**₱1,500,000 Unfavorable**

Interpretation:

The company expects to generate ₱1.5 million less gross profit than planned.

Notice that the unfavorable revenue variance and unfavorable cost variance both contributed to the deterioration.

### 5.4 Operating Profit Variance

Operating profit measures the profit generated from normal business operations after deducting operating expenses.

Simplified formula:

\[
OP=GP-OPEX
\]

Where:

- OP = Operating profit (₱)
- GP = Gross profit (₱)
- OPEX = Operating expenses (₱)

Budget:

- Gross profit = ₱4,000,000
- Operating expenses = ₱2,000,000

\[
OP_B=4,000,000-2,000,000
\]

\[
OP_B=₱2,000,000
\]

Forecast:

- Gross profit = ₱2,500,000
- Operating expenses = ₱2,200,000

\[
OP_F=2,500,000-2,200,000
\]

\[
OP_F=₱300,000
\]

Operating profit variance:

\[
OPV=OP_F-OP_B
\]

\[
OPV=300,000-2,000,000
\]

\[
OPV=-₱1,700,000
\]

**₱1,700,000 Unfavorable**

Interpretation:

The company's projected operating profit is ₱1.7 million below budget.

This is particularly important because it reflects the combined effects of revenue, production costs, and operating expenses.

Note: This is a simplified operating profit calculation. Actual company reporting may include additional operating income, expense classifications, and accounting adjustments.

### 5.5 Inventory Value Variance

Inventory value measures the financial value of materials, WIP, and finished goods held by the company.

Simplified formula:

\[
IV=Q\times UC
\]

Where:

- IV = Inventory value (₱)
- Q = Inventory quantity (units)
- UC = Unit inventory cost (₱/unit)

Example:

Budget:

- Ending inventory = 1,000 units
- Unit cost = ₱600

\[
IV_B=1,000\times600
\]

\[
IV_B=₱600,000
\]

Forecast:

- Ending inventory = 1,500 units
- Unit cost = ₱620

\[
IV_F=1,500\times620
\]

\[
IV_F=₱930,000
\]

Inventory value variance:

\[
IVV=IV_F-IV_B
\]

\[
IVV=930,000-600,000
\]

\[
IVV=+₱330,000
\]

**₱330,000 Additional Inventory Investment**

Interpretation:

The company expects to hold ₱330,000 more inventory value than budgeted.

This may increase working capital requirements.

Important accounting consideration: Actual inventory valuation may use weighted-average cost, FIFO, standard cost, or another approved costing method. The simplified quantity-times-unit-cost calculation assumes a consistent valuation basis.

### 5.6 Budget vs. Forecast Percentage Variance

This formula expresses the financial gap as a percentage of the original budget.

\[
Variance\%=
\frac{Forecast-Budget}{Budget}\times100
\]

Where:

- Forecast = Latest expected financial result
- Budget = Approved financial target

Example:

Budget revenue = ₱10 million.

Forecast revenue = ₱9 million.

\[
Variance\%=
\frac{9-10}{10}\times100
\]

\[
=-10\%
\]

**10% Below Budget**

Interpretation:

The company expects revenue to be 10% lower than its financial target.

For costs, the same formula applies, but a positive percentage indicates an unfavorable increase in cost.

**Common error:** Do not interpret every positive percentage as favorable. Always identify whether you are evaluating revenue, profit, or cost.

### 5.7 Working Capital Impact

Working capital represents resources tied up in the company's short-term operating activities.

For S&OP, one useful measure is Net Operating Working Capital (NOWC).

A simplified formula is:

\[
NOWC=Inventory+AR-AP
\]

Where:

- Inventory = Inventory value (₱)
- AR = Accounts Receivable (₱)
- AP = Accounts Payable (₱)

Accounts Receivable represents money customers owe the company.

Accounts Payable represents money the company owes its suppliers.

Example:

| Component | Budget | Forecast |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventory | ₱2,000,000 | ₱2,500,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | ₱1,000,000 | ₱1,200,000 |
| Accounts Payable | ₱800,000 | ₱900,000 |

Budget working capital:

\[
NOWC_B=2,000,000+1,000,000-800,000
\]

\[
NOWC_B=₱2,200,000
\]

Forecast working capital:

\[
NOWC_F=2,500,000+1,200,000-900,000
\]

\[
NOWC_F=₱2,800,000
\]

Working capital impact:

\[
\Delta NOWC=NOWC_F-NOWC_B
\]

\[
=2,800,000-2,200,000
\]

\[
=+₱600,000
\]

**₱600,000 Additional Working Capital**

Interpretation:

The company requires an additional ₱600,000 to support its projected operating activities.

This is generally a cash-consuming movement because more money is tied up in inventory and receivables, net of supplier financing.

**Important distinction:**

Working capital is not the same as profit.

A company can generate accounting profit while experiencing cash flow pressure because customers have not yet paid and inventory has increased.

### 5.8 Formula Summary

| Metric | Formula | Favorable direction |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue variance | Forecast − Budget | Positive |
| Cost variance | Forecast − Budget | Negative |
| Gross profit variance | Forecast GP − Budget GP | Positive |
| Operating profit variance | Forecast OP − Budget OP | Positive |
| Inventory value variance | Forecast inventory − Budget inventory | Context-dependent |
| Percentage variance | (Forecast − Budget) / Budget × 100 | Depends on metric |
| Working capital impact | Forecast NOWC − Budget NOWC | Negative, if the objective is to reduce cash tied up |

Remember this principle:

**Variance formulas calculate the financial gap. Business interpretation determines whether that gap is favorable or unfavorable.**

## 6. Practical Manufacturing Application

Let's now combine everything into one manufacturing scenario.

This section is particularly important because it demonstrates how an S&OP analyst connects operational data with financial performance.

### 6.1 Business Scenario: Manufacturing Company

Imagine that you work as an S&OP Analyst for a manufacturing company producing electronic components.

The company is preparing its financial and operational outlook for the next month.

Management established the following budget:

**Approved monthly financial targets**

| Financial metric | Budget |
|---|---:|
| Sales volume | 10,000 units |
| Average selling price | ₱1,000/unit |
| Revenue | ₱10,000,000 |
| COGS | ₱6,000,000 |
| Gross profit | ₱4,000,000 |
| Operating expenses | ₱2,000,000 |
| Operating profit | ₱2,000,000 |

The budget assumes:

- Manufacturing cost per sold unit: ₱600
- Raw material cost per unit: ₱420
- Direct labor per unit: ₱100
- Manufacturing overhead per unit: ₱80

Now, the latest demand and supply planning activities provide updated information.

### 6.2 Updated Operational Plan

**A. Demand Plan**

The latest forecast indicates that customer demand has decreased.

| Metric | Budget | Latest forecast |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales volume | 10,000 | 9,500 |
| Average selling price | ₱1,000 | ₱980 |

The company now expects to sell 9,500 units instead of 10,000 units.

The average selling price also decreased from ₱1,000 to ₱980.

**B. Supply Plan**

The company needs to consider its beginning inventory and desired ending inventory.

| Metric | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Beginning inventory | 1,000 units |
| Forecast sales | 9,500 units |
| Target ending inventory | 1,700 units |

Required production:

\[
Production=Sales+Ending-Beginning
\]

\[
=9,500+1,700-1,000
\]

\[
=10,200\text{ units}
\]

The company needs to produce 10,200 units.

Notice something interesting:

Sales decreased, but planned production increased because management wants to build inventory.

This is an important operational decision with financial consequences.

### 6.3 Translating the Operational Plan into Financial Results

Now we translate the latest operational assumptions into financial values.

**A. Projected Revenue**

\[
Revenue=Sales\ Volume\times Selling\ Price
\]

\[
=9,500\times980
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱9,310,000}
\]

Revenue comparison:

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Budget revenue | ₱10,000,000 |
| Forecast revenue | ₱9,310,000 |
| Variance | -₱690,000 |

Percentage variance:

\[
\frac{9.31M-10M}{10M}\times100=-6.9\%
\]

**Interpretation:** Revenue is expected to be 6.9% below target.

**B. Projected Manufacturing Cost**

The updated operational assumptions indicate higher unit costs.

| Cost component | Budget/unit | Forecast/unit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Raw materials | ₱420 | ₱430 |
| Direct labor | ₱100 | ₱110 |
| Manufacturing overhead | ₱80 | ₱85 |
| **Total** | **₱600** | **₱625** |

The projected manufacturing cost per unit increased from ₱600 to ₱625.

Increase:

\[
625-600=₱25/unit
\]

Percentage increase:

\[
\frac{25}{600}\times100=4.17\%
\]

The company now has a 4.17% increase in manufacturing cost per unit.

For financial reporting, we will apply these unit costs to the 9,500 units sold to estimate COGS.

\[
COGS=9,500\times625
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱5,937,500}
\]

Budget COGS:

\[
10,000\times600=₱6,000,000
\]

COGS variance:

\[
5,937,500-6,000,000=-₱62,500
\]

At first glance, this looks favorable because forecast COGS is lower than budget.

But be careful!

The company sold 500 fewer units.

The lower total COGS does not mean manufacturing efficiency improved.

In fact, the unit cost increased by 4.17%.

This is an excellent example of why financial reconciliation must separate volume effects from unit cost effects.

**C. Projected Gross Profit**

\[
GP=Revenue-COGS
\]

\[
=9,310,000-5,937,500
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱3,372,500}
\]

Compare with budget:

\[
3,372,500-4,000,000
\]

\[
=\boxed{-₱627,500}
\]

Gross profit is ₱627,500 below budget.

Gross profit margin:

\[
GPM=\frac{Gross\ Profit}{Revenue}\times100
\]

\[
=\frac{3,372,500}{9,310,000}\times100
\]

\[
=36.22\%
\]

Budget gross profit margin:

\[
\frac{4,000,000}{10,000,000}\times100=40\%
\]

The gross profit margin declined from 40% to approximately 36.22%.

This indicates that the company is generating less gross profit from every peso of sales.

**D. Projected Operating Profit**

Assume the latest operating expense forecast is ₱2.1 million.

\[
OP=GP-OPEX
\]

\[
=3,372,500-2,100,000
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱1,272,500}
\]

Budget operating profit:

\[
4,000,000-2,000,000=₱2,000,000
\]

Operating profit variance:

\[
1,272,500-2,000,000
\]

\[
=\boxed{-₱727,500}
\]

The company expects operating profit to be ₱727,500 below budget.

### 6.4 Inventory Financial Impact

The company also plans to increase ending inventory from 1,000 units to 1,700 units.

For this illustration, assume the budget and forecast ending inventory are valued using their respective unit costs.

Budget inventory value:

\[
1,000\times600=₱600,000
\]

Forecast inventory value:

\[
1,700\times625=₱1,062,500
\]

Inventory value variance:

\[
1,062,500-600,000
\]

\[
=\boxed{+₱462,500}
\]

The company expects to hold ₱462,500 more inventory value than budget.

This is a potential working capital concern.

However, remember that this is an illustrative simplified valuation. Actual accounting may use separate costs for raw materials, WIP, and finished goods.

### 6.5 Consolidated Financial Reconciliation

Let's put everything together.

**Monthly Financial Reconciliation**

| Financial metric | Budget | Forecast | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | ₱10.000M | ₱9.310M | -₱690K |
| COGS | ₱6.000M | ₱5.938M | -₱62.5K |
| Gross profit | ₱4.000M | ₱3.373M | -₱627.5K |
| Operating expenses | ₱2.000M | ₱2.100M | +₱100K |
| Operating profit | ₱2.000M | ₱1.273M | -₱727.5K |
| Ending inventory | ₱0.600M | ₱1.063M | +₱462.5K |

Notice the different interpretations:

- Revenue: unfavorable because it decreased.
- COGS: numerically lower, but partly because sales volume decreased.
- Gross profit: unfavorable because profitability declined.
- Operating expenses: unfavorable because spending increased.
- Operating profit: unfavorable because the combined financial result deteriorated.
- Inventory: increased, creating additional working capital exposure.

This is the financial picture management needs to understand.

### 6.6 Identifying the Operational Root Causes

The next responsibility of the S&OP analyst is to investigate WHY the financial results changed.

Let's connect each financial gap to potential operational causes.

| Financial deviation | Potential operational cause | Department to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue decreased | Lower customer demand | Sales |
| Revenue decreased | Lower selling price | Sales / Commercial |
| Material cost increased per unit | Higher supplier prices | Procurement |
| Material cost increased per unit | Higher material consumption or scrap | Manufacturing |
| Labor cost increased per unit | Overtime or lower productivity | Operations |
| Overhead increased per unit | Higher utility consumption or downtime | Manufacturing |
| Inventory increased | Production exceeded shipment requirements | Production Planning |
| Inventory increased | Lower customer shipments | Sales / Logistics |

An important professional skill is to avoid assuming the root cause without supporting data.

For example, if material cost per unit increased, do not immediately conclude that suppliers increased their prices.

Investigate:

1. Did the purchase price increase?
2. Did material consumption increase?
3. Did scrap or rejection rates increase?
4. Did the product mix change?
5. Did the company purchase materials from alternative suppliers?

The financial gap tells you WHERE to investigate. Operational data helps establish WHY it happened.

### 6.7 Developing Adjustments to Improve Financial Alignment

After identifying the gaps, the S&OP team evaluates possible adjustments.

| Proposed adjustment | Operational impact | Financial impact |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce unnecessary production | Lower inventory accumulation | Lower working capital requirement |
| Improve production yield | Less material waste | Lower manufacturing cost |
| Reduce overtime | Lower labor expenses | Improved operating profit |
| Negotiate material pricing | No necessary change in production quantity | Lower procurement cost |
| Prioritize higher-margin products | Changes production mix | Potentially higher gross profit |
| Improve shipment fulfillment | Higher customer deliveries | Potential revenue recovery |

Let's examine a specific example.

Suppose manufacturing identifies that material waste is increasing because production yield has deteriorated.

The team proposes a process improvement that reduces material consumption cost by ₱10 per unit.

For 9,500 units sold:

\[
Savings=9,500\times10
\]

\[
=\boxed{₱95,000}
\]

If this saving is fully realized and recognized in COGS, gross profit and operating profit could improve by ₱95,000, assuming other conditions remain unchanged.

This is how an operational improvement translates into financial benefits.

However, the analyst must also verify implementation timing, achievable yield improvement, and whether the savings are already included in the forecast.

### 6.8 The Final Reconciliation Decision

After analyzing the operational and financial gaps, management must decide what to do.

There are three possible directions:

**Option 1: Adjust the Operational Plan**

Example:

- Reduce production to prevent unnecessary inventory buildup.
- Improve yield to reduce material consumption.
- Reallocate capacity to more profitable products.
- Adjust procurement quantities.

This is appropriate when operational changes can realistically improve the financial outcome.

**Option 2: Adjust the Financial Forecast**

Example:

- Accept that customer demand has weakened.
- Update the expected selling price.
- Include known supplier price increases.
- Recognize additional operating expenses.

This is appropriate when the original assumptions are no longer realistic.

**Option 3: Reconsider the Business Target**

Example:

- A major customer cancels a significant order.
- A new competitor permanently changes market pricing.
- A major equipment failure limits production capacity.

Management may need to reconsider whether the original target remains achievable.

However, changing a budget target should not be used to hide poor performance or avoid accountability.

The revised forecast must reflect realistic business expectations, while the original approved budget remains an important performance reference unless formally revised under company policy.

**The objective is not to force the forecast to match the budget. The objective is to establish a realistic operational plan with a transparent financial outlook and agreed management actions.**

## 7. Financial Reconciliation in S&OP

Now let's discuss how this works inside an actual S&OP process.

### 7.1 How Finance and Operations Collaborate

Financial reconciliation is a cross-functional activity.

Different departments contribute different information.

| Department | Main responsibility |
|---|---|
| Sales | Demand forecast, selling price and revenue expectations |
| Operations | Production volume, labor and manufacturing expenses |
| Supply Chain | Inventory, procurement, materials and logistics |
| Procurement | Supplier prices, purchasing commitments and lead times |
| Finance | Financial valuation, budget comparison, profit and cash flow |
| S&OP Analyst | Consolidation, variance analysis and identification of gaps |
| Management | Business decisions, priorities and resource allocation |

Finance does not independently create the entire financial outlook.

It needs accurate operational assumptions from the other departments.

Likewise, Operations needs financial information to understand the consequences of its decisions.

### 7.2 The S&OP Analyst's Role

As an S&OP analyst, your responsibility is to connect the information from different departments.

Your work generally involves five activities.

**1. Consolidate the operational assumptions**

Collect the latest:

- Demand forecast
- Sales volume
- Production requirements
- Material requirements
- Inventory projections
- Capacity assumptions

**2. Translate operational quantities into financial values**

Calculate or consolidate:

- Projected revenue
- Material costs
- Manufacturing costs
- Operating expenses
- Inventory valuation
- Gross profit
- Operating profit
- Working capital requirements

**3. Compare the financial forecast against budget**

Identify:

- Revenue shortfalls
- Cost overruns
- Profitability gaps
- Inventory investment increases
- Working capital risks

**4. Investigate the operational causes**

Determine whether the financial gaps are driven by:

- Volume
- Price
- Mix
- Efficiency
- Capacity
- Procurement
- Inventory
- Customer behavior

**5. Communicate the gaps and possible actions**

Present the information in a way that management can understand and use to make decisions.

Your job is not simply to report that revenue is below target.

Your job is to explain what is driving the shortfall, what can realistically be done, and what financial consequences will follow.

### 7.3 Financial Reconciliation During Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where the cross-functional team evaluates unresolved issues before management makes final decisions.

Financial reconciliation is especially important during this stage.

A useful financial reconciliation report should contain:

| Report section | Information to present |
|---|---|
| Financial targets | Approved budget |
| Latest financial outlook | Updated financial forecast |
| Financial gaps | Budget versus forecast variances |
| Operational drivers | Reasons behind the deviations |
| Business risks | Potential negative consequences |
| Opportunities | Potential improvements |
| Proposed actions | Recommended adjustments |
| Decision required | What management needs to approve |

Let's imagine presenting our earlier manufacturing scenario during Pre-S&OP.

A professional summary might sound like this:

> The latest financial outlook indicates that revenue is projected to be 6.9% below budget, primarily driven by lower forecasted sales volume and reduced average selling price. Operating profit is expected to be ₱727,500 below target, while ending inventory value is projected to increase by ₱462,500.
>
> Key operational concerns include increased manufacturing unit cost, higher labor expenses, and inventory accumulation resulting from the planned production volume.
>
> Recommended actions include reviewing the production build-up, investigating material consumption and yield performance, and evaluating opportunities to optimize the product mix. Management alignment is required on the inventory target and the resulting financial outlook.

Notice how this communicates:

1. What happened.
2. How much financial impact it created.
3. What operational factors contributed.
4. What actions are being considered.
5. What decisions are needed.

That is much more useful than presenting a spreadsheet filled with unexplained numbers.

### 7.4 How Management Evaluates Financial Gaps

Management should not automatically approve every operational adjustment that improves short-term profitability.

Business decisions involve trade-offs.

Consider these examples:

| Decision | Potential benefit | Potential risk |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce production | Lower inventory and working capital | Future stockouts |
| Reduce safety stock | Lower inventory investment | Lower service level |
| Reduce overtime | Lower labor cost | Delayed shipments |
| Prioritize high-margin products | Higher profitability | Unfulfilled lower-margin customer commitments |
| Purchase materials earlier | Improved supply protection | Higher inventory and cash requirements |

For example, reducing production might improve working capital in the short term.

However, if customer demand suddenly recovers, the company might experience shortages.

Management therefore needs to evaluate both financial performance and operational service requirements.

The best decision is not necessarily the one that produces the lowest cost.

It is the one that creates the best overall business outcome within the company's strategic priorities and constraints.

### 7.5 Why S&OP Needs One Integrated Business Plan

This is perhaps the most important lesson in Topic 7.6.

Imagine three departments working independently:

- Sales promises ₱12 million in revenue.
- Operations plans production for ₱10 million worth of demand.
- Finance prepares a forecast based on ₱11 million.

Each department has its own numbers.

But which number represents the company's actual business commitment?

There is no reliable answer until these plans are reconciled.

An integrated business plan ensures that:

- Demand assumptions are agreed upon.
- Supply capabilities support the demand plan.
- Inventory targets are operationally achievable.
- Financial forecasts reflect the agreed operational assumptions.
- Management understands the remaining financial gaps.
- Business actions have clear ownership.

**One company. One set of assumptions. One integrated business plan.**

That is the ultimate purpose of S&OP Financial Reconciliation.

## 8. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate the most important concepts before moving to the exercises.

### 8.1 Concepts You Must Understand

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Financial Reconciliation | Translates operational plans into financial results and identifies gaps against targets |
| Operational Reconciliation | Determines whether the supply plan can satisfy demand |
| Financial Forecast | Latest estimate of expected financial performance |
| Budget | Approved financial target |
| Actual | Recorded financial performance |
| Revenue Variance | Difference between forecasted and budgeted revenue |
| Cost Variance | Difference between forecasted and budgeted costs |
| Gross Profit | Revenue minus COGS |
| Operating Profit | Gross profit minus operating expenses, in a simplified income statement |
| Inventory Value | Financial value of inventory held |
| Working Capital | Money tied up in operating assets, net of operating liabilities |
| Volume | Financial impact caused by changes in quantities |
| Price | Financial impact caused by changes in selling or purchasing prices |
| Mix | Financial impact caused by changes in product composition |
| Efficiency | Financial impact caused by changes in resource consumption |

### 8.2 Five Important Principles

1. **Operational plans must have financial implications.** Production quantities, procurement requirements, and inventory targets must be translated into financial values.

2. **A favorable cost variance does not automatically mean better performance.** Lower total costs might simply result from producing or selling fewer units.

3. **Financial variances need operational explanations.** Revenue shortfalls, cost increases, and inventory accumulation should be investigated using operational data.

4. **Profit and cash flow are different.** A company can report a profit while experiencing cash constraints due to inventory accumulation and delayed customer payments.

5. **The goal is an integrated business plan.** Finance, Sales, Operations, and Supply Chain must work using consistent assumptions.

### 8.4 Foundation for Topic 7.7: Financial Scenario Analysis

Topic 7.6 teaches you how to identify the financial consequences of an existing operational plan.

Topic 7.7 will take this further.

Instead of asking:

*What will happen financially if we execute the current plan?*

*What will happen financially if we change the plan?*

For example:

- What if sales volume increases by 10%?
- What if material prices increase by 5%?
- What if production yield improves?
- What if we reduce ending inventory?
- What if we prioritize higher-margin products?
- What if capacity becomes constrained?

This introduces the concept of scenario-based decision-making.
