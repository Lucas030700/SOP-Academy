# Module 4 – Supply Planning
## Topic 4.7: Supply Planning Strategies

**Level:** Beginner to Professional | Taglish

**Main Learning Objective:** Matutunan mong pumili ng appropriate supply planning strategy para matugunan ang customer demand habang kino-control ang production capacity, inventory, operational costs, at delivery performance.

## 1. Topic Overview

### What are Supply Planning Strategies?

Sa previous topics, pinag-aralan natin kung paano sinusukat ang production capacity, resources, lead time, at bottlenecks.

Ngayon, pag-uusapan naman natin:

**Kapag alam na natin ang customer demand at available production capacity, paano natin ise-set ang production and supply activities para ma-meet ang demand?**

Ito ang purpose ng Supply Planning Strategies.

Halimbawa, isang manufacturing company produces 10,000 units per month.

Pero ang customer demand ay:

| Month | Customer Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 12,000 |
| March | 15,000 |
| April | 9,000 |

May problema tayo:

- January: May excess production capacity.
- February: Tumataas ang demand.
- March: Mas mataas ang demand kaysa normal production capacity.
- April: Bumababa ulit ang demand.

Ang tanong: Dapat ba nating dagdagan ang production sa February at March? Mag-build ba tayo ng inventory bago dumating ang peak demand? Mag-overtime ba tayo? O mag-subcontract tayo sa ibang manufacturer?

These are supply planning decisions.

### Why is this topic important?

Hindi sapat na malaman lang natin kung gaano karaming products ang kailangan ng customers.

Kailangan din nating malaman kung paano economically and operationally possible na ma-produce at ma-deliver ang required quantity.

Ang isang magandang supply plan ay kailangang:

1. Meet customer demand.
2. Stay within available capacity or identify required capacity adjustments.
3. Maintain appropriate inventory levels.
4. Control manufacturing and supply chain costs.
5. Protect customer delivery performance.
6. Consider workforce and supplier limitations.

Let's connect our previous topics:

## 2. Layman's Explanation

### Think of a manufacturing company as a restaurant.

Imagine na may restaurant ka.

Normal capacity mo is 100 meals per day.

Pero nagbabago ang customer orders:

- Monday: 70 meals
- Tuesday: 90 meals
- Wednesday: 150 meals
- Thursday: 120 meals
- Friday: 60 meals

May iba't ibang paraan para ma-handle ang situation.

**Strategy A: Produce based on expected demand**

Kapag 150 meals ang expected customers bukas, maghahanda ka ng 150 meals.

Pero kailangan mong siguraduhin na may enough ingredients, kitchen equipment, at workers.

**Strategy B: Produce consistently and build inventory**

Instead of preparing exactly what customers need each day, maghahanda ka ng consistent quantity.

Kapag 70 meals lang ang kailangan pero 100 ang naihanda, may excess inventory.

Puwede itong gamitin kapag tumaas ang demand kinabukasan, assuming safe and suitable for storage.

**Strategy C: Adjust production based on demand**

Kapag mataas ang demand, magdadagdag ka ng workers or working hours.

Kapag mababa ang demand, babawasan mo ang production.

**Strategy D: Outsource**

Kung hindi kaya ng kitchen mo ang 150 meals, puwede kang kumuha ng additional food supplier.

These are simplified examples of supply planning strategies.

Sa manufacturing, similar ang concept, pero mas complicated dahil may machine capacity, production cycle time, raw materials, supplier lead times, quality requirements, and inventory costs.

### The central question

Hindi lang:

*How much should we produce?*

Kundi:

**How should we produce, when should we produce, and what resources or adjustments are needed to satisfy demand at an acceptable cost?**

## 3. Technical Explanation

### 3.1 What is Supply Planning?

Supply Planning is the process of determining how a company will meet expected demand using available and planned supply resources.

It involves decisions concerning:

- Production quantities
- Production timing
- Inventory levels
- Manufacturing capacity
- Raw material availability
- Supplier commitments
- Subcontracting
- Production scheduling
- Distribution and fulfillment requirements

The objective is to create a supply response that balances customer requirements with operational and financial constraints.

### 3.2 Supply Plan vs. Production Plan

Magkaugnay sila, pero hindi sila identical.

| Aspect | Supply Plan | Production Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Overall supply response | Manufacturing output |
| Focus | How to meet demand | What and how much to manufacture |
| Activities | Production, inventory, sourcing, subcontracting, supply allocation | Production quantities, timing, lines, shifts |
| Constraints | Capacity, suppliers, inventory, materials, logistics | Machines, labor, materials, production time |
| Output | Overall supply commitments and requirements | Planned manufacturing quantities |

**Example:**

A company needs to supply 15,000 units to customers.

Its supply plan could be:

- 10,000 units from internal production.
- 3,000 units from existing inventory.
- 2,000 units from subcontracting.

Its production plan may only require manufacturing 10,000 units internally.

Notice the difference?

The supply plan explains how the total customer requirement will be satisfied. The production plan specifies the manufacturing activities needed to support that plan.

### 3.3 Factors Influencing Supply Strategy Selection

Hindi lahat ng manufacturing companies ay gumagamit ng parehong strategy.

The appropriate strategy depends on several factors:

| Factor | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Demand variability | Highly fluctuating demand may require flexibility. |
| Production capacity | Determines how much output can be generated. |
| Inventory availability | Existing stocks can reduce immediate production requirements. |
| Product characteristics | Shelf life, customization, and complexity affect production decisions. |
| Manufacturing lead time | Determines how early production must start. |
| Material availability | Production cannot proceed without required materials. |
| Production cost | Overtime, inventory, and outsourcing have different costs. |
| Customer requirements | Delivery commitments and service levels influence priorities. |
| Workforce flexibility | Determines whether additional shifts or labor are possible. |
| Supplier flexibility | Determines whether external supply can respond to changes. |

**Professional insight:** The lowest-cost production strategy is not automatically the best supply strategy.

For example, producing in large quantities may reduce unit manufacturing costs but increase inventory holding costs and working capital requirements.

Kailangan nating tingnan ang total business impact.

## 4. Detailed Discussion: Different Supply Planning Strategies

May dalawang importanteng ways to classify supply strategies:

1. **Production and inventory strategies:** How and when products are manufactured relative to customer demand.
2. **Supply flexibility strategies:** How companies adjust their available supply resources when actual or forecast demand changes.

### 4.1 Production and Inventory Strategies

#### A. Make-to-Stock (MTS)

**Produce first, sell later.**

Make-to-Stock means manufacturing products based on forecasted demand before receiving actual customer orders.

Simple explanation:

Gumagawa muna ang company ng products, then ini-store sa finished goods inventory. Kapag may customer order, available na agad ang product.

**Example:**

A manufacturing company forecasts monthly demand of 10,000 units.

Instead of waiting for customer orders, it manufactures 10,000 units and stores them in its warehouse.

**Advantages:**
- Faster customer delivery.
- Products are readily available.
- Production can be scheduled efficiently.
- Opportunities for longer production runs.

**Disadvantages:**
- Risk of excess inventory.
- Requires working capital.
- Risk of obsolete or slow-moving stocks.
- Forecast errors can create inventory problems.

**When to use MTS:**
- Products have relatively predictable demand.
- Customers expect immediate availability.
- Products have standardized specifications.
- Production lead time is longer than acceptable customer delivery lead time.

**S&OP perspective:** The analyst must ensure that the forecast supports the planned inventory build-up. Otherwise, the company may produce more than the market actually needs.

#### B. Make-to-Order (MTO)

**Receive order, then produce.**

Make-to-Order means manufacturing starts after receiving an actual customer order.

Simple explanation:

Hindi muna gumagawa ng finished goods inventory. Maghihintay muna ng confirmed order bago mag-manufacture.

**Example:**

A customer orders 500 customized industrial components.

The manufacturer schedules production of those 500 units only after receiving the order.

**Advantages:**
- Lower finished goods inventory.
- Reduced risk of obsolete finished products.
- Better customization.
- Production is linked to actual customer requirements.

**Disadvantages:**
- Longer customer waiting time.
- Production scheduling becomes more order-dependent.
- Demand spikes can create backlogs.
- Requires reliable material availability.

**When to use MTO:**
- Products are highly customized.
- Demand is unpredictable.
- Finished goods are expensive to store.
- Customers can accept longer delivery lead times.

**S&OP perspective:** The analyst must evaluate whether available production capacity and material lead times can support the expected order volume and promised delivery dates.

#### C. Assemble-to-Order (ATO)

**Prepare components first, assemble after order.**

Assemble-to-Order is a combination of MTS and MTO.

The company manufactures or stocks common components in advance but performs final assembly after receiving a customer order.

**Example:**

A computer manufacturer maintains inventory of:

- Motherboards
- Memory modules
- Storage devices
- Power supplies
- Computer cases

Kapag nag-order ang customer ng customized computer, saka lang iko-combine ang components based on the customer's specifications.

**Advantages:**
- Faster delivery than fully MTO.
- More customization than MTS.
- Lower finished goods inventory.
- Common components can be stocked efficiently.

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires accurate component planning.
- Component shortages can delay final assembly.
- Requires flexible assembly capacity.
- More complex inventory coordination.

**When to use ATO:**
- Products have common components but different final configurations.
- Customers require customization.
- Component lead times are relatively long.
- Final assembly is comparatively quick.

#### MTS vs. MTO vs. ATO

| Criteria | MTS | MTO | ATO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Production trigger | Forecast | Actual order | Actual order for assembly |
| Finished goods inventory | High or planned | Low | Usually low |
| Component inventory | Required | Depends on process | Usually important |
| Customer lead time | Short | Longer | Moderate |
| Customization | Low | High | Moderate to high |
| Forecast importance | Very high | Important for resources | High for components |

**Remember:** These are order-fulfillment approaches. They are different from level, chase, and hybrid production strategies, which describe how production quantities are adjusted over time.

### 4.2 Level Production Strategy

**Stable production volume.**

Level Production means maintaining relatively consistent production output despite changes in customer demand.

Ang idea: Instead na palaging baguhin ang production quantity, maintain a relatively stable production rate.

**Example:**

A factory has the following monthly demand:

| Month | Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 12,000 |
| March | 15,000 |
| April | 9,000 |

Instead of changing production every month, the company produces 11,000 units monthly.

What happens?

- January: Produces more than demand, so inventory increases.
- February: Uses some of the accumulated inventory.
- March: Uses more inventory to satisfy higher demand.
- April: Builds inventory again.

**Advantages:**
- Stable workforce requirements.
- More consistent machine utilization.
- Easier production scheduling.
- Potentially lower overtime and hiring costs.
- More predictable material requirements.

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires inventory buildup during low-demand periods.
- Higher inventory holding costs.
- Requires sufficient storage space.
- May create inventory obsolescence risks.

**When to use:**
- Demand fluctuates but is relatively predictable.
- Inventory can be stored economically.
- Production changes are expensive.
- Workforce stability is important.

**Key concept:** Level production transfers some of the burden of demand variability from production capacity to inventory.

Kapag tumaas ang demand, inventory ang tumutulong para ma-meet ang requirements.

### 4.3 Chase Demand Strategy

**Production follows demand.**

Chase Demand means adjusting production output to closely follow changes in customer demand.

Instead of maintaining a constant production rate, the company increases or decreases output depending on requirements.

**Example:**

| Month | Demand | Planned Production |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 8,000 | 8,000 |
| February | 12,000 | 12,000 |
| March | 15,000 | 15,000 |
| April | 9,000 | 9,000 |

**Advantages:**
- Lower inventory requirements.
- Production closely follows demand.
- Less risk of excess finished goods.
- Potentially lower working capital requirements.

**Disadvantages:**
- Frequent production adjustments.
- Overtime and additional shifts may be necessary.
- Workforce scheduling becomes more difficult.
- Machine changeovers may increase.
- Supplier requirements may fluctuate.

**When to use:**
- Inventory holding costs are high.
- Products have short shelf lives.
- Production capacity is flexible.
- Workforce and supplier capacity can respond quickly.

**Key concept:** Chase demand transfers more of the burden of demand variability to production capacity.

Kapag mataas ang demand, kailangan mong dagdagan ang production resources. Kapag mababa, kailangan mong bawasan ang output.

### 4.4 Hybrid Production Strategy

**Combination of level and chase.**

Hybrid Strategy combines stable production with controlled production adjustments.

Hindi kailangang completely fixed ang production. Hindi rin kailangang eksaktong sundan ang demand.

Instead, the company maintains a base production rate and uses flexibility mechanisms when needed.

**Example:**

A factory normally produces 10,000 units monthly.

When demand increases to 15,000 units, it can:

- Maintain 10,000 units as regular production.
- Produce an additional 2,000 units through overtime.
- Use 3,000 units from existing inventory.

This is a hybrid approach.

**Advantages:**
- Balances production stability and inventory.
- Reduces extreme production adjustments.
- More flexible during demand fluctuations.
- Can control overtime and inventory costs.

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires more planning coordination.
- Inventory and capacity must be monitored together.
- May still require overtime or subcontracting during peak periods.

**When to use:** When neither pure level nor pure chase is economically or operationally suitable.

In practice, many manufacturing companies use hybrid strategies because actual operations rarely allow perfectly stable production or unlimited production flexibility.

### 4.5 Inventory Build-Ahead Strategy

Inventory Build-Ahead means producing additional quantities before expected demand increases.

Instead of waiting for the peak-demand period, the company manufactures in advance while capacity is available.

**Example:**

A manufacturer expects demand to increase from 10,000 to 15,000 units in March.

However, March production capacity is limited to 12,000 units.

The company can produce additional inventory during January and February to prepare for March.

**Why is this useful?**

Because production capacity and customer demand do not always occur at the same time.

May available capacity ngayon, pero baka hindi available kapag kailangan na ng customers ang products.

**Advantages:**
- Prepares for seasonal demand.
- Reduces peak-period capacity pressure.
- Protects customer service.
- Makes better use of available production capacity.

**Disadvantages:**
- Higher inventory carrying costs.
- More working capital tied up.
- Risk of inaccurate forecasts.
- Potential storage limitations.

### 4.6 Production Smoothing

Production Smoothing means reducing unnecessary production fluctuations by maintaining a more stable and predictable production pattern.

It is closely related to level production.

However, production smoothing emphasizes reducing operational instability, including:

- Frequent schedule changes.
- Excessive machine setup changes.
- Workforce fluctuations.
- Material requirement fluctuations.
- Production interruptions.

**Example:**

Instead of producing:

10,000 → 15,000 → 7,000 → 14,000 units,

the company may maintain production at approximately 11,500 units per month and use inventory to absorb demand variations.

**Important distinction:**
- Level production is a planning strategy that maintains relatively consistent output.
- Production smoothing is the broader operational approach of minimizing fluctuations and stabilizing production activities.

### 4.7 Supply Flexibility Strategies

Supply flexibility refers to the company's ability to adjust its supply resources and operations to respond to changes in demand or supply conditions.

#### A. Overtime and Additional Shifts

Increasing working hours or adding shifts to increase production output.

Example:

Normal operation:
- 1 shift = 8 hours.
- Production = 1,000 units per shift.

If demand increases, the company may introduce a second shift.

Potential output becomes 2,000 units per day, assuming sufficient machines, workers, materials, and other resources.

**Considerations:**
- Overtime premium.
- Employee fatigue.
- Quality risks.
- Equipment maintenance requirements.
- Availability of qualified operators.

#### B. Subcontracting and Outsourcing

Subcontracting means assigning part of the production requirements to an external manufacturer.

Example:

Internal production capacity = 10,000 units.

Required supply = 13,000 units.

The company may outsource 3,000 units to a qualified supplier.

**Advantages:**
- Additional production capacity.
- Reduced need for permanent investment.
- Useful for temporary demand spikes.

**Risks:**
- Higher unit costs.
- Quality consistency.
- Supplier dependency.
- Longer coordination and transportation lead times.
- Intellectual property and confidentiality considerations.

#### C. Alternative Production Lines and Facilities

A company may transfer production to another line or facility with available capacity.

Example:

| Production Line | Available Capacity |
|---|---:|
| Line A | 5,000 |
| Line B | 4,000 |
| Line C | 6,000 |
| **Total** | **15,000** |

If Line A experiences an unexpected problem, the company may shift eligible production to Lines B and C.

However, this depends on:

- Equipment compatibility.
- Product qualification.
- Technical capability.
- Labor availability.
- Quality approval.
- Material compatibility.

Hindi automatic na kapag may available capacity ang ibang line, puwede nang ilipat ang production.

#### D. Production Rescheduling

Production Rescheduling means changing the timing or sequence of planned manufacturing activities.

Example:

Originally:
- Product A: Monday
- Product B: Tuesday
- Product C: Wednesday

A priority customer suddenly needs Product C earlier.

The company may move Product C to Monday.

But this may affect Product A and B delivery commitments.

**Important considerations:**
- Changeover requirements.
- Material availability.
- Existing customer commitments.
- Production sequence.
- Bottleneck utilization.

#### E. Production Prioritization and Allocation

When supply is insufficient, companies must decide which orders or customers should receive available products first.

Example:

Available supply = 8,000 units.

Customer requirements:

| Customer | Required Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer A | 4,000 |
| Customer B | 3,000 |
| Customer C | 5,000 |
| **Total** | **12,000** |

The company cannot satisfy all 12,000 units.

It needs an allocation policy based on business priorities.

Possible considerations:
- Contractual commitments.
- Customer importance.
- Order priority.
- Profitability.
- Strategic accounts.
- Customer service agreements.

Allocation must follow approved business rules, not arbitrary decisions by individual planners.

#### F. Supplier Flexibility and Alternative Sourcing

Companies may work with suppliers that can:
- Increase material supply.
- Reduce procurement lead times.
- Provide alternative materials, subject to approval.
- Deliver in smaller or more frequent quantities.
- Support emergency requirements.

Example:

A manufacturer normally purchases raw material from Supplier A with a 30-day lead time.

Supplier A cannot meet the increased requirement.

The company evaluates Supplier B, which has available capacity and a 20-day lead time.

However, technical qualification, quality approval, and commercial evaluation must happen before switching suppliers.

### 4.8 Managing Demand-Supply Imbalances

A demand-supply imbalance occurs when the required demand is different from the available supply.

There are two main situations.

#### Situation A: Demand exceeds available supply

**Supply shortage.**

Example:
- Demand = 15,000 units.
- Available supply = 12,000 units.
- Supply shortage = 3,000 units.

Possible responses:

1. Increase production through overtime.
2. Add production shifts.
3. Use available inventory.
4. Subcontract production.
5. Reallocate available supply.
6. Negotiate delivery schedules with customers.
7. Prioritize high-priority orders.
8. Evaluate alternative sourcing.

The company must determine which combination provides the best balance of cost and service.

#### Situation B: Supply exceeds demand

**Excess supply.**

Example:
- Demand = 8,000 units.
- Available supply = 12,000 units.
- Excess supply = 4,000 units.

Possible responses:

1. Reduce production.
2. Reschedule manufacturing.
3. Build inventory if future demand supports it.
4. Reduce overtime or additional shifts.
5. Delay procurement where commercially possible.
6. Reallocate inventory to other products, locations, or customers when appropriate.

**Important:** Excess supply is not automatically a good thing.

Producing more than required can increase storage costs, working capital, and obsolescence risk.

### 4.9 Balancing Inventory, Capacity, Lead Time, and Service Levels

These four elements are closely connected.

| Element | Main Question | Business Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | How much stock is available? | Determines immediate supply buffer. |
| Capacity | How much can we produce? | Determines production capability. |
| Lead time | How long before supply becomes available? | Determines when production or procurement must start. |
| Service level | How reliably can we meet customer requirements? | Determines customer fulfillment performance. |

Consider this example:

A customer requires 10,000 units within five days.

The company has:
- Finished goods inventory: 3,000 units.
- Production capacity: 1,000 units/day.
- Production lead time: 2 days.

Available supply from inventory and production within five days:

\[
3,000+(1,000\times5)=8,000
\]

If the full five days are available for production, the company can supply 8,000 units, leaving a 2,000-unit gap.

But if the two-day production lead time means production cannot begin until two days later, only three production days remain:

\[
3,000+(1,000\times3)=6,000
\]

Now the shortage is 4,000 units.

This illustrates why lead time assumptions matter when evaluating supply feasibility.

## 5. Practical Examples and Applications

Let's connect these concepts to an actual manufacturing environment.

Imagine a company manufacturing electronic components.

Its customer demand fluctuates monthly, while its production capacity is limited.

The supply planner must decide whether to:
- Maintain stable production.
- Follow customer demand.
- Build inventory before peak demand.
- Use overtime.
- Outsource additional production.

### 5.1 How Different Strategies Respond to the Same Situation

| Situation | Level Strategy | Chase Strategy | Hybrid Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand decreases | Maintain output and build inventory | Reduce output | Slightly reduce output |
| Demand increases | Use accumulated inventory | Increase production | Use inventory and increase production |
| Capacity is limited | May be protected by advance production | May require overtime | Uses inventory and selective flexibility |
| Inventory costs increase | Potential concern | Usually lower inventory | Moderate inventory |
| Workforce stability | Generally high | May fluctuate | Moderate to high |

### 5.2 Common Planning Mistakes

| Incorrect Approach | Why It Is Incorrect | Possible Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Produce exactly the forecast without checking inventory | Ignores existing stock | Excess inventory |
| Increase production without checking bottlenecks | Assumes all resources can increase output | Unrealistic production plan |
| Use overtime as the default solution | Ignores labor and equipment constraints | Higher costs and fatigue |
| Outsource without quality approval | Ignores qualification requirements | Quality issues |
| Build inventory based only on forecast growth | Ignores demand uncertainty | Excess and obsolete stock |
| Prioritize customers without allocation rules | Ignores business commitments | Customer disputes |

**Professional lesson:** A supply planning strategy must be supported by data, constraints, and clear business assumptions.

## 6. Formulas and Calculations

These formulas will become particularly useful when working with Excel, ERP reports, and supply planning dashboards.

### 6.1 Required Production Quantity

This calculates how much additional production is required to satisfy demand while considering existing inventory.

\[
P=D+EI-BI
\]

Where:

| Variable | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| \(P\) | Required production | Units |
| \(D\) | Expected demand | Units |
| \(EI\) | Desired ending inventory | Units |
| \(BI\) | Beginning inventory | Units |

**Example:**
- Expected demand = 12,000 units.
- Beginning inventory = 3,000 units.
- Desired ending inventory = 2,000 units.

Step 1: Identify the demand requirement.

\[
D=12,000
\]

Step 2: Add desired ending inventory.

\[
12,000+2,000=14,000
\]

Step 3: Subtract beginning inventory.

\[
P=14,000-3,000
\]

**Required production: 11,000 units.**

Interpretation:

The company only needs to manufacture 11,000 units because it already has 3,000 units available.

**Common error:** Forgetting to subtract beginning inventory results in overestimating production requirements.

### 6.2 Projected Ending Inventory

\[
EI=BI+P-D
\]

Where:
- \(EI\) = Ending inventory.
- \(BI\) = Beginning inventory.
- \(P\) = Production quantity.
- \(D\) = Demand.

Example:
- Beginning inventory = 5,000.
- Production = 10,000.
- Demand = 12,000.

\[
EI=5,000+10,000-12,000
\]

\[
EI=3,000\text{ units}
\]

Interpretation:

After satisfying demand, the company has 3,000 units remaining.

Note: This simplified equation assumes no additional inventory movements, such as scrap, transfers, returns, or adjustments.

### 6.3 Production Requirements Under Level Strategy

Level production maintains a relatively constant production quantity.

A simplified calculation is:

\[
P_{\text{level}}=
\frac{\text{Total Demand}+EI-BI}{N}
\]

Where:
- Total Demand = Total expected demand over the planning horizon.
- \(EI\) = Desired ending inventory.
- \(BI\) = Beginning inventory.
- \(N\) = Number of planning periods.

Example:
- Total demand over four months = 44,000 units.
- Beginning inventory = 2,000 units.
- Desired ending inventory = 2,000 units.
- Planning horizon = 4 months.

\[
P_{\text{level}}=
\frac{44,000+2,000-2,000}{4}
\]

\[
P_{\text{level}}=11,000
\]

The company should produce 11,000 units per month.

Important assumption: This calculation provides the average production requirement. The monthly inventory projection must still be checked to ensure there are no shortages along the way.

### 6.4 Production Requirements Under Chase Strategy

Under a chase strategy, production closely follows each period's demand.

\[
P_t=D_t+EI_t-BI_t
\]

Where:
- \(P_t\) = Production requirement for the current period.
- \(D_t\) = Demand for the current period.
- \(EI_t\) = Desired ending inventory.
- \(BI_t\) = Beginning inventory.

Example:
- Demand = 15,000.
- Beginning inventory = 2,000.
- Desired ending inventory = 2,000.

\[
P_t=15,000+2,000-2,000
\]

\[
P_t=15,000
\]

The company must produce 15,000 units to satisfy demand and maintain its desired inventory.

Unlike level production, the production quantity changes depending on demand.

### 6.5 Capacity Utilization

This measures how much of the available production capacity is being used.

\[
\text{Capacity Utilization}=
\frac{\text{Actual or Planned Output}}
{\text{Available Capacity}}\times100
\]

Example:
- Planned output = 10,000 units.
- Available capacity = 12,000 units.

\[
=\frac{10,000}{12,000}\times100
\]

\[
=83.33\%
\]

Interpretation:

The production plan uses 83.33% of the available capacity.

The remaining 16.67% represents unused capacity under the same capacity definition.

**Important:** Always use a consistent capacity basis. Rated capacity, demonstrated capacity, and effective available capacity may produce different utilization figures.

### 6.6 Supply-Demand Gap

This identifies whether available supply is sufficient to meet demand.

\[
\text{Supply-Demand Gap}=S-D
\]

Where:
- \(S\) = Available supply.
- \(D\) = Demand.

Example:
- Available supply = 12,000.
- Demand = 15,000.

\[
12,000-15,000=-3,000
\]

**Shortage: 3,000 units.**

If the result is positive, supply exceeds demand.

If the result is negative, supply is insufficient.

For planning purposes, define exactly what is included in available supply. It may include usable beginning inventory, scheduled receipts, and feasible production, but avoid double-counting these quantities.

### 6.7 Additional Useful Formula: Inventory Coverage

Another useful measure when selecting a supply strategy is Days of Supply (DOS).

\[
DOS=\frac{\text{Available Inventory}}
{\text{Average Daily Demand}}
\]

Example:
- Available inventory = 6,000 units.
- Average daily demand = 300 units/day.

\[
DOS=\frac{6,000}{300}=20\text{ days}
\]

Interpretation:

The company has approximately 20 days of inventory coverage, assuming demand remains at the same rate and the inventory is fully usable.

## 7. Practical Manufacturing Application: Comparing Level and Chase Strategies

Now let's combine everything.

This will be our main case study for Topic 4.7.

### 7.1 Business Scenario

A manufacturer produces electronic components.

The company experiences fluctuating monthly customer demand.

The planning team must choose between:
- Strategy A: Level Production.
- Strategy B: Chase Demand.

**Illustrative planning assumptions:**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---:|
| Beginning inventory | 2,000 units |
| Monthly production capacity | 12,000 units |
| Monthly production demand | Variable |
| Regular production cost | ₱10/unit |
| Overtime production cost | ₱15/unit |
| Inventory holding cost | ₱0.50/unit/month |
| Desired ending inventory for chase | 2,000 units |

Assumptions for this exercise:
- Four-month planning horizon.
- No scrap, quality losses, or inventory adjustments.
- Overtime can support output above 12,000 units, up to the required quantity in the example.
- No stockouts are allowed in either strategy.
- Inventory holding cost is based on average monthly inventory.
- Production costs are assumed to be the same per unit within the specified cost categories.

### 7.2 Monthly Demand Forecast

| Month | Customer Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 12,000 |
| March | 15,000 |
| April | 9,000 |
| **Total** | **44,000** |

Observe that March has the highest demand.

But our normal monthly production capacity is only 12,000 units.

This is where strategy selection becomes important.

### 7.3 Strategy A: Level Production

The company decides to produce a constant 11,000 units per month.

Using our earlier formula:

\[
P_{\text{level}}=
\frac{44,000+2,000-2,000}{4}
\]

\[
P_{\text{level}}=11,000
\]

Now let's calculate projected inventory.

| Month | Beginning Inventory | Production | Demand | Ending Inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 2,000 | 11,000 | 8,000 | 5,000 |
| February | 5,000 | 11,000 | 12,000 | 4,000 |
| March | 4,000 | 11,000 | 15,000 | 0 |
| April | 0 | 11,000 | 9,000 | 2,000 |

**What happened?**
- January: Inventory increased because production exceeded demand.
- February: Inventory decreased slightly.
- March: The company used all available inventory to support peak demand.
- April: The company rebuilt inventory.

**Capacity evaluation**

Monthly capacity utilization:

\[
\frac{11,000}{12,000}\times100=91.67\%
\]

The company uses 91.67% of its normal monthly production capacity throughout the four months.

No overtime is required.

**Cost calculation**

Regular production cost:

\[
44,000\times ₱10=₱440,000
\]

Inventory holding cost:

\[
H=\text{Average Inventory}\times\text{Holding Cost}
\]

| Month | Average Inventory |
|---|---:|
| January | 3,500 |
| February | 4,500 |
| March | 2,000 |
| April | 1,000 |
| **Total** | **11,000 unit-months** |

\[
11,000\times ₱0.50=₱5,500
\]

Total relevant cost:

\[
₱440,000+₱5,500
\]

**₱445,500**

### 7.4 Strategy B: Chase Demand

Under this strategy, the company adjusts production to match monthly demand while maintaining 2,000 units of ending inventory.

Production requirement:

\[
P_t=D_t+EI_t-BI_t
\]

| Month | Beginning Inventory | Demand | Required Production | Ending Inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 2,000 | 8,000 | 8,000 | 2,000 |
| February | 2,000 | 12,000 | 12,000 | 2,000 |
| March | 2,000 | 15,000 | 15,000 | 2,000 |
| April | 2,000 | 9,000 | 9,000 | 2,000 |

**What happened?**

Production follows demand closely.

However, March requires 15,000 units, which is 3,000 units above normal capacity.

Therefore, the company needs overtime or another capacity adjustment.

**Capacity evaluation**

| Month | Production | Capacity | Utilization |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 8,000 | 12,000 | 66.67% |
| February | 12,000 | 12,000 | 100% |
| March | 15,000 | 12,000 | 125% |
| April | 9,000 | 12,000 | 75% |

March exceeds normal capacity by 25%.

This means the plan cannot be achieved through normal capacity alone.

**Cost calculation**

Regular production cost:

\[
41,000\times ₱10=₱410,000
\]

Overtime production cost:

\[
3,000\times ₱15=₱45,000
\]

Total production cost:

\[
₱410,000+₱45,000=₱455,000
\]

Inventory holding cost:

Inventory remains at 2,000 units throughout the four months.

\[
2,000\times4\times ₱0.50=₱4,000
\]

Total relevant cost:

\[
₱455,000+₱4,000
\]

**₱459,000**

### 7.5 Strategy Comparison

| Evaluation | Level | Chase |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total production | 44,000 | 44,000 |
| Regular production | 44,000 | 41,000 |
| Overtime production | 0 | 3,000 |
| Peak monthly output | 11,000 | 15,000 |
| Highest normal capacity utilization | 91.67% | 125% |
| Ending inventory | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| Inventory holding cost | ₱5,500 | ₱4,000 |
| Production cost | ₱440,000 | ₱455,000 |
| **Total relevant cost** | **₱445,500** | **₱459,000** |

*All costs are illustrative and exclude procurement, logistics, changeovers, additional staffing, subcontracting, and other expenses not specified in the scenario.*

### 7.6 Business Interpretation

Which strategy is better?

Based on the assumptions, Level Production is cheaper by:

\[
₱459,000-₱445,500=₱13,500
\]

That is a 3% cost difference relative to the level strategy's cost.

But let's look beyond the total cost.

**Level Production:**
- More stable manufacturing operations.
- No overtime required.
- Higher average inventory.
- Uses inventory to absorb demand fluctuations.
- Requires accurate advance production planning.

**Chase Demand:**
- Lower inventory holding cost.
- Production follows actual demand more closely.
- Requires 3,000 units of overtime capacity in March.
- Higher production cost under the assumed overtime rate.
- Greater exposure to capacity constraints.

**Important analytical insight:**

The level strategy is the lower-cost option in this scenario, but that does not automatically make it the best strategy for every company.

For example, if the product has a short shelf life, inventory holding costs are significantly higher, or forecast accuracy is poor, the chase strategy may become more attractive.

Likewise, if overtime is unavailable in March, the chase strategy as currently designed is not feasible.

### 7.7 What If the Company Uses a Hybrid Strategy?

Suppose the company wants to reduce the March capacity requirement without maintaining as much inventory as the level strategy.

It could:
- Produce 10,000 units per month as a base level.
- Build additional inventory in January and February.
- Use that inventory during March.
- Use a smaller amount of overtime if necessary.

This illustrates why hybrid strategies are important.

The company is not restricted to choosing only one pure strategy.

It can combine inventory, stable production, and flexible capacity to achieve a better overall result.

## 8. Business Perspective and Supply Planning Decisions in S&OP

Ito ang pinakamahalagang part para sa future role mo as an S&OP Analyst.

Hindi lang tayo nagko-compute ng production requirements. Ang responsibility ng S&OP analyst ay tumulong sa business na maintindihan ang consequences ng bawat supply planning decision.

### 8.1 How an S&OP Analyst Evaluates Supply Strategies

Kapag may demand forecast na, kailangan mong sagutin ang following questions:

| Evaluation Area | Questions to Ask |
|---|---|
| Demand | How much supply is required per month? |
| Inventory | How much usable inventory is available? |
| Production | How much should we manufacture? |
| Capacity | Can existing capacity support the requirement? |
| Materials | Are raw materials available on time? |
| Bottlenecks | Which resources may restrict output? |
| Lead Time | Can production meet customer delivery dates? |
| Cost | Which strategy has the lowest total relevant cost? |
| Customer Service | Can we meet customer commitments? |
| Risk | What happens if demand increases or supply decreases? |

A good S&OP analyst evaluates multiple scenarios rather than presenting only one production proposal.

### 8.2 Supply Review: What Should You Present?

During Supply Review, the objective is to understand whether the supply organization can support the demand plan.

You should be prepared to present:

**A. Demand versus Supply**

| Month | Demand | Available Supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 8,000 | 13,000 | +5,000 |
| February | 12,000 | 12,000 | 0 |
| March | 15,000 | 11,000 | -4,000 |
| April | 9,000 | 11,000 | +2,000 |

Positive gap means excess supply, while negative gap means supply shortage.

**B. Capacity Utilization**

Identify which months are approaching or exceeding available capacity.

**C. Inventory Projection**

Determine whether inventory will increase, decrease, or reach critical levels.

**D. Supply Constraints**

Identify the reasons behind supply limitations:
- Machine capacity.
- Manpower.
- Raw material shortages.
- Supplier delays.
- Production yield.
- Bottleneck operations.

**E. Alternative Supply Scenarios**

Present possible solutions with corresponding costs and operational implications.

### 8.3 Pre-S&OP: How to Present Recommendations

During Pre-S&OP, cross-functional teams review the proposed demand and supply balance and prepare recommendations for management decisions.

Your recommendation should answer four questions:

1. What is the problem?
2. What are the available alternatives?
3. What are the financial and operational implications?
4. What action do you recommend?

For example:

**Illustrative S&OP recommendation**

**Issue:** March demand exceeds normal production capacity by 3,000 units.

**Alternative 1:** Build inventory ahead of March through level production.

**Alternative 2:** Maintain chase production and use 3,000 units of overtime capacity.

**Financial comparison:**
- Level strategy: ₱445,500.
- Chase strategy: ₱459,000.

**Recommendation:** Adopt level production under the current assumptions because it provides lower relevant cost, stable utilization, and sufficient projected inventory to satisfy March demand.

**Conditions:** Validate forecast accuracy, material availability, storage capacity, and inventory obsolescence risk before final approval.

Notice that the recommendation includes not only the preferred strategy but also its assumptions and conditions.

### 8.4 Balancing Operational Feasibility with Financial Objectives

A strategy can be financially attractive but operationally impossible.

Example:

The finance team prefers lower inventory because it reduces working capital.

However, the supply team knows that production lead time is long and March demand is expected to increase.

If the company reduces inventory too aggressively, it may experience customer shortages.

On the other hand, producing too much inventory may increase:
- Warehousing costs.
- Working capital requirements.
- Inventory aging.
- Obsolescence risk.

The S&OP process helps different departments reach a balanced business decision.

| Department | Primary Concern |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer demand and delivery commitments |
| Operations | Production capacity and operational stability |
| Supply Chain | Inventory and material availability |
| Procurement | Supplier capacity and purchasing costs |
| Finance | Working capital, costs, and profitability |
| Management | Overall business performance and strategic priorities |

**Professional mindset:** Your job is not to maximize production, minimize inventory, or achieve 100% capacity utilization at all costs.

Your job is to help the organization achieve the best overall business outcome.

## 9. Key Takeaways

### 9.1 Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What You Should Remember |
|---|---|
| Supply Planning | Determines how the company will meet expected demand. |
| Production Planning | Determines what and how much the factory will manufacture. |
| MTS | Manufacture based on forecast. |
| MTO | Manufacture after receiving an order. |
| ATO | Prepare components in advance, assemble after receiving an order. |
| Level Production | Maintain relatively stable production output. |
| Chase Demand | Adjust production to follow demand. |
| Hybrid Strategy | Combine stable production and flexible adjustments. |
| Inventory Build-Ahead | Produce early to prepare for future demand. |
| Production Smoothing | Minimize unnecessary production fluctuations. |
| Supply Flexibility | Adjust resources and operations to respond to changing requirements. |

### 9.2 Important Formulas

| Formula | Purpose |
|---|---|
| \(P=D+EI-BI\) | Required production quantity |
| \(EI=BI+P-D\) | Projected ending inventory |
| \(P_{\text{level}}=\frac{Total\ Demand+EI-BI}{N}\) | Average level production |
| \(P_t=D_t+EI_t-BI_t\) | Period-specific chase production |
| \(\frac{Output}{Capacity}\times100\) | Capacity utilization |
| \(S-D\) | Supply-demand gap |
| \(\frac{Inventory}{Average\ Daily\ Demand}\) | Days of Supply |

### 9.3 Professional-Level Understanding

Remember these five principles:

1. **Inventory can compensate for limited production capacity**, provided it is available at the right time and place.
2. **Flexible production can reduce inventory requirements**, but may increase manufacturing costs.
3. **Supply strategies involve trade-offs.** There is no universally best strategy.
4. **Capacity utilization above 100% of normal capacity signals a need for additional capacity or another supply adjustment.**
5. **A good S&OP recommendation considers operational feasibility, financial impact, and customer service simultaneously.**
