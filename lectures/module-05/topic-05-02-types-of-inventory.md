# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

### 1.1 What are we going to discuss?

In Topic 5.1, we established that inventory refers to materials, components, supplies, and finished products that a company holds to support its operations and meet customer demand.

Now, let's go one step further.

Hindi lahat ng inventory ay pare-pareho ang purpose.

For example, imagine a manufacturing company that produces electronic components.

It maintains:
- Raw materials waiting to enter production.
- Materials currently being processed.
- Finished products waiting for customer orders.
- Spare parts used to maintain production machines.
- Additional inventory reserved to protect against unexpected demand.
- Materials currently being transported from suppliers.

Lahat ng iyan ay inventory, pero magkakaiba ang kanilang purpose.

This is why companies classify inventory into different types.

### 1.2 Why is inventory classification important?

Imagine na nakita ng S&OP analyst na mayroon ang company ng 10,000 units of inventory.

Is that enough?

Not necessarily.

What if:
- 4,000 units are raw materials?
- 3,000 units are still undergoing production?
- 2,000 units are finished goods?
- 1,000 units are spare parts?

Although the company has 10,000 units of inventory, hindi ibig sabihin na mayroon itong 10,000 units available to fulfill customer orders.

**Inventory classification helps companies understand what they have, where it is, and why they are holding it.**

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

Let's use a simple manufacturing scenario.

Imagine you own a factory producing electric fans.

Para makagawa ng isang electric fan, kailangan mo ng:

- Plastic resin for the housing.
- Electric motor.
- Copper wire.
- Screws.
- Power cord.
- Packaging materials.

Ngayon, may iba't ibang stages ang materials mo.

**Stage 1: Before production**

Bumili ka ng 1,000 electric motors from a supplier.

Nasa warehouse pa sila at naghihintay gamitin.

These are Raw Materials or components inventory.

**Stage 2: During production**

Kinuha ng production team ang motors at ikinabit sa fan assembly.

Hindi pa tapos ang electric fan.

These are part of Work-in-Process inventory.

**Stage 3: After production**

Natapos na ang 800 electric fans at inilagay sa finished goods warehouse.

These are Finished Goods inventory.

**Stage 4: Supporting production**

May stock ka rin ng machine bearings, lubricants, cleaning materials, and replacement belts.

Hindi sila directly nagiging bahagi ng electric fan, pero kailangan sila para tumakbo ang factory.

These are MRO inventory.

Notice something?

The company maintains inventory for different reasons:

1. To manufacture products.
2. To protect against unexpected problems.
3. To maintain continuous operations.
4. To prepare for future demand.
5. To cover materials while they are being transported.

Iyan ang foundation ng inventory classification.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

There are three major classification dimensions that we need to understand.

## Three dimensions of inventory classification

**1. By Production Stage**

Where is the inventory in the manufacturing process?

RM → WIP → FG

**2. By Function or Purpose**

Why is the inventory being maintained?

Cycle Stock, Safety Stock, Pipeline Inventory, etc.

**3. By Demand Characteristics**

What determines the demand for the inventory?

Independent Demand vs. Dependent Demand

These are different classification dimensions.

Hindi sila mutually exclusive.

For example, a finished product can simultaneously be:

- Finished Goods by production stage.
- Safety Stock by inventory purpose.
- Independent Demand Inventory by demand characteristics.

This distinction is extremely important.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Inventory Classification Fundamentals

Inventory classification is the process of grouping inventory items according to their characteristics, production stage, purpose, demand behavior, or other relevant attributes.

### Why do companies classify inventory?

There are five major reasons.

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Planning | Determine what materials need to be purchased or produced. |
| Monitoring | Identify inventory levels and movements. |
| Control | Prevent shortages, excess inventory, and unnecessary stock accumulation. |
| Prioritization | Identify which materials require more attention. |
| Decision-making | Support procurement, production, and customer fulfillment decisions. |

For example:

Suppose a factory has:
- 5,000 kg of raw materials.
- 2,000 units of WIP.
- 3,000 units of finished goods.

Hindi mo puwedeng basta pagsamahin ang quantities na iyan dahil magkaiba ang units of measurement at operational purpose.

Kailangan mo silang i-classify properly.

## 4.2 Inventory by Production Stage

This classification identifies where inventory is located within the manufacturing process.

There are four common categories.

### A. Raw Materials (RM)

**Before production**

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang mga materials na binili ng company pero hindi pa ginagamit sa production.

Parang ingredients bago magluto.

For an electric fan manufacturer:

- Plastic resin.
- Copper wire.
- Steel sheets.
- Electric motors.
- Screws.
- Electronic components.

**Technical explanation:**

Raw Materials Inventory consists of purchased materials and components that have not yet been issued into the production process.

These materials are maintained to ensure production continuity.

**Why do companies maintain RM inventory?**

1. To ensure materials are available when production needs them.
2. To protect against supplier delays.
3. To accommodate procurement lead times.
4. To support production schedules.

**Manufacturing example:**

A factory produces 10,000 electric fans per month.

It needs 500 kg of barium titanate powder per month.

If the supplier requires 30 days to deliver, the company may maintain sufficient raw material inventory to support production while waiting for replenishment.

**Important distinction:**

Not every purchased item is necessarily raw material.

A machine replacement bearing, for example, is generally MRO inventory rather than raw material because it does not become part of the manufactured product.

### B. Work-in-Process (WIP)

**During production**

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito naman ang mga products na nagsimula nang gawin pero hindi pa tapos.

Parang pagkain na niluluto pa lang.

Hindi na siya ordinaryong ingredient, pero hindi pa rin siya ready-to-serve.

For manufacturing:

- Components undergoing assembly.
- Materials undergoing casting.
- Semi-finished products waiting for firing.
- Products waiting for electrical testing.

**Technical explanation:**

Work-in-Process Inventory refers to materials and products that have entered the production process but have not yet completed all required manufacturing operations.

WIP may include:

- Direct materials already issued to production.
- Direct labor applied.
- Allocated manufacturing overhead.

**Why do companies have WIP?**

Because manufacturing requires time.

Hindi instant ang conversion ng raw materials into finished products.

For example:

| Production Stage | Inventory Status |
|---|---|
| Casting | WIP |
| Stacking | WIP |
| Cutting | WIP |
| Firing | WIP |
| Electrical testing | WIP |
| Sorting | WIP |
| Final inspection completed | Potentially FG |

The exact classification depends on the company's inventory accounting and production reporting rules.

**Important concept: WIP accumulation**

Imagine:

- Casting produces 1,000 units/day.
- Stacking processes 800 units/day.

Every day, casting produces 200 more units than stacking can process.

Result: WIP accumulates between the two operations.

This is an example of a production bottleneck.

We'll discuss the relationship between WIP and inventory accumulation later in this lesson.

### C. Finished Goods (FG)

**After production**

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito na ang mga products na tapos nang gawin at ready na for sale or customer delivery.

Parang pagkain na luto na at puwede nang ihain.

Examples:

- Completed MLCC products.
- Fully assembled electric fans.
- Completed automotive components.
- Finished electronic devices.

**Technical explanation:**

Finished Goods Inventory refers to completed products that have passed the required production and quality requirements and are available for sale or customer fulfillment.

**Why do companies maintain FG inventory?**

- To fulfill customer orders quickly.
- To support forecasted demand.
- To protect against demand fluctuations.
- To reduce customer delivery lead time.

**Example:**

A factory forecasts monthly customer demand of 50,000 units.

However, its production schedule may not always match daily customer orders.

The company can produce products ahead of actual orders and maintain finished goods inventory.

This allows the company to fulfill customer requirements without waiting for new production every time.

### D. Maintenance, Repair, and Operations (MRO) Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang mga materials and supplies na ginagamit para mapatakbo at mapanatili ang factory.

Hindi sila directly ginagamit para maging final product, pero kailangan sila para gumana ang production operation.

Examples:

- Machine lubricants.
- Replacement bearings.
- Belts.
- Filters.
- Cleaning chemicals.
- Protective gloves.
- Maintenance tools.

**Technical explanation:**

MRO Inventory consists of supplies, spare parts, consumables, and maintenance materials used to support manufacturing and business operations.

**Why is MRO important?**

Imagine a production machine suddenly stops because its bearing is damaged.

The company has enough raw materials and customer orders, but production cannot continue.

If the replacement bearing is unavailable, the machine downtime may increase.

This is why MRO inventory supports operational reliability.

**Important distinction:**

| Inventory | Classification |
|---|---|
| Copper wire used in the product | Raw Material |
| Copper wire being processed | WIP |
| Completed electrical component | Finished Goods |
| Machine replacement bearing | MRO |

### Quick Comparison: Production Stage

| Type | Condition | Main Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| RM | Not yet used in production | Supply production with materials |
| WIP | Currently undergoing production | Support manufacturing flow |
| FG | Production completed | Fulfill customer demand |
| MRO | Supports factory operations | Maintain operational continuity |

## 4.3 Inventory by Function or Purpose

Now, let's introduce another perspective.

Earlier, we classified inventory based on where it is in production.

This time, we classify it based on WHY the company is maintaining it.

There are five important types.

### A. Cycle Stock

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang normal inventory na ginagamit ng company habang naghihintay sa next replenishment.

Imagine na umuubos ka ng 10 kg of rice every week.

Hindi ka bibili ng bagong rice every time na kukuha ka ng isang cup.

Bumibili ka ng stock na tatagal hanggang sa next purchase.

That is the basic idea of cycle stock.

**Technical explanation:**

Cycle Stock is the inventory maintained to satisfy expected demand between successive replenishment orders.

It exists because companies usually purchase or produce inventory in batches rather than continuously.

**Example:**

A manufacturer consumes 1,000 kg of material per week.

It orders 2,000 kg every two weeks.

Assuming constant consumption and immediate replenishment at the start of each cycle:

| Time | Inventory |
|---|---:|
| Start | 2,000 kg |
| End of Week 1 | 1,000 kg |
| End of Week 2 | 0 kg |
| Next replenishment | 2,000 kg |

The inventory gradually decreases until the next replenishment.

**Important concept:**

Under the simplified assumption of steady demand and instantaneous replenishment:

\[
Average\ Cycle\ Stock=\frac{Q}{2}
\]

Where:

- \(Q\) = Replenishment order quantity.
- Average cycle stock = Average inventory held during the replenishment cycle.

For our example:

\[
=\frac{2,000}{2}=1,000\ kg
\]

This is the average cycle stock, assuming no safety stock and no overlapping replenishment cycles.

### B. Safety Stock

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito naman ang extra inventory na iniingatan ng company just in case something unexpected happens.

For example:

You normally consume 1,000 kg of raw material per week.

But what if:
- Supplier delivery is delayed?
- Actual demand suddenly increases?
- Production consumes more material than expected?

You need additional inventory to protect your operation.

That extra inventory is safety stock.

**Technical explanation:**

Safety Stock is additional inventory maintained above expected requirements to protect against uncertainty in demand, supply, or replenishment lead time.

**Why does it exist?**

Because actual business conditions do not always follow the plan.

Consider this:

| Situation | Expected | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Weekly material demand | 1,000 kg | 1,200 kg |
| Supplier lead time | 2 weeks | 3 weeks |

The company may consume materials faster than expected while waiting longer for replenishment.

Without sufficient safety stock, it risks a stockout.

**Important distinction:**

Cycle stock and safety stock have different purposes.

- Cycle Stock = Covers normal expected demand between replenishments.
- Safety Stock = Protects against uncertainty.

A company may have both at the same time.

### C. Pipeline or In-Transit Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang inventory na binili na ng company pero nasa transportation process pa.

For example:

Nag-order ka ng 5,000 kg of raw materials from a supplier in Japan.

Na-dispatch na ang materials, pero nasa barko pa papuntang Philippines.

Hindi pa physically available sa warehouse mo, pero bahagi na ito ng supply pipeline.

**Technical explanation:**

Pipeline Inventory refers to inventory that is moving through the supply chain or undergoing transportation between locations.

It may include:

- Supplier-to-factory shipments.
- Factory-to-distribution-center shipments.
- Inter-warehouse transfers.
- Goods awaiting transportation or transfer processing.

**Why does pipeline inventory exist?**

Because materials and products require time to move between locations.

Even if the supplier has already shipped the materials, the company cannot necessarily use them immediately.

**Example:**

A factory consumes 500 units per day.

Its replenishment shipment takes 10 days to arrive.

Under steady consumption and a continuous-flow assumption:

\[
Pipeline\ Inventory = Daily\ Demand \times Lead\ Time
\]

\[
=500 \times 10
\]

\[
=5,000\ units
\]

The company has approximately 5,000 units tied up in the replenishment pipeline.

This is also known as *pipeline stock*.

**Important distinction:**

Pipeline inventory is not automatically available for immediate production.

Its usability depends on shipment status, ownership, receiving requirements, quality inspection, and other operational conditions.

### D. Anticipation or Seasonal Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang inventory na iniipon in advance dahil alam ng company na may paparating na increase in demand or potential supply disruption.

For example:

A company manufactures electric fans.

Alam nitong mas mataas ang demand tuwing summer.

Instead of waiting for summer before increasing production, it builds inventory ahead of the expected demand increase.

**Technical explanation:**

Anticipation Inventory is inventory accumulated in advance of expected increases in demand, planned events, seasonal fluctuations, or anticipated supply constraints.

**Examples:**

- Air conditioners before summer.
- School supplies before school opening.
- Holiday products before Christmas.
- Agricultural equipment before planting season.

**Why maintain anticipation inventory?**

Because production and procurement capacity may not be sufficient to respond immediately when demand increases.

Building inventory ahead of time helps companies prepare for predictable demand patterns.

**Example:**

| Month | Forecast Demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 10,000 |
| February | 12,000 |
| March | 15,000 |
| April | 25,000 |
| May | 30,000 |

The company may increase production during January to March so it can build finished goods inventory before peak demand in April and May.

### E. Decoupling Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang inventory na inilalagay between production processes para hindi agad tumigil ang buong production line kapag may problem sa isang stage.

Imagine a production line with three processes:

Casting → Stacking → Cutting

What if the stacking machine suddenly stops?

If there is no inventory between casting and stacking, the casting process may also need to stop.

But if there is some WIP inventory between these two processes, casting can continue temporarily.

**Technical explanation:**

Decoupling Inventory is inventory maintained between successive operations or stages to reduce the dependency of one process on another.

It acts as a buffer that allows individual processes to operate with some degree of independence.

**Why is it important?**

It helps:
- Reduce the impact of machine breakdowns.
- Prevent immediate stoppage of upstream operations.
- Absorb temporary production rate differences.
- Improve manufacturing flow.

**Example:**

| Process | Daily Capacity |
|---|---:|
| Casting | 1,000 units |
| Stacking | 900 units |
| Cutting | 950 units |

Casting produces 1,000 units daily, while stacking can process only 900.

A buffer of WIP inventory may develop between casting and stacking.

However, this does not mean that unlimited WIP is beneficial.

Too much decoupling inventory can hide production bottlenecks and increase holding costs.

### Comparison: Inventory by Function

| Type | Why it exists | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle Stock | Normal replenishment | Regular material supply |
| Safety Stock | Protection against uncertainty | Extra material for delays |
| Pipeline Inventory | Materials in transit | Shipment from Japan |
| Anticipation Inventory | Prepare for future demand | Summer products |
| Decoupling Inventory | Separate production operations | WIP between machines |

**Important professional insight:**

These five categories can overlap.

For example, a finished goods product may be both:
- Cycle Stock because it supports normal customer demand.
- Safety Stock because some quantity is reserved for unexpected demand.

The distinction depends on its purpose, not merely its physical location.

## 4.4 Inventory by Demand Characteristics

This classification answers a different question:

**What determines how much inventory we need?**

There are two major categories.

### A. Independent Demand Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito ang inventory na ang demand ay directly driven by customer requirements or market demand.

For example:

Customers order 5,000 units of an electronic component.

The demand for those finished products comes from customers.

The company needs to forecast how many units customers will purchase.

**Technical explanation:**

Independent Demand refers to demand that is not directly calculated from the demand for another item within the same production structure.

It is usually influenced by:
- Customer orders.
- Market demand.
- Sales forecasts.
- Historical sales patterns.
- Market trends.

**Examples:**

- Finished goods.
- Replacement products sold to customers.
- Aftermarket spare parts sold independently.

**How is it planned?**

Common planning approaches include:
- Demand forecasting.
- Historical consumption analysis.
- Statistical forecasting.
- Customer order management.

Independent demand is typically the starting point of demand planning.

### B. Dependent Demand Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Ito naman ang inventory na ang requirement ay nakadepende sa ibang product.

For example:

A customer orders 1,000 electric fans.

Each electric fan requires:
- 1 motor.
- 1 power cord.
- 3 blades.
- 4 screws.

Hindi mo kailangang mag-forecast independently kung ilang motors ang kailangan.

You can calculate the motor requirement based on the electric fan production requirement.

**Technical explanation:**

Dependent Demand refers to demand for components, subassemblies, and raw materials that is derived from the production requirements of another item.

It is commonly calculated using:

- Bill of Materials (BOM).
- Master Production Schedule (MPS).
- Material Requirements Planning (MRP).

**Example:**

A company plans to manufacture 1,000 electric fans.

Its BOM requires:

| Component | Quantity per Fan |
|---|---:|
| Motor | 1 |
| Power cord | 1 |
| Fan blades | 3 |
| Screws | 4 |

The dependent demand becomes:

| Component | Calculation | Requirement |
|---|---:|---:|
| Motor | 1,000 × 1 | 1,000 |
| Power cord | 1,000 × 1 | 1,000 |
| Fan blades | 1,000 × 3 | 3,000 |
| Screws | 1,000 × 4 | 4,000 |

This is the fundamental principle of dependent demand planning.

### Independent vs. Dependent Demand

| Category | Independent Demand | Dependent Demand |
|---|---|---|
| Demand source | Customer or market | Production requirements |
| Common items | Finished goods | Components and materials |
| Planning method | Forecasting | MRP/BOM calculation |
| Example | Electric fan | Motor required for fan production |

### Important: The relationship between finished goods and components

Let's say the company forecasts 5,000 units of finished goods demand.

That forecast becomes the basis for calculating component requirements.

**Demand explosion through the BOM**

- **Finished Goods:** 5,000 units (Independent Demand)
- **Motor:** 5,000 units (1 per product)
- **Fan blades:** 15,000 units (3 per product)
- **Power cord:** 5,000 units (1 per product)
- **Screws:** 20,000 units (4 per product)

This process is called **Demand Explosion**.

It translates finished goods requirements into the quantities of materials and components needed to manufacture them.

One important qualification: these are gross component requirements. Actual net procurement or production requirements must also consider usable inventory, scheduled receipts, scrap, and other planning factors.

## 4.5 Inventory Movement and Relationships

Now that we understand the different classifications, let's see how inventory moves throughout a manufacturing operation.

### A. The manufacturing inventory flow

**Supplier Procurement**
(Purchase Orders and Supplier Deliveries)

↓

**Raw Materials (RM)**
(Received and stored materials)

↓

*Material issuance*

↓

**Work-in-Process (WIP)**
(Materials undergoing manufacturing)

↓

*Production completion and quality approval*

↓

**Finished Goods (FG)**
(Completed products ready for fulfillment)

↓

**Customer Delivery**
(Shipment and fulfillment)

**MRO Inventory** supports machines and operations throughout the manufacturing process. It does not normally become part of the finished product.

This is the general inventory transformation process.

However, real manufacturing operations are more complicated because inventory can exist at multiple stages simultaneously.

### B. How inventory accumulates across production stages

Let's use a simplified manufacturing example.

Suppose a factory has:

| Production Stage | Daily Output/Capacity |
|---|---:|
| Casting | 1,000 units |
| Stacking | 900 units |
| Cutting | 850 units |
| Firing | 900 units |
| Sorting | 850 units |

Assume each stage starts with no WIP and the stated quantities represent daily completed output.

The production flow might look like this:

- Casting produces 1,000 units.
- Stacking processes 900 units.
- Cutting processes 850 units.
- Firing processes 900 units, but only 850 units are available from cutting.
- Sorting processes 850 units.

The inventory accumulation becomes:

| Location | Daily WIP Accumulation |
|---|---:|
| Before stacking | 100 units |
| Before cutting | 50 units |
| Before firing | 0 units |
| Before sorting | 0 units |

The first process produces more than the second process can handle.

Therefore, WIP accumulates between casting and stacking.

**Business interpretation:**

Even though casting has higher output, that does not automatically mean the factory has higher finished goods output.

The actual system throughput is constrained by the flow of all production stages.

This is why S&OP analysts should not look only at total production capacity. They must understand where inventory accumulates and which operation constrains the flow.

### C. What affects inventory composition?

There are three major business activities.

| Activity | Impact on Inventory |
|---|---|
| Procurement | Increases raw materials and inbound pipeline inventory. |
| Production | Converts RM into WIP and eventually FG. |
| Customer demand | Reduces available FG through shipments. |

Let's examine a simplified example.

A company starts with:

- RM: 5,000 units.
- WIP: 2,000 units.
- FG: 3,000 units.

During the month:

- Receives 4,000 units of RM.
- Issues 3,000 units into production.
- Completes 2,000 units of production.
- Ships 2,500 units to customers.

Assuming no scrap, adjustments, or other movements:

| Inventory | Beginning | Movement | Ending |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RM | 5,000 | +4,000 - 3,000 | 6,000 |
| WIP | 2,000 | +3,000 - 2,000 | 3,000 |
| FG | 3,000 | +2,000 - 2,500 | 2,500 |

Notice that inventory is constantly changing its composition.

The company received materials, transformed them through production, and shipped finished products.

This is the basic inventory flow that S&OP needs to understand.

## 5.2 The same inventory can have multiple classifications

This is one of the most important concepts in Topic 5.2.

Consider BT powder.

**Example: BT Powder**

- **Production Stage: Raw Material.** The BT powder is stored in the warehouse and has not entered production.
- **Function: Cycle Stock.** The BT powder supports normal production consumption between replenishments.
- **Demand Characteristic: Dependent Demand.** Its requirement is calculated from the planned production quantity and material consumption.

Now imagine that another quantity of BT powder is specifically maintained as a buffer against supplier delays.

It is still:

- Raw Material by production stage.
- Safety Stock by function.
- Dependent Demand by demand characteristics.

Same material. Different classification dimensions.

This is exactly how professional inventory analysis works.

# 6. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

For this topic, we have already introduced three useful calculations.

### 6.1 Average Cycle Stock

\[
Average\ Cycle\ Stock = \frac{Q}{2}
\]

Where:

- \(Q\) = Replenishment quantity.
- Unit = pieces, kg, liters, etc.

**Example:**

A company orders 4,000 kg every month.

\[
Average\ Cycle\ Stock = \frac{4,000}{2}
\]

\[
=2,000\ kg
\]

Interpretation: Under the simplified assumptions, the average cycle stock held during the replenishment cycle is 2,000 kg.

### 6.2 Pipeline Inventory

\[
Pipeline\ Inventory = D \times L
\]

Where:

- \(D\) = Average demand per unit of time.
- \(L\) = Replenishment lead time in the same time unit.

**Example:**

Daily demand = 300 units.

Lead time = 15 days.

\[
Pipeline\ Inventory = 300 \times 15
\]

\[
=4,500\ units
\]

Interpretation: Approximately 4,500 units are tied up in the replenishment pipeline under steady demand.

### 6.3 Dependent Demand

\[
Component\ Requirement =
FG\ Production\ Requirement
\times
BOM\ Quantity
\]

**Example:**

Planned production = 8,000 units.

Each finished product requires 2 components.

\[
=8,000 \times 2
\]

\[
=16,000\ components
\]

Interpretation: The production plan generates a gross requirement of 16,000 components before considering existing inventory and scheduled receipts.

### Common Calculation Errors

1. Mixing different units of time, such as daily demand multiplied by monthly lead time.
2. Treating pipeline inventory as immediately available warehouse stock.
3. Confusing gross component requirements with net material requirements.
4. Treating safety stock as normal cycle stock.
5. Counting the same inventory twice under overlapping classifications.

# 7. BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE AND S&OP DECISION-MAKING

Now let's connect all these concepts to actual S&OP activities.

An S&OP analyst does not simply ask:

"How much inventory do we have?"

A better question is:

"What inventory do we have, where is it located, what purpose does it serve, and can it support our demand plan?"

### 7.1 Impact on Supply Planning

Different inventory types affect supply planning differently.

| Inventory Type | S&OP Consideration |
|---|---|
| RM | Can we procure enough materials for planned production? |
| WIP | Are there bottlenecks or production delays? |
| FG | Can we fulfill customer demand? |
| MRO | Are maintenance resources sufficient to sustain operations? |
| Cycle Stock | Is the replenishment cycle appropriate? |
| Safety Stock | Is the protection level sufficient? |
| Pipeline | When will incoming materials actually arrive? |
| Anticipation | Should we build inventory ahead of expected demand? |
| Decoupling | Are buffers supporting production flow effectively? |

### 7.2 Impact on Production Decisions

Consider this situation.

A company forecasts 100,000 units of customer demand next month.

Its current inventory situation is:

| Inventory | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Finished Goods | 30,000 |
| WIP | 20,000 |
| Raw Materials | Enough for 40,000 units of production |

A beginner might conclude:

"We have 90,000 units of inventory. We only need to produce 10,000 more."

But this is incorrect.

Why?

Because:
- FG is already completed.
- WIP still needs to pass through remaining production operations.
- RM is not yet a finished product and must be converted through production.

The actual supply assessment must consider production completion timing, material availability, capacity, quality yield, and customer delivery dates.

This is why inventory classification matters.

### 7.3 Impact on Customer Service

Imagine a customer needs 10,000 units by November 30.

The company has:
- 3,000 units of available FG.
- 5,000 units of WIP.
- Materials for another 5,000 units.

Does the company have enough inventory?

Potentially, yes, but it depends on whether the WIP can be completed, whether additional production is feasible, and whether the materials can be processed before the required delivery date.

Inventory quantity alone does not establish customer fulfillment capability.

### 7.4 Common Inventory Classification Mistakes

| Mistake | Possible Consequence |
|---|---|
| Treating all inventory as available FG | Overestimating customer fulfillment capability |
| Ignoring WIP accumulation | Missing production bottlenecks |
| Counting pipeline stock as available warehouse stock | Incorrect supply position |
| Mixing safety stock with cycle stock | Poor replenishment decisions |
| Treating all component demand as forecast demand | Incorrect material planning |
| Ignoring MRO inventory | Unexpected equipment downtime |
| Building excessive decoupling inventory | Higher inventory costs and hidden process inefficiencies |

### 7.5 What should an S&OP analyst investigate?

When reviewing inventory, ask:

1. What is the current inventory by production stage?
2. How much is available for customer fulfillment?
3. How much is still undergoing production?
4. How much is committed to existing customer orders?
5. How much is reserved as safety stock?
6. What materials are currently in transit?
7. Are there materials that could become bottlenecks?
8. Is inventory accumulating because of production constraints?
9. Are future demand increases already reflected in the inventory plan?

These questions help translate inventory data into meaningful supply planning decisions.

# 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Let's consolidate the entire discussion.

## 8.1 The three classification dimensions

| Dimension | Main Question | Categories |
|---|---|---|
| Production Stage | Where is the inventory? | RM, WIP, FG, MRO |
| Function/Purpose | Why is the inventory maintained? | Cycle, Safety, Pipeline, Anticipation, Decoupling |
| Demand Characteristics | What determines its demand? | Independent, Dependent |

## 8.2 Important terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| RM | Materials not yet used in production |
| WIP | Products undergoing production |
| FG | Completed products ready for fulfillment |
| MRO | Materials supporting operations and maintenance |
| Cycle Stock | Normal inventory between replenishments |
| Safety Stock | Additional inventory protecting against uncertainty |
| Pipeline Inventory | Inventory moving through the supply chain |
| Anticipation Inventory | Inventory built ahead of expected demand |
| Decoupling Inventory | Buffer inventory between production stages |
| Independent Demand | Demand driven by customers or markets |
| Dependent Demand | Demand derived from another item's requirements |
| BOM | Structure defining materials and quantities needed to manufacture a product |
| Demand Explosion | Conversion of finished goods requirements into component requirements |

## 8.3 Five concepts you must remember

1. Inventory can be classified using different dimensions.
2. RM, WIP, and FG describe inventory according to production stage.
3. Cycle Stock, Safety Stock, and other functional categories explain why inventory is maintained.
4. Independent and Dependent Demand describe how inventory requirements are determined.
5. Inventory quantity does not automatically mean inventory availability.

**The most important principle:**

An S&OP analyst must understand not just how much inventory exists, but also its stage, purpose, demand characteristics, and actual availability.
