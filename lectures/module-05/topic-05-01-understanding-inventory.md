# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 What exactly is inventory?

Let's use a simple example.

Imagine you own a small bakery.

Every morning, customers buy bread. To operate your bakery, you need:
- Flour
- Sugar
- Eggs
- Butter
- Packaging materials
- Finished bread ready for selling

Would you purchase flour only after a customer orders bread?

Probably not.

You would maintain some flour, sugar, eggs, and other materials in advance.

You would also prepare some bread before customers arrive.

All these materials and products that you keep for future use or sale are considered inventory.

**In simple terms: Inventory is the collection of materials, products, and goods that a company keeps to support its operations or satisfy future customer demand.**

In manufacturing, inventory can exist at different stages:
- Before production.
- During production.
- After production.

Hindi lang finished products ang inventory. Kahit raw materials na hindi pa nagagamit ay inventory na rin.

## 2.2 Inventory vs. Stock vs. Available Supply

These three terms are sometimes used interchangeably in business conversations. However, they can have different meanings depending on the company's inventory system and planning terminology.

| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Inventory | The overall materials and products held by the company. |
| Stock | The quantity of a particular material or product held at a specific location or recorded in a system. |
| Available Supply | The quantity that can actually be used to satisfy demand or support operations. |

Let's use a manufacturing example.

Suppose your warehouse has 1,000 units of a particular raw material.

However:
- 100 units are damaged.
- 200 units are already allocated to another production order.
- 700 units are available for new requirements.

### Inventory breakdown

**Total recorded inventory: 1,000 units**

| Inventory status | Quantity | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Available for use | 700 | 70% |
| Allocated inventory | 200 | 20% |
| Damaged inventory | 100 | 10% |
| **Total** | **1,000** | **100%** |

Notice something important:

Although the company has 1,000 units recorded in its inventory, it cannot use all 1,000 units to satisfy new production requirements.

This illustrates an important principle:

**Having inventory does not automatically mean having usable or available inventory.**

In actual planning systems, availability may also depend on quality status, reservations, location, material restrictions, and expected receipts.

## 2.3 The Role of Inventory in Balancing Demand and Supply

Let's simplify this.

- Demand = What customers need.
- Supply = What the company can provide.
- Inventory = Products and materials already available to help bridge the difference.

Suppose:

| Description | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 1,000 units |
| Current production output | 800 units |
| Beginning finished goods inventory | 300 units |

The company has:

800 + 300 = 1,100 units of potential supply.

It can satisfy the 1,000-unit customer demand and retain 100 units as ending inventory, assuming all 1,100 units are usable and available within the required period.

Without the beginning inventory of 300 units, the company would have a shortage of 200 units.

This is why inventory acts as a buffer between demand and supply.

# 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the professional terminology used in inventory management and S&OP.

## 3.1 Formal Definition of Inventory

In supply chain management, inventory refers to the stock of materials, components, work-in-process, finished products, and other goods held by an organization for production, consumption, distribution, or sale.

Inventory represents resources that have been acquired or produced but have not yet been consumed, transformed, or delivered to their intended destination.

There are two important perspectives:

**Operational perspective**

Inventory ensures that materials and products are available when required.

**Financial perspective**

Inventory represents money invested in goods that have not yet been converted into sales revenue or consumed in operations.

For example:

A company purchases ₱5 million worth of raw materials.

That ₱5 million is now tied up in inventory until those materials are consumed, processed, or otherwise disposed of.

This means inventory is both an operational resource and a financial investment.

## 3.2 Inventory as a Supply Chain Buffer

A buffer is something that protects a process from interruptions or differences between what is needed and what is available.

In manufacturing, inventory provides protection against:
- Demand fluctuations.
- Supplier delivery delays.
- Production interruptions.
- Transportation problems.
- Differences in production and consumption rates.

Consider this situation:

A manufacturing facility consumes 500 kg of raw material every day.

Its supplier normally delivers every five days.

If the company purchases exactly 2,500 kg every five days, what happens if the supplier experiences a two-day delay?

The manufacturing facility may run out of materials.

Maintaining additional inventory can protect production from this uncertainty.

However, maintaining too much inventory creates additional costs.

This introduces one of the central principles of inventory management:

**Inventory management is about maintaining sufficient inventory to support operations without unnecessarily tying up company resources.**

# 4. Detailed Discussion

## 4.1 Purpose and Functions of Inventory

Why do companies deliberately keep inventory instead of maintaining only what they immediately need?

There are several reasons.

### A. Meeting Customer Demand

Companies maintain finished goods inventory so they can fulfill customer orders without waiting for a new production cycle.

Example:

A customer orders 500 units today.

If the company already has 500 available finished goods, it can fulfill the order immediately.

Without finished goods inventory, the company may need to manufacture the products first.

This could increase customer waiting time.

**Business benefit:** Better customer service and shorter order fulfillment time.

### B. Supporting Continuous Production

Manufacturing operations require a continuous flow of materials.

Imagine a production line that requires:
- Raw material A.
- Raw material B.
- Raw material C.

Even if Materials A and B are available, production may stop if Material C is missing.

Therefore, companies maintain sufficient raw material inventory to avoid unnecessary production interruptions.

**Business benefit:** Improved production continuity and utilization of available capacity.

### C. Managing Supply and Demand Variability

Variability refers to changes or uncertainty in normal operating conditions.

For example:

Demand forecast: 10,000 units.

Actual demand: 12,000 units.

The additional 2,000 units represent demand above the forecast.

If the company has sufficient inventory, it may satisfy the additional demand without immediately increasing production.

The same principle applies to supplier variability.

If a supplier normally delivers in seven days but occasionally takes ten days, additional inventory can protect operations.

**Business benefit:** Better resilience against uncertainty.

### D. Protecting Against Delays and Disruptions

Suppose your supplier experiences:
- Equipment breakdown.
- Labor shortages.
- Transportation delays.
- Raw material shortages.
- Natural disasters.

Your company may be unable to receive the required materials on schedule.

Inventory can provide temporary protection while the supply issue is resolved.

**Business benefit:** Reduced risk of production stoppage and missed customer deliveries.

### E. Supporting Operational Flexibility

Inventory allows manufacturing companies to respond to operational changes.

For example:

A company normally produces Model A and Model B.

Unexpectedly, customer demand for Model A increases.

If the company already has some finished goods inventory of Model A, it can respond faster while adjusting its production schedule.

**Business benefit:** Greater flexibility in responding to changing business requirements.

## 4.2 Inventory Throughout the Supply Chain

Inventory does not exist only inside the warehouse.

It moves through different stages of the supply chain.

Consider a manufacturing company that produces electronic components.

### Simplified inventory flow

**Supplier / Procurement**
Purchased raw materials and components

↓

**Raw Materials Inventory**
Materials waiting to be used in production

↓

**Manufacturing**
Materials being processed and transformed

↓

**Finished Goods Inventory**
Completed products ready for customer orders

↓

**Distribution / Customer**
Products delivered to customers or distribution centers

Each stage has its own inventory requirements.

The procurement team focuses on material availability.

The manufacturing team focuses on material flow and production continuity.

The warehouse team focuses on inventory storage, movement, and accuracy.

The distribution team focuses on product availability and customer fulfillment.

These activities must be coordinated.

For example, purchasing too much raw material may increase inventory costs, while producing too many finished goods may create excess stocks if customer demand does not materialize.

## 4.3 Relationship Between Inventory, Demand, Production, and Procurement

These four elements are closely connected.

| Business element | Relationship with inventory |
|---|---|
| Demand | Determines how much product customers are expected to consume or purchase. |
| Production | Converts raw materials into finished products and changes inventory quantities. |
| Procurement | Acquires materials required to support future production. |
| Inventory | Connects and buffers these activities over time. |

Let's consider a simplified example.

A company forecasts 5,000 units of demand next month.

It currently has:
- 1,000 finished goods units.
- 500 units expected from confirmed production before the demand period.
- A planned production quantity of 4,000 units.

The company must determine whether the existing and planned supply will be sufficient.

However, it cannot simply look at production quantity.

It must also consider:
- Beginning inventory.
- Confirmed customer orders.
- Expected production output.
- Material availability.
- Production lead time.
- Planned deliveries.
- Required ending inventory.

This is why inventory management is integrated with demand planning, supply planning, and procurement.

## 4.4 Inventory Costs and Business Impact

Maintaining inventory provides benefits, but it is not free.

Inventory creates several types of business costs.

### A. Holding or Carrying Costs

These are costs associated with keeping inventory over a period of time.

Examples:
- Warehouse rental.
- Storage equipment.
- Insurance.
- Handling expenses.
- Inventory damage.
- Deterioration.
- Capital financing costs.

Imagine that your company stores ₱10 million worth of materials.

That inventory occupies warehouse space and ties up money that could otherwise be used for other business activities.

The longer inventory remains unused, the longer company resources remain tied up.

### B. Ordering and Procurement Costs

These are costs incurred when purchasing or replenishing inventory.

Examples:
- Purchase order processing.
- Supplier coordination.
- Transportation.
- Receiving and inspection.
- Administrative activities.

For example:

If a company orders raw materials 100 times a year instead of 20 times, it may incur more frequent administrative and receiving activities.

This does not automatically mean fewer orders are always better. Larger orders can increase holding costs.

This creates a trade-off between ordering costs and holding costs.

### C. Shortage and Stockout Costs

A stockout occurs when inventory is insufficient to satisfy a requirement.

Example:

A customer orders 1,000 units, but the company only has 700 available units.

The company has a shortage of 300 units.

Possible consequences include:
- Delayed deliveries.
- Lost sales.
- Customer dissatisfaction.
- Production interruptions.
- Emergency purchasing.
- Expedited transportation.

In manufacturing, a stockout of one critical raw material may stop an entire production line.

### D. Excess and Obsolete Inventory Costs

Excess inventory means the company has more inventory than it reasonably needs.

Obsolete inventory refers to inventory that is no longer usable or saleable under normal business requirements.

For example:

A company purchases 20,000 electronic components for a product.

However, the customer changes the product specification.

Some of the existing components may no longer be compatible with the new design.

The company may now have obsolete inventory.

Potential consequences:
- Inventory write-offs.
- Disposal costs.
- Reduced profitability.
- Additional storage requirements.
- Cash tied up in unusable materials.

### Inventory Cost Comparison

| Inventory condition | Main business concern |
|---|---|
| Too little | Stockouts and production interruptions |
| Appropriate level | Balance between availability and investment |
| Too much | High carrying costs and excess inventory |
| Obsolete | Potential financial losses and write-offs |

## 4.5 Inventory's Impact on Financial Performance

This is an important concept for an S&OP analyst.

Inventory is not just an operational matter. It directly affects financial performance.

### 1. Working Capital

Working capital represents the short-term financial resources available to support business operations.

Inventory is generally classified as a current asset.

However, money invested in inventory cannot be used for other purposes until that inventory is converted into cash through sales or otherwise released.

### 2. Cash Flow

Suppose your company purchases ₱5 million of raw materials today.

The company has already spent money, but it may not receive revenue from those materials until:
1. They are processed.
2. The finished products are completed.
3. The products are delivered.
4. The customer pays.

The longer this cycle takes, the longer the company's cash remains tied up.

### 3. Profitability

Excess inventory may reduce profitability through:
- Storage expenses.
- Damage and deterioration.
- Obsolescence.
- Inventory write-offs.
- Financing costs.

On the other hand, insufficient inventory can reduce revenue through lost sales and missed customer commitments.

Therefore, inventory decisions require a balance between operational requirements and financial performance.

# 5. Practical Manufacturing Application

Let's use an example closer to your manufacturing background.

## 5.1 Scenario: Electronic Component Manufacturing

Imagine a company manufacturing Multilayer Ceramic Capacitors (MLCC).

The manufacturing process requires several materials and production stages.

For simplicity, assume the company manufactures 10,000 units of finished products per month.

It maintains inventory at three major stages.

### A. Raw Materials Inventory (RM)

These are materials purchased from suppliers but not yet consumed in production.

Examples:
- Barium titanate powder.
- Binder.
- Solvents.
- Plasticizers.
- Electrode materials.
- Packaging materials.

Why maintain RM inventory?

Because procurement and supplier delivery require time.

If the production schedule requires BT powder tomorrow but the supplier needs seven days to deliver, the company needs sufficient inventory before production begins.

**What happens if RM inventory is insufficient?**

Production may be delayed or stopped.

**What happens if RM inventory is excessive?**

Warehouse space, storage expenses, and inventory investment increase. Some materials may also have shelf-life or storage-condition restrictions.

### B. Work-in-Process Inventory (WIP)

These are materials that have already entered the manufacturing process but have not yet become finished products.

Examples:
- Prepared slurry.
- Cast sheets.
- Stacked sheets.
- Cut green chips.
- Products waiting for firing.
- Products waiting for electrical inspection.

Why maintain WIP inventory?

Because manufacturing consists of multiple processes that may have different processing times and capacities.

For example:

Casting may produce material faster than the stacking process can consume it.

The excess material may temporarily accumulate between these operations.

This is WIP inventory.

**What happens if WIP is insufficient?**

Downstream processes may experience starvation, meaning they do not have enough material to continue operating.

**What happens if WIP is excessive?**

- Longer production lead times.
- More material handling.
- Increased work-in-process value.
- Delayed detection of production problems.
- Greater risk of damage or quality deterioration.

### C. Finished Goods Inventory (FG)

These are products that have completed manufacturing and passed the required inspections and quality requirements.

Examples:
- Electrically tested MLCC products.
- Products that passed sorting.
- Finished products ready for shipment.

Why maintain FG inventory?

To fulfill customer orders without waiting for another complete manufacturing cycle.

**What happens if FG inventory is insufficient?**

The company may miss shipment commitments or delay customer deliveries.

**What happens if FG inventory is excessive?**

The company may have:
- High inventory carrying costs.
- Excess warehouse utilization.
- Cash tied up in unsold products.
- Risk of product obsolescence.

## 5.2 Comparing the Three Inventory Stages

| Inventory | Purpose | Risk if insufficient | Risk if excessive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw Materials | Support production | Production stoppage | High material investment |
| WIP | Maintain process flow | Downstream starvation | Longer lead time and hidden inefficiencies |
| Finished Goods | Fulfill customer demand | Missed shipments | Excess stocks and tied-up cash |

An important observation:

**Inventory at every stage serves a different operational purpose.**

Hindi ibig sabihin na kapag maraming inventory ay automatically maganda ang operations.

For example, mataas ang WIP inventory mo dahil hindi makasabay ang firing process sa output ng stacking.

On paper, maraming inventory ang company.

But operationally, it may indicate a bottleneck.

This is an important distinction between inventory quantity and inventory health.

# 6. Formulas and Calculations

For Topic 5.1, we will introduce only one fundamental inventory relationship.

More advanced calculations such as Average Daily Usage (ADU), Days of Supply (DOS), Safety Stock, Reorder Point, and Inventory Turnover will be discussed in succeeding topics.

## 6.1 Basic Inventory Balance

The basic inventory equation is:

[
\text{Ending Inventory} =
\text{Beginning Inventory}
+\text{Receipts}
-\text{Usage}
]

Where:

| Variable | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Beginning Inventory | Inventory available at the start of the period |
| Receipts | Inventory received or produced during the period |
| Usage | Inventory consumed, shipped, or otherwise issued during the period |
| Ending Inventory | Inventory remaining at the end of the period |

### Practical Example

A manufacturing facility starts the month with 5,000 kg of raw materials.

During the month:
- Received from suppliers: 3,000 kg.
- Consumed in production: 6,500 kg.

**Step 1: Identify the beginning inventory.**

[
BI = 5,000 \text{ kg}
]

**Step 2: Add inventory receipts.**

[
5,000+3,000=8,000 \text{ kg}
]

**Step 3: Subtract material consumption.**

[
8,000-6,500=1,500 \text{ kg}
]

### Ending Raw Material Inventory: 1,500 kg

| Movement | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Beginning inventory | 5,000 kg |
| Supplier receipts | +3,000 kg |
| Production consumption | -6,500 kg |
| **Ending inventory** | **1,500 kg** |

### Business Interpretation

The company has 1,500 kg remaining after production consumption.

However, does this automatically mean the company has sufficient inventory?

Not necessarily.

We still need to know:
- How much material will production consume next month?
- When will the next supplier delivery arrive?
- Is the material usable and quality-approved?
- Are some quantities already reserved?
- How variable is supplier lead time?

This demonstrates an important principle:

**Inventory balance tells us how much inventory remains. Inventory planning determines whether that quantity is sufficient for future requirements.**

Also, this simplified formula assumes all receipts and usage are measured on the same basis and excludes adjustments such as write-offs, transfers, and inventory corrections unless included in the movement figures.

# 7. Business Perspective and S&OP Decision-Making

Now let's move from understanding inventory to thinking like an S&OP analyst.

## 7.1 The Inventory Balancing Problem

An S&OP analyst must understand that both insufficient and excessive inventory create business risks.

### Scenario A: Understocking

The company maintains insufficient inventory relative to its requirements.

Possible consequences:
- Production shortages.
- Missed delivery commitments.
- Emergency procurement.
- Lower customer satisfaction.
- Lost revenue.

### Scenario B: Overstocking

The company maintains more inventory than required.

Possible consequences:
- Higher inventory carrying costs.
- Reduced cash availability.
- Warehouse congestion.
- Higher obsolescence risk.
- Reduced inventory productivity.

The objective is not to eliminate inventory.

The objective is to maintain the appropriate inventory level based on business requirements and risk.

## 7.2 How S&OP Decisions Influence Inventory

Let's examine some common S&OP decisions.

| S&OP decision | Potential inventory impact |
|---|---|
| Increase demand forecast | May require higher production and inventory |
| Increase production ahead of demand | May increase finished goods inventory |
| Reduce production | May reduce inventory or create future shortages |
| Increase safety stock | Increases inventory protection and investment |
| Extend supplier lead time | May require additional raw material inventory |
| Change product mix | May create excess inventory of some products and shortages of others |
| Delay customer shipments | May increase finished goods inventory |

Notice that inventory is affected by decisions made across multiple departments.

Sales may increase the forecast.

Production may build additional products.

Procurement may purchase additional materials.

Finance may become concerned about increasing working capital.

These departments may have different priorities.

S&OP provides the forum for aligning those decisions.

## 7.3 How an S&OP Analyst Evaluates Inventory Risks

Suppose the demand planning team increases next month's forecast by 20%.

As an S&OP analyst, hindi sapat na sabihin lang na:

"We need to increase inventory because demand is increasing."

You need to investigate:

**Demand perspective**
- Is the demand increase supported by actual customer orders?
- Is it temporary or expected to continue?
- Which products are experiencing increased demand?

**Supply perspective**
- Can production accommodate the increased requirements?
- Are raw materials available?
- Are suppliers capable of delivering the additional materials?

**Inventory perspective**
- How much usable inventory is currently available?
- How much is already allocated?
- Is existing inventory sufficient to cover the increased demand?
- Is there a risk of excess inventory in other products?

**Financial perspective**
- How much additional working capital is required?
- What are the carrying costs?
- What is the financial consequence of maintaining additional inventory?

**Customer perspective**
- Can the company fulfill customer commitments?
- Which customers or products should receive priority if supply is limited?

The purpose is to understand the consequences of each decision before management commits to an action.

## 7.4 Example: Demand Increase vs. Inventory Investment

Suppose:

| Item | Original plan | Revised plan |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly demand | 10,000 units | 12,000 units |
| Available finished goods | 2,000 units | 2,000 units |
| Planned production | 9,000 units | 9,000 units |

Under the original plan:

[
2,000+9,000-10,000=1,000
]

Ending inventory = 1,000 units.

Under the revised plan:

[
2,000+9,000-12,000=-1,000
]

The company now faces a potential shortage of 1,000 units.

What can management consider?

1. Increase production, if capacity and materials permit.
2. Expedite raw material procurement.
3. Reallocate existing inventory from other products or locations, if feasible.
4. Adjust customer delivery commitments.
5. Review demand priorities and allocation rules.

Notice that inventory alone cannot solve every supply-demand imbalance.

Sometimes, the company must change its production plan, procurement strategy, or customer commitments.

This is exactly why inventory management is integrated into S&OP.

# 8. Key Takeaways

Before proceeding to the next topic, make sure these concepts are clear.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Inventory | Materials and products maintained to support operations and future demand |
| Stock | Quantity of a particular item held or recorded |
| Available Supply | Quantity actually available to satisfy requirements |
| Inventory Buffer | Protection against demand, supply, and operational variability |
| Holding Cost | Cost of keeping inventory |
| Ordering Cost | Cost of purchasing and replenishing inventory |
| Stockout | Situation where available inventory cannot satisfy requirements |
| Excess Inventory | Inventory beyond what is reasonably needed |
| Obsolete Inventory | Inventory that is no longer usable or saleable under normal requirements |
| Working Capital | Financial resources tied up in business operations, including inventory |
| Inventory Balance | Beginning inventory + receipts - usage |

### Five principles to remember

1. Inventory is not limited to finished goods. Raw materials and WIP are also inventory.
2. Inventory supports production continuity and customer service.
3. Inventory protects the business against uncertainty.
4. Too much inventory and too little inventory both create business problems.
5. S&OP balances inventory availability, operational requirements, and financial investment.

**The most important lesson:** Inventory is not simply something a company stores. It is a strategic resource that connects demand, supply, production, procurement, and financial performance.
