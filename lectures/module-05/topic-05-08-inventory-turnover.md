# Module 5 – Inventory Management
## Topic 5.8: Inventory Turnover

**Learning objective:** Understand how companies measure inventory turnover, evaluate how efficiently inventory is being used, and identify potential excess or insufficient inventory.

**Main learning objective:** Understand how efficiently a company converts its inventory into products sold, how quickly inventory moves through the business, and how to identify potential excess or insufficient inventory.

Let's approach this from a manufacturing perspective. Imagine a company that continuously purchases raw materials, converts them into finished products, and sells those products to customers.

The company has one important question:

**Are we holding the right amount of inventory, or is too much of our money sitting inside the warehouse?**

Inventory Turnover helps answer that question.

---

# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What are we going to learn?

In the previous topics, we discussed how companies determine:

- How much inventory they need.
- How long inventory can support demand.
- How much safety stock they should maintain.
- When they should replenish their stocks.

Now, we're shifting our attention from inventory quantity to inventory efficiency.

Inventory Turnover measures how frequently a company uses or sells its inventory relative to the amount of inventory it maintains during a particular period.

For example:

A manufacturing company maintains an average inventory worth ₱10 million. During the year, the company consumes or sells products with a cost of ₱40 million.

This means the company generates inventory turnover of 4 times per year.

In simple terms, the inventory equivalent of its average stock was consumed or sold four times throughout the year.

## 1.2 Why is this important?

Companies monitor inventory turnover because inventory has financial and operational consequences.

| Business concern | Why turnover matters |
|---|---|
| Excess inventory | Indicates that capital may be tied up in inventory that moves slowly |
| Storage costs | More inventory may require additional warehouse space |
| Obsolescence | Slow-moving materials may become outdated or unusable |
| Production efficiency | Inventory may not be flowing efficiently through manufacturing |
| Customer service | Insufficient inventory may create shortages and missed orders |
| Working capital | Inventory consumes cash that could be used elsewhere |

## 1.3 Connection to previous and succeeding topics

| Topic | Connection to Inventory Turnover |
|---|---|
| 5.5 Days of Supply (DOS) | Measures how many days inventory can support demand |
| 5.6 Lead Time and Safety Stock | Explains why companies maintain protective inventory |
| 5.7 Reorder Point (ROP) | Determines when replenishment should happen |
| **5.8 Inventory Turnover** | Measures how efficiently inventory moves |
| 5.9 ABC Classification | Helps prioritize inventory management based on inventory importance |

**Important distinction:** DOS tells us how long inventory can last. Inventory Turnover tells us how many times inventory is consumed or sold relative to average inventory over a period.

Both are useful, but they answer different questions.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Imagine a small manufacturing business

Suppose you own a business that manufactures electric fans.

You maintain an inventory of motors, plastic housings, wires, and other components.

At the beginning of January, you have ₱500,000 worth of inventory.

Throughout the month, you use these materials to manufacture products and replenish your stocks.

At the end of January, your remaining inventory is ₱300,000.

The company has been consuming inventory while continuously replenishing it.

Now, imagine two different companies:

### Company A: Fast-moving inventory

**12 times/year**

*Illustrative inventory turnover.*

Inventory moves relatively quickly. The company regularly converts inventory into products sold or consumed.

### Company B: Slow-moving inventory

**2 times/year**

*Illustrative inventory turnover.*

Inventory moves relatively slowly. More capital may remain tied up in stock for longer periods.

Which company is more efficient?

At first glance, Company A appears better because it turns over inventory more frequently.

But we cannot immediately conclude that Company A is performing better.

What if Company A frequently experiences material shortages and production stoppages?

What if Company B intentionally maintains additional inventory because its suppliers have long lead times?

This is why inventory turnover must be interpreted together with other operational indicators.

**The goal is not simply to maximize turnover. The goal is to achieve an appropriate balance between inventory efficiency and customer service.**

## 2.2 Understanding the basic idea

Think of inventory as money temporarily stored in physical materials and products.

When inventory moves through the business and becomes sold products, the company recovers the value of that inventory through sales.

The faster inventory moves, the less time capital generally remains tied up in stock.

This is the basic idea behind inventory turnover.

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 Formal definition

**Inventory Turnover Ratio** is a financial and operational efficiency metric that measures how many times a company's average inventory is consumed or sold during a specified reporting period.

It is commonly expressed as:

- Times per year
- Times per quarter
- Times per month

For example:

An annual inventory turnover of 6 means the company's Cost of Goods Sold during the year is equivalent to six times its average inventory value.

## 3.2 Inventory Turnover vs. DOS vs. Inventory Holding Period

These three metrics are related, but they are not interchangeable.

| Metric | What it measures | Typical unit |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory Turnover | Frequency of inventory consumption or sales relative to average stock | Times |
| Days of Supply (DOS) | How long current inventory can support expected demand or consumption | Days |
| Inventory Holding Period | Average time inventory remains held before being consumed or sold | Days |
| Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) | Average number of days inventory remains in the business before being sold or consumed | Days |

A simplified relationship exists between turnover and inventory days:

\[
DIO = \frac{\text{Number of Days}}{\text{Inventory Turnover}}
\]

For annual turnover:

\[
DIO = \frac{365}{\text{Annual Turnover}}
\]

For example:

If annual inventory turnover is 5:

\[
DIO = \frac{365}{5}=73\text{ days}
\]

This means inventory represents approximately 73 days of consumption or sales on average.

**Important:** DIO is a historical financial metric, while DOS commonly uses current inventory and forecasted or expected demand. They may produce different results.

## 3.3 High vs. Low Inventory Turnover

| High turnover | Low turnover |
|---|---|
| Inventory moves quickly | Inventory moves slowly |
| Potentially efficient inventory utilization | Potential excess inventory |
| Lower average inventory relative to consumption | Higher average inventory relative to consumption |
| Potentially lower holding costs | Potentially higher holding costs |
| Possible risk of stockouts | Possible risk of obsolescence |

Neither condition is automatically good or bad.

The appropriate turnover depends on the product, industry, supplier lead time, production strategy, and customer requirements.

# 4. Detailed Discussion

Now let's examine the actual components and mechanics behind the calculation.

## 4.1 Inventory Turnover Components

There are two main components:

1. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
2. Average Inventory

### A. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

**Layman's explanation:**

COGS represents the cost of the products that a company has already sold to customers.

For a manufacturing company, this generally includes the production costs associated with the goods sold, such as:

- Direct materials
- Direct labor
- Manufacturing overhead

For example:

A company manufactures electric motors.

The total manufacturing cost of one motor is ₱500. The company sells 1,000 motors during the month.

\[
COGS = 1,000 \times ₱500
\]

\[
COGS = ₱500,000
\]

The company has ₱500,000 in COGS for those motors.

**Why do we use COGS instead of Sales Revenue?**

This is an important accounting concept.

Suppose the company sells the same 1,000 motors for ₱800 each.

| Component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sales revenue | ₱800,000 |
| Manufacturing cost | ₱500,000 |
| Gross profit | ₱300,000 |

If we use sales revenue to calculate inventory turnover, we include the company's profit margin.

But inventory is recorded at its cost, not its selling price.

Therefore, using COGS provides a more consistent comparison between the cost of inventory consumed or sold and the cost of inventory maintained.

**Remember:**

- COGS = Cost of goods sold.
- Sales Revenue = Selling value of goods sold.
- They are not interchangeable.

### B. Average Inventory

**Layman's explanation:**

Average inventory represents the typical inventory value maintained by a company during a particular period.

Why not simply use ending inventory?

Because ending inventory only represents the stock remaining on the last day of the reporting period.

It does not necessarily represent the inventory level maintained throughout the period.

Consider this situation:

| Date | Inventory Value |
|---|---:|
| January 1 | ₱500,000 |
| January 31 | ₱300,000 |

The company started with ₱500,000 and ended with ₱300,000.

Using only the ending inventory might make inventory turnover appear higher than it would if we considered the inventory maintained throughout the month.

That's why we calculate average inventory.

### C. Beginning Inventory and Ending Inventory

These two values are used to calculate average inventory.

**Beginning Inventory (BI)**

The inventory value at the start of the reporting period.

**Ending Inventory (EI)**

The inventory value at the end of the reporting period.

For example:

- Beginning inventory: ₱500,000
- Ending inventory: ₱300,000

Average inventory:

\[
\frac{500,000+300,000}{2}=400,000
\]

The company's average inventory is ₱400,000.

### D. Inventory Valuation and Consistency

This is particularly important when working with manufacturing data.

Inventory turnover must compare values measured using consistent valuation bases.

For example:

| Numerator | Denominator | Valid? |
|---|---|---|
| COGS at manufacturing cost | Average inventory at manufacturing cost | Yes |
| Sales revenue | Average inventory at manufacturing cost | Generally inappropriate |
| Material consumption at standard cost | Average raw material inventory at standard cost | Yes |
| Material consumption at actual cost | Average raw material inventory at standard cost | Potentially inconsistent |

Why?

Imagine that your COGS is ₱10 million, but your average inventory is valued at selling prices.

Your calculation would compare two different valuation systems.

The resulting ratio could be misleading.

**Professional consideration:** Companies should also maintain consistent accounting treatment, including inventory costing methods and treatment of production overhead, when comparing turnover across periods.

## 4.2 Inventory Turnover Calculation

Now let's introduce the main formula.

### Basic Inventory Turnover Formula

\[
\boxed{ITR=\frac{COGS}{Average\ Inventory}}
\]

Where:

- ITR = Inventory Turnover Ratio
- COGS = Cost of Goods Sold during the period
- Average Inventory = Average inventory value during the same period

### Average Inventory Formula

\[
\boxed{Average\ Inventory=\frac{BI+EI}{2}}
\]

- BI = Beginning Inventory
- EI = Ending Inventory

### Example 1: Basic Inventory Turnover

A manufacturing company has the following annual inventory information:

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Beginning Inventory | ₱4,000,000 |
| Ending Inventory | ₱6,000,000 |
| Annual COGS | ₱30,000,000 |

**Step 1: Calculate Average Inventory**

\[
AI=\frac{4,000,000+6,000,000}{2}
\]

\[
AI=₱5,000,000
\]

**Step 2: Calculate Inventory Turnover**

\[
ITR=\frac{30,000,000}{5,000,000}
\]

**Annual Inventory Turnover: 6.0 times**

The company's annual COGS is equivalent to six times its average inventory value.

**Business interpretation:**

The company maintains an average inventory of ₱5 million while generating ₱30 million in annual COGS.

This indicates that its inventory turns over six times per year.

It does not mean that every individual item was sold exactly six times. Different materials and products can have different turnover rates.

## 4.3 Monthly, Quarterly, and Annual Inventory Turnover

Inventory turnover can be calculated using different reporting periods.

The important rule is:

**The COGS period and inventory measurement period must match.**

| Reporting period | Formula |
|---|---|
| Monthly | Monthly COGS ÷ Average monthly inventory |
| Quarterly | Quarterly COGS ÷ Average quarterly inventory |
| Annual | Annual COGS ÷ Average annual inventory |

Let's use another example.

A company has the following data:

| Period | COGS | Average Inventory |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | ₱2M | ₱4M |
| February | ₱3M | ₱4M |
| March | ₱4M | ₱4M |
| Q1 Total | ₱9M | ₱4M |

**Monthly turnover**

January:

\[
\frac{2M}{4M}=0.50
\]

February:

\[
\frac{3M}{4M}=0.75
\]

March:

\[
\frac{4M}{4M}=1.00
\]

**Quarterly turnover**

\[
ITR=\frac{9M}{4M}=2.25
\]

**Q1 Turnover = 2.25 times**

Notice that we did not divide quarterly COGS by three before calculating quarterly turnover.

The quarterly COGS already represents the total cost of goods sold during the three-month period.

### Important: Annualizing Inventory Turnover

Suppose you calculate inventory turnover for January only and obtain 0.50.

Does this mean annual turnover is 0.50?

No. That is only the turnover during January.

To estimate annualized turnover:

\[
Annualized\ ITR=Monthly\ ITR\times12
\]

\[
=0.50\times12
\]

\[
=6.0\ times/year
\]

For quarterly turnover:

\[
Annualized\ ITR=Quarterly\ ITR\times4
\]

\[
=2.25\times4=9.0
\]

**Important assumption:** Annualization assumes that the observed period is reasonably representative of the entire year.

If January has unusually high production or March has seasonal demand, simply multiplying by 12 or 4 may create misleading projections.

For more reliable analysis, use a rolling 12-month calculation when sufficient historical data is available.

## 4.4 Inventory Turnover Using Physical Quantities vs. Inventory Value

This is an important distinction, especially when working with multiple materials and products.

There are two approaches.

### A. Value-Based Inventory Turnover

This is the commonly used financial inventory turnover formula.

\[
ITR=\frac{COGS}{Average\ Inventory\ Value}
\]

Example:

- Annual COGS = ₱20 million
- Average inventory = ₱5 million

\[
ITR=4\ times
\]

**When to use:**

- Financial reporting
- Working capital analysis
- Company-wide inventory efficiency
- S&OP financial reviews

### B. Quantity-Based Inventory Turnover

This approach measures how frequently physical inventory is consumed or sold.

\[
Quantity\ Turnover=
\frac{Quantity\ Consumed\ or\ Sold}
{Average\ Inventory\ Quantity}
\]

Example:

A factory consumes 12,000 kg of raw material annually.

Its average raw material inventory is 2,000 kg.

\[
Quantity\ Turnover=\frac{12,000}{2,000}
\]

\[
=6\ times/year
\]

**When to use:**

- Raw material consumption monitoring
- Individual material analysis
- Production planning
- Warehouse movement analysis

However, do not simply combine different physical units.

For example, you cannot meaningfully add 1,000 kg of powder, 500 liters of solvent, and 2,000 pieces of components and treat the result as one common inventory quantity.

For multiple materials, use separate calculations or a consistent monetary valuation.

### When should you use which?

| Business objective | Recommended measurement |
|---|---|
| Evaluate financial inventory efficiency | Value-based |
| Monitor raw material consumption | Quantity-based |
| Compare different materials | Value-based or individual quantity-based |
| Evaluate warehouse inventory movement | Quantity-based, with clear unit definitions |
| Evaluate overall company inventory | Value-based |

## 4.5 Inventory Days and Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO)

We now connect Inventory Turnover to inventory holding time.

Remember:

Inventory Turnover measures how many times inventory turns over.

DIO measures the approximate number of days inventory remains in the business before being sold or consumed.

### Formula

\[
\boxed{DIO=\frac{Number\ of\ Days}{Inventory\ Turnover}}
\]

For annual reporting:

\[
\boxed{DIO=\frac{365}{Annual\ ITR}}
\]

### Example 2: Calculate DIO

Using our earlier example:

- Annual COGS = ₱30 million
- Average inventory = ₱5 million
- Annual turnover = 6 times

\[
DIO=\frac{365}{6}
\]

\[
DIO=60.83\ days
\]

**Days Inventory Outstanding: 60.83 days**

Approximately 61 days of inventory, based on historical annual COGS and average inventory.

### Why is DIO useful?

Imagine two products:

| Metric | Product A | Product B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Annual Turnover | 12 | 4 |
| DIO | 30.4 days | 91.25 days |

Product A moves significantly faster than Product B.

Product B remains in inventory for approximately three times as long, based on the respective turnover calculations.

This can help identify:

- Slow-moving products
- Potential excess inventory
- Opportunities to reduce inventory investment
- Differences in production and replenishment strategies

**Note:** DIO is an average accounting-based measure. It does not tell us exactly how long every individual batch or material has been sitting in the warehouse. For actual aging analysis, companies also use inventory aging reports.

## 4.6 Relationship Between Inventory Turnover and DOS

Let's make the distinction clearer.

| Inventory Turnover | DOS |
|---|---|
| Uses historical COGS or consumption | Commonly uses current inventory and forecast demand |
| Measures inventory movement over a period | Measures how long available stock can support demand |
| Expressed in times | Expressed in days |
| Useful for evaluating historical efficiency | Useful for evaluating current inventory coverage |

For example:

A company has 1,000 units of a component.

Its average daily demand is 50 units.

\[
DOS=\frac{1,000}{50}=20\ days
\]

The company currently has 20 days of supply.

Now, suppose its annual turnover is 12 times.

Its historical DIO is approximately:

\[
DIO=\frac{365}{12}=30.42\ days
\]

Why are they different?

Because DOS is based on current stock and demand assumptions, while DIO is based on historical average inventory and actual COGS.

They can differ because of demand changes, seasonality, inventory fluctuations, and changes in replenishment.

**S&OP insight:** Use DOS to understand current inventory coverage and turnover/DIO to evaluate historical inventory efficiency.

## 4.7 Comparing Inventory Turnover Across Products or Periods

Inventory turnover becomes more useful when we compare performance.

Consider the following example:

| Product | Annual COGS | Average Inventory | Turnover |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | ₱12M | ₱2M | 6.0 |
| B | ₱10M | ₱5M | 2.0 |
| C | ₱8M | ₱2M | 4.0 |

Calculations:

Product A:

\[
\frac{12M}{2M}=6
\]

Product B:

\[
\frac{10M}{5M}=2
\]

Product C:

\[
\frac{8M}{2M}=4
\]

**Interpretation:**

- Product A has the highest turnover.
- Product B has the lowest turnover.
- Product C is between the two.

Product B deserves further investigation because it maintains a relatively large inventory compared with its annual COGS.

However, we should not immediately recommend reducing Product B's inventory.

We must first investigate its lead time, demand variability, service requirements, and stockout history.

### Period-to-period comparison

Suppose a company wants to evaluate whether its inventory management improved.

| Period | Annualized Turnover |
|---|---:|
| Q1 | 4.0 |
| Q2 | 4.5 |
| Q3 | 5.0 |
| Q4 | 6.0 |

The turnover rate is increasing.

This may indicate better inventory utilization or reduced average inventory relative to COGS.

But we should investigate whether the improvement resulted from:

- Better demand forecasting
- Reduced excess inventory
- Improved procurement planning
- Faster production flow
- Increased sales
- Inventory reductions that created stockouts

A higher ratio alone does not prove that inventory management improved.

# 5. Practical Manufacturing Application

Let's apply everything to a realistic manufacturing environment.

We'll use a company that manufactures electronic components, with three major inventory categories:

1. Raw Materials (RM)
2. Work-in-Process (WIP)
3. Finished Goods (FG)

This is particularly relevant to manufacturing companies because inventory doesn't simply sit in a warehouse. It moves through several stages of production.

## 5.1 Understanding the inventory flow

**RAW MATERIALS (RM)**

Powder, chemicals, films, components

↓

**WORK-IN-PROCESS (WIP)**

Materials currently undergoing manufacturing

↓

**FINISHED GOODS (FG)**

Completed products ready for shipment

↓

**CUSTOMER / SALES**

Finished products are sold and delivered.

Each category has a different operational purpose.

- RM ensures production has sufficient materials.
- WIP represents materials being converted into finished products.
- FG supports customer orders and delivery requirements.

Therefore, inventory turnover should also be investigated at the category level.

## 5.2 Manufacturing scenario

Suppose an electronics manufacturer wants to evaluate its inventory performance for 2026.

The following are illustrative annual inventory figures.

**A. Inventory balances**

| Inventory Category | Beginning Inventory | Ending Inventory |
|---|---:|---:|
| Raw Materials | ₱2.8M | ₱3.2M |
| WIP | ₱3.5M | ₱4.5M |
| Finished Goods | ₱5.5M | ₱6.5M |
| **Total** | **₱11.8M** | **₱14.2M** |

**B. Inventory movement during the year**

| Inventory Category | Annual Cost Flow |
|---|---:|
| Raw Material Consumption | ₱18M |
| WIP Cost Transferred to FG | ₱24M |
| Finished Goods COGS | ₱30M |

Notice that each inventory category has a different cost flow.

- RM: Cost of materials issued to production.
- WIP: Cost of production completed and transferred to finished goods.
- FG: Cost of finished goods sold to customers.

This distinction is important because each category represents a different stage of manufacturing.

## 5.3 Step 1: Calculate Average Inventory

Use:

\[
AI=\frac{BI+EI}{2}
\]

**Raw Materials**

\[
AI_{RM}=\frac{2.8M+3.2M}{2}
\]

\[
AI_{RM}=₱3M
\]

**Work-in-Process**

\[
AI_{WIP}=\frac{3.5M+4.5M}{2}
\]

\[
AI_{WIP}=₱4M
\]

**Finished Goods**

\[
AI_{FG}=\frac{5.5M+6.5M}{2}
\]

\[
AI_{FG}=₱6M
\]

Summary:

| Category | Average Inventory |
|---|---:|
| RM | ₱3M |
| WIP | ₱4M |
| FG | ₱6M |
| **Total** | **₱13M** |

## 5.4 Step 2: Calculate Category-Level Inventory Turnover

For category-level operational analysis, use the appropriate cost flow for each inventory stage.

\[
Category\ Turnover=
\frac{Relevant\ Annual\ Cost\ Flow}
{Average\ Inventory}
\]

**Raw Material Turnover**

\[
=\frac{18M}{3M}=6
\]

**RM Turnover = 6 times/year**

The average raw material inventory is equivalent to six times the annual material consumption.

**WIP Turnover**

\[
=\frac{24M}{4M}=6
\]

**WIP Turnover = 6 times/year**

The cost of production completed and transferred to finished goods is six times the average WIP inventory.

**Finished Goods Turnover**

\[
=\frac{30M}{6M}=5
\]

**FG Turnover = 5 times/year**

The company sells finished goods equivalent to five times its average finished goods inventory.

### Consolidated results

| Category | Average Inventory | Annual Cost Flow | Turnover |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RM | ₱3M | ₱18M | 6.0 |
| WIP | ₱4M | ₱24M | 6.0 |
| FG | ₱6M | ₱30M | 5.0 |

**Initial observation:**

Finished Goods has the lowest category-level turnover.

This suggests that finished products move relatively slower than the other categories, based on their respective cost flows.

However, this is only an initial indication. We still need to investigate the reasons.

## 5.5 Step 3: Calculate Overall Inventory Turnover

For the entire company, use total COGS divided by average total inventory.

\[
Overall\ ITR=\frac{Annual\ COGS}{Average\ Total\ Inventory}
\]

We already know:

- Annual COGS = ₱30M
- Average total inventory = ₱13M

Therefore:

\[
ITR=\frac{30M}{13M}
\]

\[
\boxed{ITR=2.31\ times/year}
\]

Why is the overall turnover different from the individual category turnover?

Because the overall financial calculation uses total inventory and COGS, while category-level calculations use the appropriate cost flow for each manufacturing stage.

**Important:** Do not add or average the category turnover ratios to calculate overall company turnover.

## 5.6 Step 4: Calculate Inventory Days for Each Category

We can also translate category-level turnover into approximate inventory days.

\[
Inventory\ Days=\frac{365}{Category\ Turnover}
\]

| Category | Turnover | Inventory Days |
|---|---:|---:|
| RM | 6.0 | 60.83 days |
| WIP | 6.0 | 60.83 days |
| FG | 5.0 | 73 days |

Interpretation:

- RM represents approximately 61 days of historical consumption.
- WIP represents approximately 61 days based on its cost transfer rate.
- FG represents approximately 73 days based on historical COGS.

These are historical inventory-day equivalents, not necessarily actual physical aging.

## 5.7 Step 5: Identify Potential Inventory Problems

Now let's think like an S&OP analyst.

### Raw Materials

**Initial observation**

Turnover: 6 times/year

RM turnover appears relatively fast. However, we should still investigate supplier lead times, material shortages, and safety stock requirements.

### Work-in-Process

**For investigation**

Turnover: 6 times/year

WIP is equivalent to approximately 61 inventory days. We should investigate production cycle time, bottlenecks, waiting time between processes, and production scheduling.

### Finished Goods

**For investigation**

Turnover: 5 times/year

FG has the lowest turnover and highest inventory-day equivalent. Potential causes include overproduction, inaccurate demand forecasts, declining customer demand, and shipment delays.

### What decisions might we recommend?

| Observation | Possible investigation or action |
|---|---|
| Slow-moving RM | Review purchase quantities, MOQ, supplier lead time, and forecast accuracy |
| High WIP | Investigate bottlenecks, production scheduling, and cycle time |
| Slow-moving FG | Review demand forecast, production plan, and customer orders |
| Very fast-moving RM | Check stockout frequency and supplier replenishment reliability |
| Very fast-moving FG | Check whether finished goods inventory is sufficient to meet customer demand |

Notice that we are not automatically reducing inventory.

We are identifying where investigation is necessary before recommending action.

### A critical manufacturing lesson

Suppose WIP turnover is low because products spend too much time waiting between manufacturing processes.

Reducing raw material inventory will not necessarily solve the problem.

The actual issue may be production flow.

Likewise, if FG turnover is low because the demand forecast is consistently higher than actual customer orders, increasing production will make the problem worse.

**Inventory turnover is a diagnostic indicator. It identifies where to investigate, but it does not automatically identify the root cause.**

# 6. Business Perspective and S&OP Decision-Making

This is where Inventory Turnover becomes especially useful in an S&OP environment.

An S&OP analyst doesn't simply calculate a ratio and report whether it increased or decreased.

The analyst needs to understand what operational activities caused the change and what decisions should follow.

## 6.1 How different business functions affect inventory turnover

| Function | Decisions affecting turnover |
|---|---|
| Demand Planning | Forecast accuracy, demand changes, promotional activities |
| Supply Planning | Production quantities, inventory targets, capacity allocation |
| Procurement | Purchase quantities, supplier lead times, MOQ, replenishment frequency |
| Manufacturing | Production cycle time, WIP levels, production efficiency |
| Warehouse | Storage management, inventory aging, stock movement |
| Finance | Inventory valuation, working capital, COGS, inventory investment |

Let's examine some practical situations.

### Scenario A: Demand Forecast Is Too High

A company forecasts demand of 10,000 units per month.

However, actual demand is only 6,000 units.

The company continues producing 10,000 units.

What happens?

1. Production exceeds actual demand.
2. Finished goods inventory increases.
3. Average inventory increases.
4. COGS may not increase proportionally.
5. Inventory turnover decreases.

**Possible S&OP decision:** Reconcile the demand forecast with actual customer demand and adjust the production plan.

### Scenario B: Procurement Orders Too Much

A supplier offers a discount if the company purchases 50,000 units instead of 20,000 units.

Procurement accepts the offer.

However, actual monthly consumption is only 5,000 units.

The company now has more inventory than necessary.

Potential consequences:

- Higher inventory investment
- Increased storage requirements
- Greater risk of obsolescence
- Lower inventory turnover

**Possible S&OP decision:** Evaluate whether the purchase discount justifies the additional inventory carrying cost and working capital requirement.

### Scenario C: Production Bottleneck Increases WIP

Suppose the casting process can produce 10,000 units per day, but the next process can only handle 6,000 units per day.

What happens?

- WIP accumulates.
- Inventory waits between manufacturing stages.
- Average WIP increases.
- WIP turnover may decrease.

**Possible S&OP decision:** Evaluate capacity balancing, production sequencing, and bottleneck improvement.

### Scenario D: Inventory Turnover Becomes Too High

Suppose a company aggressively reduces its safety stock.

Its inventory turnover increases from 6 to 12 times annually.

Initially, this appears positive.

However, supplier lead time is 45 days, and the company frequently experiences material shortages.

Now production cannot meet customer requirements.

Potential consequences:

- Production interruptions
- Emergency procurement
- Higher logistics costs
- Missed customer delivery commitments
- Lower service levels

**Possible S&OP decision:** Reassess safety stock, supplier reliability, replenishment frequency, and reorder points.

This is why high inventory turnover is not automatically desirable.

## 6.2 High vs. Low Turnover: What Should an Analyst Investigate?

| Condition | Potential causes | Investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Very low turnover | Excess inventory, declining demand, overproduction | Forecast vs. actual demand, inventory aging |
| Very high turnover | Low inventory, strong demand, insufficient safety stock | Stockout frequency, service level, DOS |
| Sudden turnover decrease | Inventory buildup, demand decline | Inventory movement and demand changes |
| Sudden turnover increase | Inventory reduction, sales increase, supply shortage | COGS, average inventory, customer service |
| High RM turnover | Frequent material consumption or low stock | Lead time, replenishment, safety stock |
| Low FG turnover | Slow product movement or excess finished goods | Customer demand, shipment performance |

## 6.3 Inventory Turnover and Other S&OP KPIs

Inventory Turnover should not be analyzed in isolation.

A good S&OP analyst evaluates it alongside other performance indicators.

| KPI | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Inventory Turnover | How efficiently does inventory move? |
| DOS | How many days can current inventory support demand? |
| Inventory Aging | How long have specific inventory batches been held? |
| Forecast Accuracy | How close is forecasted demand to actual demand? |
| Service Level | Are we meeting customer demand? |
| Stockout Rate | How frequently are inventory shortages occurring? |
| Inventory Value | How much capital is tied up in inventory? |
| Lead Time | How long does replenishment take? |

**Professional insight:** A company may improve inventory turnover while maintaining a high customer service level. That is generally a more meaningful improvement than increasing turnover at the expense of product availability.

## 6.4 How to Investigate Unusual Inventory Turnover

As an S&OP analyst, you can follow this sequence:

1. **Identify the abnormal movement.** Determine whether turnover is unusually high, low, increasing, or decreasing.
2. **Validate the calculation.** Check COGS, beginning inventory, ending inventory, valuation methods, and reporting periods.
3. **Identify the affected inventory category.** Separate RM, WIP, and FG to understand where the problem occurs.
4. **Investigate operational causes.** Review demand, production, procurement, lead time, and inventory movements.
5. **Evaluate business consequences.** Determine the impact on working capital, production continuity, inventory holding costs, and customer service.
6. **Recommend corrective action.** Propose adjustments to forecasts, production quantities, purchase schedules, safety stock, or inventory targets.

# 7. Formula Reference and Important Calculation Considerations

Let's consolidate the formulas you should be familiar with as an S&OP analyst.

## 7.1 Formula Summary

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Average Inventory | \((BI+EI)/2\) |
| Inventory Turnover | \(COGS/AI\) |
| Inventory Days / DIO | \(Days/ITR\) |
| Annualized Monthly Turnover | \(Monthly\ ITR \times 12\) |
| Annualized Quarterly Turnover | \(Quarterly\ ITR \times 4\) |
| Quantity Turnover | \(Quantity\ Consumed/Average\ Quantity\) |
| DOS | \(Current\ Inventory/Average\ Daily\ Demand\) |

Where:

- BI = Beginning Inventory
- EI = Ending Inventory
- AI = Average Inventory
- ITR = Inventory Turnover Ratio
- COGS = Cost of Goods Sold
- DOS = Days of Supply

## 7.2 Important Considerations When Calculating

There are several things you should remember when working with actual company data.

**1. Average inventory should represent the reporting period.**

The beginning-ending average is a simple approximation.

For businesses with fluctuating inventory, monthly average balances can provide a more representative measurement.

For example:

\[
Average\ Inventory=
\frac{\sum Monthly\ Ending\ Inventory}{12}
\]

This is a commonly used approximation for average annual inventory. Daily or more frequent balances may be preferable when inventory fluctuates significantly.

**2. Always match the reporting period.**

Do not divide annual COGS by monthly average inventory and interpret the result as a monthly turnover ratio.

**3. Maintain consistent valuation.**

COGS and average inventory should use compatible cost bases.

**4. Be careful when comparing different products.**

A high-value, low-volume product may behave differently from a low-value, high-volume product.

**5. Consider seasonality.**

A company manufacturing seasonal products may experience significant inventory changes throughout the year.

A single month's turnover may not represent its actual annual performance.

**6. Understand the inventory category.**

Company-wide turnover may hide problems in specific categories.

For manufacturing, separate RM, WIP, and FG analysis can provide more useful operational insights.

# 8. Key Takeaways

Before we proceed to the exercises, let's consolidate the most important concepts.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Inventory Turnover | Measures how frequently average inventory is consumed or sold during a period |
| COGS | Used as the numerator because inventory is valued at cost |
| Average Inventory | Represents the typical inventory maintained during the reporting period |
| High Turnover | May indicate efficient inventory utilization, but could also indicate insufficient stock |
| Low Turnover | May indicate excess or slow-moving inventory |
| DIO | Converts turnover into an approximate number of inventory days |
| DOS | Measures current inventory coverage based on demand |
| Category-Level Turnover | Helps identify potential issues in RM, WIP, and FG |
| Annualization | Converts shorter-period turnover into an estimated annual rate |
| S&OP Application | Supports decisions involving demand, supply, production, procurement, and inventory investment |

### Five concepts I particularly want you to remember

1. **Inventory Turnover is an efficiency metric, not an inventory quantity metric.**
2. COGS is generally used instead of sales revenue because inventory is recorded at cost.
3. Higher turnover does not always mean better inventory management.
4. Inventory turnover should be analyzed together with DOS, lead time, safety stock, and service level.
5. The purpose of inventory analysis is to balance inventory investment with operational requirements and customer service.

### Connection to Topic 5.9: ABC Classification

We have now learned how to evaluate inventory movement and efficiency.

But there is another important question:

**Should a company give equal attention to every inventory item?**

Imagine a factory maintaining 5,000 different inventory items.

Some items may represent millions of pesos in inventory investment, while others may represent only a few hundred pesos.

Managing all 5,000 items with the same level of attention may not be efficient.

This leads us to **ABC Classification**, where inventory items are categorized according to their relative importance, commonly based on annual consumption value.

Inventory Turnover tells us how efficiently inventory moves.

ABC Classification helps us determine which inventory items deserve greater management attention.
