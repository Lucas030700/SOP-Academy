# MODULE 8 – THE S&OP PROCESS, GOVERNANCE & MANAGEMENT
## Topic 8.9: S&OP vs. S&OE

**Learning objective:** Understand how organizations connect medium-term business planning with short-term operational execution, and how S&OP and S&OE work together to maintain alignment between customer demand, production, supply, inventory, capacity, and financial objectives.

**Teaching approach:** Simple → Technical → Practical → Analytical

---

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

Let's start with a situation.

Imagine na ang company ninyo ay may approved production plan for the next six months.

Based on the latest S&OP meeting:

- Expected customer demand: 100,000 units per month.
- Approved production plan: 100,000 units per month.
- Available production capacity: 110,000 units per month.
- Required raw materials: Sufficient for the approved plan.
- Inventory: Within the company's target level.

Everything looks good.

However, during the following week:

1. A major customer suddenly orders an additional 15,000 units.
2. One production machine experiences unexpected downtime.
3. A critical raw material supplier delays its delivery.
4. Available finished goods inventory is insufficient to cover all customer orders.

Question:

**Should the company wait for the next monthly S&OP meeting before responding?**

Of course not.

The company needs an immediate operational response.

But should it also revise its entire monthly or medium-term business plan because of these events?

Not necessarily.

This is where S&OP and S&OE become important.

## 1.1 What are S&OP and S&OE?

**Sales & Operations Planning (S&OP)** is a medium-term, cross-functional planning and decision-making process that aligns demand, supply, capacity, inventory, and financial objectives.

Its primary purpose is to determine:

*What should the business plan to achieve over the coming months, and what resources or decisions are necessary to achieve it?*

**Sales & Operations Execution (S&OE)** is a short-term operational planning and execution process that monitors actual conditions and manages immediate deviations from the approved business plan.

Its primary purpose is to determine:

*What should the organization do today or this week to keep operations running and meet customer commitments?*

Think of them as two connected management processes:

- S&OP establishes the direction.
- S&OE manages the immediate execution.
- Actual execution results provide feedback for future S&OP decisions.

### The relationship between S&OP and S&OE

**S&OP: Medium-term business planning**

Sets the approved demand, supply, capacity, inventory and financial direction.

↓

**Approved plan and targets**

↓

**S&OE: Short-term operational execution**

Monitors actual conditions and resolves immediate demand and supply problems.

↓

**Actual performance and risks**

↓

**Performance feedback**

Actual results, deviations, recurring problems and new assumptions.

↓

**Feedback into the next S&OP cycle**

## 1.2 Why do organizations need both?

Because medium-term planning and short-term execution solve different problems.

Imagine a manufacturing company with a six-month production plan.

Even if the plan is excellent, actual operations will experience unexpected changes:

- Machine breakdowns.
- Customer order cancellations.
- Unexpected demand increases.
- Supplier delivery delays.
- Material quality problems.
- Production yield deterioration.
- Labor shortages.

S&OP alone cannot manage every daily operational issue.

On the other hand, if a company only focuses on daily execution, it may become reactive.

For example, production teams might continuously increase production to satisfy customer orders without considering:

- Future demand.
- Inventory buildup.
- Available capacity.
- Financial targets.
- Long-term supplier commitments.

This creates a business that is operationally busy but strategically misaligned.

**The key principle: S&OP provides planning discipline, while S&OE provides execution discipline.**

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

Let's use a simple manufacturing analogy.

Imagine that you are managing a factory producing electronic components.

Your company receives orders from different customers.

You need to decide:

- How many units should be produced?
- How much raw material should be purchased?
- How many production workers are required?
- How much inventory should be maintained?
- Can the factory support the expected demand?

These are medium-term planning questions.

This is S&OP.

Now imagine that production is already running.

At 10 AM, the production supervisor informs you:

"The machine is down. We cannot complete today's planned production."

At the same time, Purchasing informs you:

"The supplier cannot deliver the required material today."

These are immediate operational problems.

This is S&OE.

## 2.1 An everyday analogy

Think about planning a road trip.

| Activity | Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Deciding your destination | S&OP |
| Planning the route and budget | S&OP |
| Estimating fuel requirements | S&OP |
| Monitoring traffic during the trip | S&OE |
| Finding an alternative route | S&OE |
| Responding to a vehicle breakdown | S&OE |
| Revising the overall trip plan because of major changes | S&OP |

You don't need to redesign your entire road trip every time you encounter traffic.

But if the destination changes, the budget becomes insufficient, or the trip needs to be extended by several days, you may need to reconsider the overall plan.

Ganoon din sa manufacturing.

**S&OE handles operational adjustments within the existing plan. S&OP handles broader planning changes that affect the business direction.**

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

Now let's introduce the professional terminology.

## 3.1 Understanding the planning horizons

One of the biggest differences between S&OP and S&OE is the planning horizon.

### Illustrative planning horizons

**S&OE: Short-term**

Daily to 13 weeks.

Example: Daily production scheduling, weekly order fulfillment and immediate shortage management.

**S&OP: Medium-term**

3 to 24 months.

Example: Monthly demand and supply planning, capacity decisions, inventory targets and financial alignment.

*These are illustrative ranges, not universal industry standards. Actual horizons depend on the company's business model, product lead times and planning practices.*

For example:

- S&OE may focus on production and customer orders for the next five working days.
- S&OP may evaluate monthly demand and supply requirements for the next 12 months.

Why is this distinction important?

Because different time horizons require different types of decisions.

A production supervisor can reschedule tomorrow's production.

However, approving a new production line, expanding factory capacity, or changing long-term supplier agreements may require management decisions through S&OP or another appropriate governance process.

## 3.2 S&OP vs. S&OE: Side-by-side comparison

This is one of the most important comparison tables in this topic.

| Dimension | S&OP | S&OE |
|---|---|---|
| Full name | Sales & Operations Planning | Sales & Operations Execution |
| Primary purpose | Align the business plan | Execute and control the near-term plan |
| Time horizon | Medium-term | Short-term |
| Typical horizon | 3–24 months | Daily to 13 weeks |
| Frequency | Monthly | Daily or weekly |
| Planning level | Aggregate | Detailed |
| Planning granularity | Product family, business unit, region | Individual SKU, order, production line |
| Main focus | Demand-supply balance | Immediate execution |
| Demand information | Forecast and demand outlook | Actual orders and near-term demand changes |
| Supply information | Capacity, inventory and supply availability | Actual production, material and delivery status |
| Participants | Senior management and functional leaders | Planners, schedulers and operational teams |
| Decision-makers | Executive and functional management | Operational managers within delegated authority |
| Typical decisions | Capacity expansion, inventory strategy, supply allocation | Rescheduling, expediting, order prioritization |
| Financial focus | Revenue, margin, inventory investment and costs | Immediate operational and service costs |
| Reporting | Monthly planning reports and scenarios | Daily/weekly exception reports |
| Main output | Approved integrated business plan | Updated execution priorities and operational schedules |

Notice the distinction in planning granularity.

### Aggregate planning vs. detailed planning

Suppose your company manufactures three product families.

| Product family | Monthly S&OP plan |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 50,000 units |
| Product B | 30,000 units |
| Product C | 20,000 units |
| **Total** | **100,000 units** |

S&OP may establish that the company needs to produce 100,000 units next month.

S&OE goes deeper.

For example:

| SKU | Customer | Required delivery | Production schedule |
|---|---|---|---|
| A101 | Customer X | Nov 20 | Nov 17 |
| A102 | Customer Y | Nov 21 | Nov 18 |
| B201 | Customer Z | Nov 22 | Nov 19 |
| C301 | Customer W | Nov 23 | Nov 20 |

The S&OE team works with detailed orders, production schedules, material availability and delivery commitments.

**Important:** S&OP is not necessarily limited to product families, and S&OE is not necessarily limited to individual SKUs. The distinction is the level of detail appropriate to the decision.

## 3.3 Strategic, tactical and operational responsibilities

You should also understand how these two processes fit into the broader management structure.

| Level | Main concern | Typical decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Long-term business direction | Factory expansion, market entry, major investment |
| Tactical | Medium-term business alignment | Monthly demand, supply, capacity and inventory plans |
| Operational | Immediate execution | Production sequencing, material shortages and order fulfillment |

S&OP primarily operates at the tactical level, with strategic decisions escalated when necessary.

S&OE primarily operates at the operational level.

However, they are connected.

For example:

A machine breakdown may initially be an operational problem.

If the machine is expected to remain unavailable for three months, the issue may become a medium-term capacity problem requiring S&OP decisions.

This is an important concept that we will revisit.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

Let's examine how each process actually works.

## 4.1 The S&OP Process

Recall our previous topics in Module 8.

In Topic 8.1, we discussed the recurring S&OP cycle.

In Topics 8.3–8.6, we covered the major review meetings.

In Topic 8.7, we discussed roles and responsibilities.

In Topic 8.8, we discussed approved plans, outputs and implementation.

Now we connect all those concepts to S&OE.

### The monthly S&OP cycle

1. **Data Gathering and Preparation**

   Collect actual demand, sales, inventory, production, capacity, supplier and financial data.

2. **Demand Review**

   Evaluate historical demand, customer orders, forecasts and market changes.

3. **Supply Review**

   Evaluate production capacity, material availability, inventory and supply constraints.

4. **Pre-S&OP Meeting**

   Identify demand-supply gaps and evaluate possible solutions and scenarios.

5. **Executive S&OP Meeting**

   Management approves the integrated business plan and resolves issues requiring executive decisions.

6. **Implementation and Monitoring**

   Communicate the approved plan to the responsible teams and monitor its execution.

### What decisions are made during S&OP?

Consider this situation:

The demand forecast indicates that customer requirements will increase by 20% over the next six months.

The current factory capacity cannot support the entire increase.

The organization evaluates three alternatives:

1. Introduce additional shifts.
2. Outsource part of the production.
3. Invest in additional production equipment.

These are not ordinary daily scheduling decisions.

They have implications for:

- Labor costs.
- Manufacturing costs.
- Capital expenditure.
- Supplier commitments.
- Inventory.
- Revenue.
- Profitability.

Therefore, these decisions belong in the medium-term planning and management decision-making process.

### S&OP outputs

The S&OP process should produce:

- Approved demand plan.
- Approved supply plan.
- Production and capacity plan.
- Inventory plan.
- Procurement and material requirements outlook.
- Financially reconciled business plan.
- Management decisions.
- Action items and assigned responsibilities.

These outputs become important inputs for S&OE.

## 4.2 The S&OE Process

Now let's move from planning to execution.

Unlike S&OP, S&OE is concerned with the immediate condition of the business.

Its purpose is to detect deviations early and coordinate actions before they create significant customer or financial consequences.

### Typical S&OE cycle

1. **Monitor actual performance**

   Check customer orders, production output, inventory, material availability and supplier deliveries.

2. **Identify deviations**

   Compare actual performance and near-term requirements against the approved plan.

3. **Evaluate operational impact**

   Determine affected SKUs, orders, production lines, delivery dates and customers.

4. **Develop corrective actions**

   Reschedule production, transfer available inventory, expedite materials or prioritize customer orders.

5. **Execute and monitor**

   Implement the agreed actions and monitor whether they resolve the problem.

6. **Escalate unresolved issues**

   Refer problems requiring broader capacity, financial, supply or management decisions to the appropriate decision-makers.

### A. Daily or weekly operational monitoring

S&OE teams monitor actual operational conditions.

For example:

| Parameter | Planned | Actual | Deviation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Production output | 10,000 | 9,200 | -800 |
| Customer demand | 10,000 | 11,500 | +1,500 |
| Available inventory | 3,000 | 2,200 | -800 |
| Material delivery | Nov 18 | Nov 20 | +2 days |

These deviations require investigation.

But not all deviations require management escalation.

Some can be resolved by the operational teams.

### B. Production schedule adjustments

Suppose the approved production schedule is:

| Product | Planned production |
|---|---:|
| A | 5,000 |
| B | 3,000 |
| C | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** |

However, Product A experiences an unexpected machine problem.

The production team estimates that only 3,000 units can be completed.

The S&OE team may coordinate with Production Planning to adjust the schedule:

| Product | Original plan | Revised plan |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 3,000 |
| B | 3,000 | 4,000 |
| C | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **9,000** |

The revised plan keeps production focused on available capacity and immediate customer requirements.

But there is now a 1,000-unit production gap.

The team must determine whether:

- Existing inventory can cover the shortage.
- Production can recover the lost output.
- Another line can manufacture the product.
- Customer delivery commitments need to be revised.

If the problem cannot be resolved within the current operational authority and planning constraints, it must be escalated.

### C. Managing material shortages

Example:

A critical raw material is required for production tomorrow.

However, the supplier informs Purchasing that the delivery will be delayed by three days.

S&OE coordinates:

1. Check available raw material inventory.
2. Identify affected production orders.
3. Check whether alternative materials are approved.
4. Determine whether another production order can be prioritized.
5. Coordinate with the supplier for an earlier delivery.
6. Evaluate the impact on customer commitments.

If the shortage only affects one production schedule and can be resolved through available inventory, S&OE may handle it.

But if the shortage affects several weeks of production and threatens the entire monthly supply plan, it becomes an S&OP-level concern.

### D. Customer order prioritization

Imagine that your company has 5,000 units available, but customer requirements total 7,000 units.

You cannot fulfill all orders immediately.

S&OE coordinates with Sales and Customer Service to determine fulfillment priorities.

Possible considerations:

- Customer contractual commitments.
- Required delivery dates.
- Strategic customer importance.
- Financial impact.
- Product availability.
- Allocation rules established by management.

The goal is not simply to give available inventory to whoever requests it first.

The goal is to make a controlled decision that balances customer service and business priorities.

## 4.3 Escalation: When does an operational problem become a planning problem?

This is arguably the most important analytical concept in Topic 8.9.

Not every operational problem should go to S&OP.

But some operational problems eventually become too significant to be managed through S&OE alone.

Let's distinguish them.

| Situation | Initial owner | Possible escalation |
|---|---|---|
| One-day machine breakdown | S&OE / Production | Maintenance or operational management |
| Material shortage affecting today's schedule | S&OE / Purchasing | Supply management if unresolved |
| Supplier delay affecting several months | S&OE / Procurement | S&OP |
| Demand increase for one customer | S&OE / Sales | S&OP if it materially changes the demand plan |
| Persistent capacity shortage | Production / S&OE | S&OP |
| Need for additional production equipment | Management | S&OP and strategic investment governance |
| Major inventory imbalance | S&OE / Inventory Planning | S&OP if it affects medium-term targets |

### The escalation principle

Use this question:

**Can we resolve the problem within the existing approved plan, available resources and delegated authority?**

If YES:

Handle it through S&OE.

If NO:

Evaluate escalation to S&OP or the appropriate management authority.

For example:

A machine breakdown lasting four hours may be resolved through schedule adjustments.

A machine breakdown lasting two months may require:

- Capacity reallocation.
- Overtime.
- Outsourcing.
- Additional production shifts.
- Revised customer commitments.
- Financial impact assessment.

The second situation is no longer simply a daily scheduling problem.

It affects the medium-term supply plan.

# 5. HOW S&OP AND S&OE WORK TOGETHER

Let's connect both processes into one integrated system.

## 5.1 S&OP provides direction to S&OE

Remember Topic 8.8?

We discussed how approved plans must be communicated and translated into operational responsibilities.

This is precisely where the connection happens.

S&OP establishes:

- What the company expects to sell.
- How much it needs to produce.
- What capacity should be available.
- How much inventory should be maintained.
- What supply commitments are required.
- What business priorities management has approved.

S&OE uses these plans as its operational reference.

For example:

S&OP approves a monthly production target of 100,000 units.

S&OE translates that target into near-term execution requirements:

| Period | Production target |
|---|---:|
| Week 1 | 25,000 |
| Week 2 | 25,000 |
| Week 3 | 25,000 |
| Week 4 | 25,000 |
| **Total** | **100,000** |

The weekly schedule may then be translated into daily production orders.

The exact breakdown depends on working days, capacity, demand timing and inventory requirements.

## 5.2 S&OE provides feedback to S&OP

The relationship also works in reverse.

S&OE continuously generates actual operational information.

For example:

| Metric | Approved plan | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly production | 100,000 | 92,000 |
| Customer demand | 100,000 | 108,000 |
| Supplier delivery reliability | 95% | 82% |
| Production availability | 95% | 87% |

These results may indicate that the approved plan is no longer achievable.

The information is then incorporated into the next S&OP review.

Management may need to reconsider:

- Future production capacity.
- Demand assumptions.
- Inventory targets.
- Supplier arrangements.
- Customer commitments.
- Financial projections.

This is called the **planning and execution feedback loop**.

## 5.3 Managing deviations from the approved plan

Let's introduce an important term.

**Plan Deviation** refers to the difference between the approved plan and actual or expected performance.

For example:

Production Deviation = Actual Production − Planned Production

If planned production is 100,000 units and actual production is 92,000 units:

92,000 − 100,000 = -8,000

The production deviation is -8,000 units.

The deviation itself does not explain the problem.

An analyst must investigate:

- Was there insufficient demand?
- Was production capacity unavailable?
- Were raw materials delayed?
- Was there a quality issue?
- Was there an unexpected equipment breakdown?

This distinction is important because the corrective action depends on the root cause.

### Three possible deviation outcomes

| Outcome | Description | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Recoverable | Temporary issue that can be corrected | S&OE corrective action |
| Manageable but persistent | Issue continues but remains within operational flexibility | S&OE monitoring and possible management escalation |
| Structural or material | Issue changes the medium-term business outlook | S&OP review and management decision |

## 5.4 Integrated S&OP-S&OE framework

1. **S&OP: Approve the business plan**

   Demand, supply, inventory, capacity and financial targets.

2. **S&OE: Execute the near-term plan**

   Daily and weekly production, material and customer order coordination.

3. **Monitor actual performance**

   Compare actual results against targets and identify exceptions.

4. **Evaluate and escalate**

   Resolve operational deviations or escalate material business impacts.

5. **Update the S&OP outlook**

   Incorporate actual performance, revised assumptions and management decisions.

This is a continuous feedback relationship, not two independent processes.

# 6. KPIs AND PERFORMANCE MONITORING

S&OP and S&OE use related KPIs, but they examine performance from different perspectives.

S&OP is concerned with whether the business plan is balanced and achievable.

S&OE is concerned with whether the organization is executing the near-term plan effectively.

## 6.1 S&OP KPIs

| KPI | What it measures | Business purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Forecast Accuracy | How close the forecast is to actual demand | Evaluate forecast reliability |
| Forecast Bias | Whether forecasts systematically overestimate or underestimate demand | Identify planning bias |
| Inventory Days of Supply | How long inventory can support demand | Monitor inventory position |
| Inventory Turns | How frequently inventory is consumed and replenished | Evaluate inventory efficiency |
| Capacity Utilization | How much available capacity is being used | Evaluate capacity requirements |
| Supply-Demand Balance | Whether planned supply can meet expected demand | Identify medium-term gaps |
| Revenue Plan Attainment | Actual or projected revenue against plan | Monitor financial alignment |
| Gross Margin | Profitability after cost of goods sold | Evaluate financial performance |

## 6.2 S&OE KPIs

| KPI | What it measures | Business purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Schedule Adherence | Actual production versus scheduled production | Monitor execution discipline |
| Production Attainment | Actual output versus production target | Monitor daily/weekly output |
| On-Time Delivery (OTD) | Deliveries made on or before the committed date | Monitor delivery performance |
| Order Fill Rate | Percentage of customer demand fulfilled | Monitor customer service |
| Material Availability | Availability of required production materials | Prevent production interruptions |
| Backlog | Outstanding customer orders not yet fulfilled | Monitor fulfillment risk |
| Supplier On-Time Delivery | Whether suppliers deliver as promised | Identify immediate supply risks |
| Downtime | Time production equipment is unavailable | Monitor production interruptions |

## 6.3 Important KPI distinctions

Let's examine three examples.

**Example 1: Forecast Accuracy**

S&OP asks:

"Was our demand forecast reasonably accurate over the planning period?"

S&OE asks:

"Are the actual customer orders received this week consistent with the near-term demand plan?"

The same demand information can be used differently depending on the planning horizon.

**Example 2: Capacity Utilization vs. Schedule Adherence**

S&OP focuses on whether the business has sufficient capacity to meet future demand.

S&OE focuses on whether the available production resources are following the approved short-term schedule.

A factory may have sufficient monthly capacity but still experience poor schedule adherence because of daily equipment failures.

**Example 3: Inventory**

S&OP evaluates whether inventory levels support the medium-term business strategy.

S&OE checks whether the required materials and finished goods are available to fulfill immediate production and customer requirements.

## 6.4 How operational KPIs affect business performance

Consider the following relationship:

**Low production schedule adherence**

↓

**Production output falls below plan**

↓

**Finished goods inventory decreases**

↓

**Customer orders cannot be fulfilled on time**

↓

**Revenue, customer satisfaction and financial performance are affected**

This is why S&OE is not merely a production scheduling activity.

Operational execution directly affects business results.

# 7. PRACTICAL MANUFACTURING CASE STUDY

Now let's put everything together.

This case study will simulate a realistic manufacturing situation involving unexpected demand, production downtime, raw material shortages and delayed supplier deliveries.

## 7.1 Business background

Imagine that you are an S&OP Analyst working for an electronic components manufacturer.

The company produces three product families: A, B and C.

The approved monthly S&OP plan is:

| Parameter | Approved plan |
|---|---:|
| Monthly demand | 100,000 units |
| Monthly production | 100,000 units |
| Available capacity | 110,000 units |
| Opening finished goods inventory | 10,000 units |
| Planned closing inventory | 10,000 units |

For simplicity, assume that all production is saleable, no scrap or quality losses occur, and the 10,000-unit opening inventory is available for customer fulfillment.

The monthly inventory equation is:

Closing Inventory = Opening Inventory + Production − Demand

= 10,000 + 100,000 − 100,000

= **10,000 units**

The plan is balanced.

## 7.2 Unexpected events

During the month, four events occur.

**Event 1: Unexpected customer demand**

A major customer requests an additional 15,000 units, increasing expected monthly demand from 100,000 to 115,000 units.

**Event 2: Production downtime**

A critical production machine breaks down, reducing expected monthly production from 100,000 to 90,000 units.

**Event 3: Raw material shortage**

Available raw materials are insufficient for the revised production schedule.

**Event 4: Supplier delivery delay**

The supplier delays the delivery of a critical material by seven days.

Now, what should happen?

Let's analyze each event.

## 7.3 S&OE response: Immediate operational actions

**Event 1: Unexpected customer demand**

The S&OE team should:

- Verify the additional customer order.
- Confirm the required delivery date.
- Check available finished goods.
- Determine whether existing production schedules can accommodate the order.
- Coordinate with Sales regarding delivery commitments.

The team should not automatically accept every additional order without checking material and capacity availability.

**Event 2: Production downtime**

The S&OE team should:

- Coordinate with Maintenance.
- Determine the expected equipment recovery time.
- Identify affected production orders.
- Evaluate alternative production lines.
- Reschedule unaffected products.
- Determine whether inventory can cover immediate requirements.

**Event 3: Raw material shortage**

The S&OE team should:

- Check available raw material inventory.
- Identify affected SKUs.
- Evaluate material allocation.
- Coordinate with Purchasing.
- Determine whether alternative approved materials are available.
- Reprioritize production based on material availability.

**Event 4: Supplier delivery delay**

The S&OE team should:

- Confirm the revised supplier delivery date.
- Request expedited delivery where feasible.
- Check alternative supplier availability.
- Recalculate production feasibility.
- Inform affected production and customer service teams.

These are operational responses.

But there is a bigger question:

Can the organization still achieve its approved monthly plan?

## 7.4 Quantifying the business impact

Let's consolidate the situation.

| Parameter | Original plan | Revised outlook |
|---|---:|---:|
| Opening inventory | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Production | 100,000 | 90,000 |
| Demand | 100,000 | 115,000 |
| Closing inventory | 10,000 | -15,000 |

Using the inventory equation:

10,000 + 90,000 − 115,000 = -15,000

A negative inventory balance indicates a projected shortage of 15,000 units.

This is a supply-demand imbalance.

### Projected supply-demand position

- Available supply including opening inventory: 100,000 units.
- Expected demand: 115,000 units.
- **Projected shortage: 15,000 units.**

*Illustrative calculation, assuming no additional receipts, recovery production or demand changes.*

At this point, S&OE can still investigate short-term recovery options.

However, if the company cannot recover the 15,000-unit gap within its existing operational flexibility, the issue requires broader management decisions.

## 7.5 S&OP response: Medium-term business decisions

Now the issue becomes a planning problem.

The management team needs to evaluate possible alternatives.

| Option | Description | Potential implications |
|---|---|---|
| Option A | Authorize overtime | Higher labor and operating costs |
| Option B | Transfer production to another line or facility | Additional coordination and capacity constraints |
| Option C | Expedite raw materials | Higher procurement and logistics costs |
| Option D | Outsource production | Additional cost and quality considerations |
| Option E | Negotiate revised customer delivery dates | Potential customer service and revenue impact |
| Option F | Reallocate available inventory | Other customers or product families may be affected |

These alternatives should be evaluated based on:

- Total recoverable production.
- Material availability.
- Additional operating costs.
- Customer priority.
- Revenue impact.
- Gross margin.
- Inventory implications.
- Feasibility within the remaining time.

S&OP or the relevant management authority then determines the broader business response.

For example, management may approve:

1. Additional overtime for two weeks.
2. Expedited delivery of critical raw materials.
3. Temporary production reallocation.
4. Revised delivery commitments for selected customers.

S&OE then translates those decisions into updated operational schedules.

**Notice the coordination: S&OP decides the broader response; S&OE implements and monitors it.**

## 7.6 End-to-end responsibility matrix

| Activity | S&OE | S&OP |
|---|---|---|
| Verify additional customer order | Responsible | Informed |
| Recalculate immediate material availability | Responsible | Informed |
| Reschedule daily production | Responsible | Informed |
| Coordinate equipment recovery | Responsible | Informed |
| Expedite supplier delivery | Responsible | May approve broader cost implications |
| Evaluate monthly capacity gap | Provides actual data | Responsible |
| Evaluate outsourcing alternatives | Provides feasibility information | Coordinates management decision |
| Approve major capacity changes | Provides analysis | Management decision |
| Revise medium-term business plan | Provides execution feedback | Responsible |
| Implement revised production schedule | Responsible | Monitors alignment |

The exact division of authority varies by company. The table illustrates a common operating model, not a universal RACI structure.

## 7.7 What should the S&OP Analyst report?

As the S&OP Analyst, you should not simply report:

"Production is delayed by 10,000 units."

You should communicate the complete business impact.

### Illustrative S&OP exception report

**Status: Critical supply-demand gap**

**Situation**

Unexpected demand increased by 15,000 units, while production downtime reduced expected monthly output by 10,000 units.

**Business impact**

Projected supply is 100,000 units against demand of 115,000 units, resulting in a 15,000-unit shortage.

**Root causes**

- Unexpected customer demand increase.
- Production equipment downtime.
- Raw material shortage.
- Seven-day supplier delivery delay.

**Immediate S&OE actions**

- Production schedule adjustment.
- Inventory allocation review.
- Supplier expediting.
- Evaluation of alternative production capacity.

**Management decisions required**

- Approval of additional operating expenditure.
- Production reallocation.
- Potential outsourcing.
- Customer delivery prioritization.

**Recommendation**

Evaluate a combined production recovery and material expediting strategy, subject to capacity, cost and customer commitment validation.

This is the difference between simply reporting operational data and supporting business decisions.

# 8. S&OP ANALYST'S ROLE

Now let's discuss your role in connecting these two processes.

An S&OP Analyst is not necessarily the person who directly manages production schedules or resolves supplier problems.

Your primary responsibility is to help the organization understand whether its plans remain achievable and what business risks require attention.

## 8.1 Monitoring planning assumptions and actual performance

Every S&OP plan is based on assumptions.

For example:

- Customer demand will increase by 5%.
- Production capacity will remain stable.
- Supplier lead times will remain at 30 days.
- Inventory will remain within the approved target.
- Equipment availability will remain at the expected level.

Your responsibility is to monitor whether those assumptions remain valid.

For example:

| Planning assumption | Actual observation | Potential implication |
|---|---|---|
| Demand growth of 5% | Demand increased by 15% | Demand plan may need revision |
| Stable production capacity | Repeated machine downtime | Future supply constraints |
| 30-day supplier lead time | Actual lead time is 45 days | Procurement and inventory risks |
| 95% production availability | Actual availability is 87% | Capacity shortfall |

An assumption that is no longer valid can make the approved plan unreliable.

## 8.2 Identifying deviations and potential risks

You should be able to distinguish between:

- Actual performance deviation.
- Forecast deviation.
- Supply constraint.
- Capacity constraint.
- Inventory risk.
- Customer service risk.

For example:

A production shortage may be caused by insufficient capacity, but it may also be caused by material unavailability.

The numerical result may look identical, but the corrective action is different.

Your analysis should identify the cause, not just the symptom.

## 8.3 Coordinating information between teams

An S&OP Analyst frequently works with:

| Department | Information required |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer orders, demand changes and forecast updates |
| Production | Actual output, production capacity and schedule adherence |
| Purchasing | Material availability and supplier delivery status |
| Inventory/Warehouse | Raw material and finished goods inventory |
| Finance | Revenue, operating costs and financial impact |
| Supply Planning | Supply constraints and production feasibility |
| Management | Business priorities and decisions |

Your role is to ensure that everyone works with consistent information.

This connects directly to Topic 8.7, where we discussed stakeholder responsibilities and decision rights.

## 8.4 Supporting escalation and management decisions

An S&OP Analyst should help answer five important questions:

1. What happened?
2. Why did it happen?
3. What is the impact on the approved plan?
4. What options are available?
5. What decision or support is required?

For example:

Instead of saying:

"Supplier delivery is delayed."

You should explain:

"Supplier delivery has shifted from November 18 to November 25. This affects three production orders and creates an estimated 8,000-unit supply risk. Available inventory can support production until November 20. Expedited delivery and temporary production reallocation are being evaluated. Management approval may be required if additional procurement costs exceed the operational team's authority."

That is professional S&OP communication.

## 8.5 Practical skills to develop

| Skill | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Excel and data analysis | Consolidate demand, supply and inventory data |
| Forecast analysis | Identify changes in demand assumptions |
| Variance analysis | Compare approved plans with actual results |
| KPI monitoring | Track planning and execution performance |
| Root cause analysis | Understand why deviations occur |
| Scenario analysis | Evaluate alternative business responses |
| Stakeholder coordination | Connect cross-functional information |
| Business communication | Present risks and recommendations to management |

Your manufacturing background provides a useful foundation here.

For example, when you analyze production yield, downtime, defect rates or process deviations, you are already practicing the type of analytical thinking that helps identify operational risks affecting an S&OP plan.

The next step is learning how to connect those operational observations to demand, supply, inventory and financial implications.

# 9. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Let's consolidate everything.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| S&OP | Establishes the medium-term integrated business plan |
| S&OE | Manages short-term execution and operational deviations |
| Planning horizon | S&OP is generally monthly and medium-term; S&OE is daily/weekly and short-term |
| Planning granularity | S&OP generally works with aggregated plans; S&OE works with detailed operational requirements |
| Decision-making | S&OP handles broader business trade-offs; S&OE handles immediate operational actions |
| Escalation | Problems exceeding operational flexibility or authority require broader management decisions |
| Feedback loop | S&OE provides actual performance information to S&OP |
| Analyst's role | Monitor deviations, identify risks, analyze impacts and support decisions |

### Five essential principles

1. **S&OP and S&OE are complementary processes.** S&OE does not replace S&OP.

2. **S&OP establishes the approved business direction.** S&OE translates that direction into short-term operational actions.

3. **Not every operational deviation requires an S&OP decision.** Resolve manageable issues at the appropriate operational level.

4. **Operational problems can become business-planning problems.** Persistent capacity shortages, supplier constraints and significant demand changes may require S&OP intervention.

5. **Execution feedback must return to planning.** An approved plan should not remain unchanged when actual business conditions materially change.

The entire Module 8 has been building toward one important understanding:

S&OP is not simply a monthly meeting where departments submit forecasts and production plans.

It is a management process that establishes an integrated business plan, assigns responsibilities, makes decisions and ensures that the organization works toward common objectives.

But an approved plan only creates value when it is executed.

That is why S&OE is essential.

**S&OP determines where the business needs to go. S&OE manages how the business gets there, day by day.**

And the S&OP Analyst helps ensure that the two remain connected through reliable data, meaningful analysis, early risk identification and effective communication.
