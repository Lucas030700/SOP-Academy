# Module 8 – Topic 8.4: Supply Review Meeting

**Main learning objective:** Understand how an organization determines whether its production capabilities, inventory, materials, suppliers, and other resources are sufficient to fulfill the agreed demand plan.

## 1. Topic Overview

### 1.1 What is a Supply Review Meeting?

Imagine na ang Sales Department ay nag-submit ng forecast na kailangan ng customers ng **10,000 units next month**.

Nagkaroon na ng Demand Review Meeting (Topic 8.3), at napagkasunduan na ang 10,000 units ang magiging official demand plan.

Pero may tanong:

**Kaya ba talagang gumawa at mag-deliver ng 10,000 units ang company?**

Consider these situations:

- May 2,000 units na available sa finished goods inventory.
- Production capacity is only 6,000 units.
- May shortage ng raw materials.
- Delayed ang delivery ng supplier.
- May production machine na scheduled for maintenance.
- May existing customer orders na hindi pa fulfilled.

Kahit 10,000 units ang demand, hindi ibig sabihin na automatic na kayang i-supply ng company ang quantity na iyon.

Dito pumapasok ang **Supply Review Meeting**.

> **What is a Supply Review Meeting?**
>
> A cross-functional meeting where the organization evaluates whether its available and planned resources can support the agreed demand plan, identifies supply constraints, and develops feasible supply alternatives.
>
> **Simplified meaning:**
>
> Demand Review asks: "How much do customers need?"
>
> Supply Review asks: "How much can we realistically supply?"

### 1.2 Why is it important?

The Supply Review prevents the company from creating a plan that looks good on paper but cannot be executed in actual operations.

Without it, companies may experience:

| Potential problem | Business consequence |
|---|---|
| Insufficient capacity | Production delays |
| Raw material shortages | Production interruptions |
| Supplier delays | Missed delivery commitments |
| Poor inventory planning | Stockouts or excess inventory |
| Unrealistic production schedules | Overtime and additional costs |
| Unidentified bottlenecks | Unfulfilled customer demand |

The main objective is not simply to maximize production.

It is to establish a supply plan that balances:

- Customer requirements
- Production capabilities
- Material availability
- Inventory requirements
- Operational costs
- Delivery commitments

### 1.3 Position within the monthly S&OP cycle

Let's connect this to the previous topics.

1. **Data Gathering and Preparation (8.2)**  
   Collect and validate demand, inventory, production, capacity, and supply data.

2. **Demand Review Meeting (8.3)**  
   Review forecasts, customer requirements, and demand assumptions. Establish the consensus demand plan.

3. **Supply Review Meeting (8.4)**  
   Evaluate whether available resources can support the consensus demand plan. Identify supply gaps and alternatives.

4. **Pre-S&OP Meeting (8.5)**  
   Reconcile demand and supply, evaluate alternatives, and prepare recommendations for executive decisions.

5. **Executive S&OP Meeting**  
   Senior management reviews the overall plan and makes decisions requiring leadership authority.

The important connection:

- Topic 8.2 provides the data.
- Topic 8.3 establishes the demand requirement.
- Topic 8.4 evaluates supply feasibility.
- Topic 8.5 reconciles the demand and supply plans.

### 1.4 Demand Review vs. Supply Review

This is one of the most important distinctions in S&OP.

| Dimension | Demand Review | Supply Review |
|---|---|---|
| Main concern | Customer demand | Supply capability |
| Main question | How much do we need? | How much can we supply? |
| Primary owner | Demand Planning / Sales | Supply Planning / Operations |
| Main inputs | Forecasts, orders, market information | Inventory, capacity, materials, production |
| Main analysis | Forecast accuracy and demand changes | Capacity, material, and inventory feasibility |
| Main output | Consensus demand plan | Proposed supply plan |
| Main risks | Demand uncertainty | Supply constraints |

**Important:** Supply Review does not independently change the agreed demand plan just because production cannot meet it.

Instead, it identifies the gap and develops alternatives.

For example:

- Demand plan: 10,000 units
- Available supply: 8,000 units
- Supply gap: 2,000 units

The organization must determine what to do with that 2,000-unit gap.

Possible options include overtime, additional shifts, subcontracting, inventory utilization, or negotiating delivery priorities.

## 2. Layman's Explanation

### 2.1 Think of a restaurant

Imagine may restaurant na nakatanggap ng orders for 500 meals tomorrow.

The restaurant manager already knows the expected customer demand.

Pero bago niya tanggapin ang lahat ng orders, kailangan niyang suriin:

- May enough ingredients ba?
- Ilan ang available cooks?
- Ilan ang kayang lutuin ng kitchen?
- May enough cooking equipment ba?
- May delayed supplier deliveries ba?
- May ingredients bang malapit nang maubos?

Suppose:

| Resource | Available |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 500 meals |
| Existing prepared meals | 50 |
| Kitchen production capacity | 350 meals |
| Available ingredients | 300 meals |

The restaurant cannot fulfill all 500 meals immediately.

Kahit kaya ng kitchen na mag-produce ng 350 meals, ingredients are only sufficient for 300 meals.

Therefore, the actual production constraint is not necessarily the kitchen capacity. It is the ingredient availability.

This introduces an important supply planning principle:

**Your production capability is limited by the availability of the resources required to produce.**

### 2.2 Applying the analogy to manufacturing

Palitan natin ang restaurant ng manufacturing company.

| Restaurant | Manufacturing |
|---|---|
| Customer meal orders | Customer demand |
| Prepared meals | Finished goods inventory |
| Ingredients | Raw materials |
| Cooks | Production workforce |
| Kitchen equipment | Production machines |
| Cooking capacity | Production capacity |
| Ingredient supplier | Raw material supplier |
| Unserved orders | Unfulfilled demand / backlog |

Ang Supply Review Meeting ay parang meeting ng restaurant manager, chef, purchasing manager, at inventory controller para malaman kung paano nila matutugunan ang orders.

Ang goal ay hindi lang malaman kung kaya o hindi kaya.

Ang goal ay malaman **kung paano magiging possible ang fulfillment, at ano ang magiging consequences ng bawat option.**

## 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the actual terminology used by Supply Planners and S&OP Analysts.

### 3.1 Consensus Demand Plan

The **Consensus Demand Plan** is the agreed demand quantity established during the Demand Review process.

It represents the organization's best agreed estimate of what customers will require during a particular planning period.

Example:

| Product | November demand |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 6,000 units |
| Product B | 4,000 units |
| **Total** | **10,000 units** |

This becomes the reference point for Supply Planning.

### 3.2 Supply Plan

The **Supply Plan** describes how the organization intends to meet the agreed demand using available resources.

It considers:

- Existing finished goods inventory
- Planned production
- Available production capacity
- Raw material availability
- Supplier deliveries
- Inventory targets
- Production lead times

Example:

| Product | Demand | Proposed production | Opening inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 6,000 | 5,000 | 1,000 |
| B | 4,000 | 3,000 | 500 |

The organization can supply all 6,000 units of A, but only 3,500 units of B.

Therefore, there is an outstanding shortage of 500 units of B.

### 3.3 Supply-Demand Alignment

Ang Supply Review ay naglalagay ng dalawang bagay sa iisang analysis:

1. What the business needs.
2. What the business can realistically provide.

The objective is to identify whether supply is sufficient, insufficient, or excessive relative to demand.

There are three possible situations:

| Situation | Meaning | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Supply = Demand | Supply can meet demand | Execute the plan |
| Supply less than Demand | Supply shortage | Increase supply or manage demand |
| Supply greater than Demand | Supply surplus | Review inventory and production |

One important distinction:

**Supply is not always equal to production.**

Total available supply can include existing finished goods, scheduled production, and other confirmed supply sources.

For example:

\[
\text{Total Available Supply}
=
\text{Opening Inventory}
+
\text{Planned Production}
+
\text{Confirmed Receipts}
\]

This distinction is fundamental when analyzing inventory and fulfillment.

### 3.4 Supply Constraints

A **Supply Constraint** is any limitation that prevents the company from producing or delivering the quantity required.

The major constraint categories are:

| Constraint | Example |
|---|---|
| Capacity | Machine can only produce 5,000 units |
| Material | Insufficient raw materials |
| Labor | Not enough qualified operators |
| Equipment | Machine breakdown |
| Supplier | Delayed material delivery |
| Logistics | Insufficient transportation capacity |
| Inventory | Insufficient finished goods |

A company can have sufficient production capacity but still experience a supply shortage because raw materials are unavailable.

Likewise, sufficient materials do not guarantee sufficient output if production capacity is constrained.

### 3.5 Capacity vs. Capability

These terms are related but not identical.

- **Capacity:** How much output can theoretically or practically be produced within a given period.
- **Capability:** Whether the organization has the necessary resources, skills, equipment, processes, and conditions to achieve the required output.

Example:

A production machine has enough theoretical capacity to produce 10,000 units per month.

However:
- Only one qualified operator is available.
- Maintenance requires two days.
- The machine has a high defect rate.
- Material feeding is limited.

The machine's theoretical capacity may be 10,000 units, but its realistic achievable output could be significantly lower.

This is why S&OP must use realistic capacity assumptions rather than machine specifications alone.

## 4. Detailed Discussion

### 4.1 Meeting Participants and Responsibilities

The Supply Review is cross-functional. Hindi ito trabaho ng Supply Planning Department lang.

Different departments provide different pieces of the supply picture.

| Department | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| Supply Planning | Consolidates demand and supply requirements; develops the supply plan |
| Production | Provides production schedules, output capability, and operational constraints |
| Operations | Evaluates manpower, equipment, efficiency, and execution risks |
| Procurement | Confirms material availability, purchase orders, and supplier commitments |
| Inventory Management | Provides stock levels, inventory coverage, and projected inventory |
| Logistics | Evaluates transportation, warehousing, and delivery capabilities |
| Finance | Evaluates financial impact, inventory costs, overtime, and additional expenses |
| Sales / Customer Service | Provides customer priorities and delivery requirements |

#### A. Supply Planner

The Supply Planner is generally responsible for translating demand requirements into a feasible supply plan.

Typical responsibilities:

**Before the meeting**
- Review the consensus demand plan.
- Collect inventory and production data.
- Validate supplier commitments.
- Calculate material requirements.
- Evaluate capacity.
- Identify potential shortages.

**During the meeting**
- Present supply-demand gaps.
- Challenge unrealistic production assumptions.
- Discuss constraints with Operations and Procurement.
- Evaluate alternative supply scenarios.

**After the meeting**
- Update the proposed supply plan.
- Document unresolved issues.
- Coordinate with responsible departments.
- Prepare information for Pre-S&OP.

#### B. Production and Operations

Their responsibility is to confirm whether the proposed production schedule is operationally achievable.

They provide information about:

- Available machines
- Production rates
- Shift schedules
- Planned maintenance
- Downtime
- Yield and scrap
- Manpower
- Production bottlenecks

For example, if Supply Planning proposes 10,000 units but Operations confirms that the production line can only produce 8,000, the discrepancy must be investigated.

#### C. Procurement

Procurement determines whether the required raw materials and components can arrive on time.

They review:

- Open purchase orders
- Supplier delivery dates
- Supplier lead times
- Minimum order quantities
- Material shortages
- Supplier reliability
- Alternative suppliers

#### D. Inventory Management

Inventory Management provides visibility into available and projected inventory.

It must distinguish between:

- On-hand inventory
- Available inventory
- Allocated inventory
- Quality-hold inventory
- In-transit inventory
- Safety stock

For example, the warehouse may report 5,000 units on hand, but 1,000 are allocated to existing customer orders and 500 are on quality hold.

Therefore, the usable inventory may be only 3,500 units.

#### E. Finance

Finance evaluates the economic implications of supply alternatives.

For example:

| Alternative | Financial implication |
|---|---|
| Overtime | Additional labor cost |
| Additional shift | Higher operating expenses |
| Subcontracting | External manufacturing cost |
| Expedited shipment | Higher logistics cost |
| Excess production | Higher inventory carrying cost |

The cheapest option is not always the best option. Delaying a major customer order might cost more than paying for overtime.

### 4.2 Roles of the Supply Planner vs. S&OP Analyst

These roles can overlap depending on company structure, but their typical focus differs.

| Supply Planner | S&OP Analyst |
|---|---|
| Develops supply requirements | Consolidates cross-functional information |
| Reviews detailed production schedules | Analyzes overall supply-demand alignment |
| Calculates material requirements | Highlights capacity and material risks |
| Coordinates execution with Operations | Prepares management-level reports |
| Updates operational supply plans | Tracks assumptions, decisions, and actions |
| Focuses on operational feasibility | Focuses on integrated business planning |

An S&OP Analyst does not necessarily need to create every production schedule.

However, the analyst must understand enough about production planning to recognize when a proposed plan is unrealistic.

For example:

If Operations reports that production will increase by 30%, the analyst should ask:

- Is additional machine capacity available?
- Is manpower sufficient?
- Are raw materials available?
- Is the production rate sustainable?
- Does the increase affect other products?
- What happens to inventory after production?

The analyst's role is to make sure that the information presented to management is consistent, explainable, and supported by data.

### 4.3 Supply Review Inputs

Before the meeting, participants need reliable information.

These are the major inputs.

| Input | What to review | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Approved demand plan | Product demand per month | Establishes supply requirements |
| Current inventory | Available FG and WIP | Determines existing supply |
| Projected inventory | Expected stock after transactions | Identifies future shortages |
| Production schedules | Planned output per line | Determines expected production |
| Production capacity | Available hours and rates | Determines feasible output |
| Raw materials | Available and required quantities | Identifies material constraints |
| Supplier commitments | Confirmed delivery dates | Determines future material availability |
| Procurement plans | Planned purchases | Supports future supply |
| Production performance | Actual vs. planned output | Validates planning assumptions |
| Downtime | Equipment availability | Reduces production capacity |
| Previous issues | Outstanding shortages and actions | Prevents recurring problems |

**Important: Data validation must happen before the meeting.**

For example, if the inventory report shows 10,000 units but the system includes obsolete or quality-hold inventory, the supply calculation may be overstated.

The Supply Review should not become a meeting for discovering basic data errors.

It should be a meeting for evaluating business implications and making decisions.

### 4.4 Supply Review Activities: Actual Meeting Flow

A typical Supply Review can follow this sequence.

1. **Review the consensus demand plan**  
   Confirm the product quantities and planning periods from Demand Review.

2. **Review current and projected inventory**  
   Identify which products have sufficient stock and which may experience shortages.

3. **Evaluate production capacity**  
   Determine whether available machines, manpower, and production hours can support the required output.

4. **Evaluate material availability**  
   Compare material requirements against on-hand inventory and confirmed incoming supply.

5. **Identify supply constraints**  
   Determine the reasons for any supply-demand gaps.

6. **Develop supply alternatives**  
   Evaluate overtime, additional shifts, expedited procurement, subcontracting, inventory utilization, and production prioritization.

7. **Establish the proposed supply plan**  
   Agree on a realistic plan, including unresolved issues and required escalations.

### 4.5 Evaluating Alternative Supply Strategies

When the supply plan cannot meet demand, the team evaluates different alternatives.

| Strategy | Advantages | Potential disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime | Increases available production hours | Higher labor cost and fatigue |
| Additional shifts | Increases production availability | Requires manpower and coordination |
| Subcontracting | Adds external capacity | Higher cost and quality-control risks |
| Expedite materials | Reduces material shortage | Higher transportation cost |
| Alternative supplier | Provides another source | Qualification and quality risks |
| Inventory utilization | Uses existing finished goods | May reduce safety stock |
| Production prioritization | Protects critical customer demand | Other customers may experience delays |
| Demand negotiation | Aligns demand with available supply | Requires customer agreement |

The key is to evaluate the consequences of each option, not just its ability to increase supply.

## 5. Supply Analysis and Calculations

This is where the S&OP Analyst starts becoming more analytical.

We will introduce the formulas first, then apply them to one manufacturing scenario.

### 5.1 Available Production Capacity

Production capacity tells us how much production can realistically be completed within the available time.

**Basic formula:**

\[
\text{Available Capacity}
=
\text{Available Production Hours}
\times
\text{Production Rate}
\]

Example:

A machine can produce 100 units per hour and has 80 available production hours.

\[
80 \times 100 = 8,000\text{ units}
\]

*Assumption: The production rate is expressed as good units per hour and already reflects normal operating efficiency.*

In actual manufacturing, available production hours may be calculated as:

\[
\text{Available Hours}
=
(\text{Scheduled Hours}-\text{Planned Downtime})
\]

If the production rate is not already adjusted for efficiency and yield:

\[
\text{Effective Capacity}
=
\text{Available Hours}
\times
\text{Gross Rate}
\times
\text{Efficiency}
\times
\text{Yield}
\]

Be careful not to apply efficiency or yield twice if the production rate already incorporates them.

### 5.2 Required Production Quantity

This formula determines how many units must be produced after considering opening inventory and the desired ending inventory.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Required Production}
=
\text{Demand}
+
\text{Target Ending Inventory}
-
\text{Opening Inventory}
}
\]

Example:

- Demand = 5,000 units
- Target ending inventory = 500 units
- Opening inventory = 1,000 units

\[
5,000+500-1,000
=
4,500\text{ units}
\]

The company needs to produce 4,500 units.

This assumes there are no additional receipts, losses, or inventory adjustments.

### 5.3 Capacity Utilization

Capacity utilization measures how much of the available production capacity is being used.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Capacity Utilization}
=
\frac{\text{Required or Planned Production Hours}}
{\text{Available Production Hours}}
\times100
}
\]

Example:

- Required production hours = 800
- Available production hours = 1,000

\[
\frac{800}{1,000}\times100
=
80\%
\]

Interpretation:

The production plan uses 80% of available capacity.

A utilization rate above 100% indicates that the planned workload exceeds the available hours.

Important: High utilization does not automatically mean good performance. Very high utilization may reduce flexibility to handle breakdowns, rush orders, and unexpected demand.

### 5.4 Projected Ending Inventory

This determines how much inventory is expected to remain at the end of a planning period.

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{Projected Ending Inventory}
=&\ \text{Opening Inventory}\\
&+\text{Production}\\
&+\text{Confirmed Receipts}\\
&-\text{Demand}\\
&-\text{Other Inventory Losses}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

Example:

- Opening inventory = 1,000 units
- Production = 4,500 units
- Demand = 5,000 units
- Other losses = 0

\[
1,000+4,500-5,000
=
500\text{ units}
\]

The projected ending inventory is 500 units.

### 5.5 Supply-Demand Gap

This measures the difference between total available supply and demand.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Supply-Demand Gap}
=
\text{Total Available Supply}
-
\text{Demand}
}
\]

Example:

- Total available supply = 8,000 units
- Demand = 10,000 units

\[
8,000-10,000=-2,000
\]

Interpretation:

There is a supply shortage of 2,000 units.

If the result is positive, there is supply remaining after demand is fulfilled, subject to inventory requirements and timing.

### 5.6 Material Availability

Material availability determines whether sufficient raw materials exist to support the production plan.

\[
\text{Material Requirement}
=
\text{Planned Production}
\times
\text{Material Consumption per Unit}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{Material Availability \%}
=
\frac{\text{Usable Available Material}}
{\text{Material Requirement}}
\times100
}
\]

Example:

- Planned production = 5,000 units
- Material consumption = 2 kg/unit
- Usable available material = 8,000 kg

Material requirement:

\[
5,000\times2=10,000\text{ kg}
\]

Availability:

\[
\frac{8,000}{10,000}\times100
=
80\%
\]

The company has 80% of the required material.

Material shortage:

\[
10,000-8,000=2,000\text{ kg}
\]

This assumes the material is usable, available in the required period, and suitable for the product.

### 5.7 Backlog and Unfulfilled Demand

Backlog represents customer demand that has not yet been fulfilled.

A basic calculation is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Unfulfilled Demand}
=
\max(0,\text{Demand}-\text{Available Supply})
}
\]

Example:

- Demand = 5,000 units
- Available supply = 4,200 units

\[
5,000-4,200=800
\]

There are 800 units of unfulfilled demand.

For cumulative backlog:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Ending Backlog}
=&\ \text{Beginning Backlog}\\
&+\text{New Orders Due}\\
&-\text{Orders Fulfilled}\\
&-\text{Orders Cancelled}
\end{aligned}
\]

Note that backlog and current-period unfulfilled demand are related but not always identical. Backlog can include orders carried forward from previous periods.

### 5.8 Production Plan vs. Actual Output

This measures the difference between planned and actual production.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Production Variance}
=
\text{Actual Output}
-
\text{Planned Output}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{Production Attainment}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Output}}
{\text{Planned Output}}
\times100
}
\]

Example:

- Planned production = 10,000 units
- Actual production = 9,200 units

Variance:

\[
9,200-10,000=-800
\]

Attainment:

\[
\frac{9,200}{10,000}\times100
=
92\%
\]

Interpretation:

Production achieved 92% of its plan, with an output shortfall of 800 units.

The analyst should investigate the reason:

- Machine downtime?
- Material shortage?
- Manpower shortage?
- Lower production rate?
- Higher defect or scrap rate?
- Changeover delays?

A production variance is a signal. The underlying cause is what management needs to understand.

## 6. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's combine everything into one realistic manufacturing case.

This is the most important part of the lesson because dito natin makikita kung paano ginagamit ang calculations during an actual Supply Review Meeting.

### 6.1 Manufacturing Scenario

Imagine you are working as an S&OP Analyst in a manufacturing company producing two products:

- Product A
- Product B

The Demand Review Meeting has already established the following demand plan for November.

> **November Consensus Demand Plan**
>
> | Product | Agreed demand |
> |---|---:|
> | Product A | 6,000 units |
> | Product B | 4,000 units |
> | **Total** | **10,000 units** |
>
> *These are agreed demand quantities, not yet confirmed production commitments.*

The Supply Planning team now needs to determine whether the company can meet this demand.

### 6.2 Step 1: Review Current Finished Goods Inventory

Inventory Management provides the following information:

| Product | Opening inventory |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 1,000 units |
| Product B | 500 units |
| **Total** | **1,500 units** |

Assume that all inventory is usable, unallocated, and available for November demand.

For simplicity, there are no other confirmed finished goods receipts.

**Initial interpretation:**

The company already has 1,500 units available before producing anything.

Therefore, the company does not necessarily need to manufacture the entire 10,000-unit demand.

### 6.3 Step 2: Calculate Required Production

For this scenario, assume the target ending inventory is zero for both products.

Formula:

\[
\text{Required Production}
=
\text{Demand}-\text{Opening Inventory}
\]

| Product | Demand | Opening inventory | Required production |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 6,000 | 1,000 | 5,000 |
| B | 4,000 | 500 | 3,500 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **1,500** | **8,500** |

The initial production requirement is 8,500 units.

However, this is only the required quantity. We still need to verify whether the factory can produce it.

### 6.4 Step 3: Evaluate Production Capacity

Operations provides the following production information:

| Parameter | Product A | Product B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Production time per unit | 0.50 hour | 0.75 hour |
| Required production | 5,000 | 3,500 |
| Required production hours | 2,500 | 2,625 |

Calculate total required hours:

\[
2,500+2,625=5,125\text{ hours}
\]

Operations confirms that the factory has only 4,800 available production hours for November.

**Capacity assessment**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Required hours | 5,125 |
| Available hours | 4,800 |
| **Capacity shortage** | **325 hours** |

The required production exceeds available capacity.

Calculate capacity utilization:

\[
\frac{5,125}{4,800}\times100
=
106.77\%
\]

**Interpretation:**

The production plan requires 106.77% of available capacity.

This means the current production requirement cannot be completed within the available production hours.

The team needs additional capacity or a revised production allocation.

### 6.5 Step 4: Evaluate Raw Material Availability

Procurement provides the following material consumption requirements:

| Product | Material requirement per unit |
|---|---:|
| A | 1 kg |
| B | 2 kg |

Calculate total raw material requirements.

**Product A:**

\[
5,000\times1=5,000\text{ kg}
\]

**Product B:**

\[
3,500\times2=7,000\text{ kg}
\]

Total requirement:

\[
5,000+7,000=12,000\text{ kg}
\]

However, Procurement confirms that only 11,000 kg will be available during November.

The remaining 1,000 kg is delayed because of a supplier delivery issue.

**Material availability assessment**

| Parameter | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Required materials | 12,000 kg |
| Available materials | 11,000 kg |
| Material shortage | 1,000 kg |
| Material availability | 91.67% |

*The calculation assumes the 11,000 kg is usable and available before the required production dates.*

**Interpretation:**

Even if Operations could provide additional production hours, the company would still be unable to complete the original production requirement because of insufficient raw materials.

This is an important analytical distinction:

- Capacity shortage: 325 hours.
- Material shortage: 1,000 kg.

These are two separate constraints that require separate corrective actions.

### 6.6 Step 5: Develop Alternative Supply Plans

Now the Supply Review team needs to evaluate possible solutions.

#### Scenario 1: Maintain existing resources

The company uses only its available 4,800 production hours and 11,000 kg of materials.

The team prioritizes Product A and allocates the remaining resources to Product B.

Proposed production:

| Product | Required production | Proposed production |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| B | 3,500 | 3,000 |
| **Total** | **8,500** | **8,000** |

Check production hours:

| Product | Production | Hours per unit | Total hours |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 0.50 | 2,500 |
| B | 3,000 | 0.75 | 2,250 |
| **Total** | | | **4,750** |

Capacity utilization:

\[
\frac{4,750}{4,800}\times100
=
98.96\%
\]

Check material consumption:

| Product | Production | Material per unit | Required material |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 1 kg | 5,000 kg |
| B | 3,000 | 2 kg | 6,000 kg |
| **Total** | | | **11,000 kg** |

Both capacity and material requirements are within available resources.

Now calculate projected inventory and unfulfilled demand.

| Product | Opening inventory | Production | Demand | Ending inventory | Backlog |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | 5,000 | 6,000 | 0 | 0 |
| B | 500 | 3,000 | 4,000 | 0 | 500 |

**Result: 500 units unfulfilled**

Interpretation:

- Product A demand is fully satisfied.
- Product B has a shortage of 500 units.
- No finished goods inventory remains.
- Production stays within available capacity.
- Raw material availability is fully utilized.

This is operationally feasible, but it does not fulfill the entire consensus demand plan.

#### Scenario 2: Overtime and expedited materials

Instead of accepting a 500-unit backlog, the company evaluates whether it can fulfill the original demand.

Required additional resources:

- Additional 325 production hours.
- Additional 1,000 kg of raw materials.

Proposed actions:

1. Authorize 325 hours of overtime or equivalent additional capacity.
2. Request expedited delivery of the delayed 1,000 kg.
3. Confirm that the expedited material arrives before the production schedule requires it.

If both conditions are achieved:

| Metric | Result |
|---|---:|
| Required production | 8,500 units |
| Available production hours after overtime | 5,125 hours |
| Required production hours | 5,125 hours |
| Material availability after expedited delivery | 12,000 kg |
| Unfulfilled demand | 0 units |

Capacity utilization:

\[
\frac{5,125}{5,125}\times100=100\%
\]

This scenario can fulfill all customer demand, but it creates additional operating and procurement costs.

It also has practically no remaining capacity buffer.

#### Scenario 3: Subcontracting or external production

The company evaluates outsourcing part of Product B production.

For illustration, assume an external manufacturer can produce 500 units of Product B using its own materials and capacity.

The internal factory then produces:

- Product A: 5,000 units.
- Product B: 3,000 units.

External manufacturer:

- Product B: 500 units.

Total available supply:

| Source | Product A | Product B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Opening inventory | 1,000 | 500 |
| Internal production | 5,000 | 3,000 |
| External production | 0 | 500 |
| **Total** | **6,000** | **4,000** |

The entire demand plan can theoretically be fulfilled.

However, the team must confirm:

- Is the external manufacturer qualified?
- Can it meet product specifications?
- Is the external production lead time acceptable?
- Are there additional transportation costs?
- Is the subcontracting cost lower than the cost of delayed orders?
- Can the outsourced quantity arrive within November?

Subcontracting is not automatically feasible just because an external company has capacity.

### 6.7 Step 6: Compare the Alternatives

Here is how the S&OP team should present the alternatives.

| Evaluation | Scenario 1 | Scenario 2 | Scenario 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal overtime | None | 325 hours | None |
| Expedited materials | None | 1,000 kg | Depends on sourcing |
| Subcontracting | None | None | 500 units |
| Demand fulfillment | 95% | 100% | 100% |
| Internal capacity utilization | 98.96% | 100% | 98.96% |
| Main risk | Backlog | No capacity buffer; delivery risk | Quality and supplier risk |

*Scenario 3 assumes the subcontractor can deliver all 500 units on time using its own materials. Demand fulfillment percentages are unit-weighted for this example.*

### 6.8 Step 7: Establish the Recommended Supply Plan

For this example, assume:

- Product A is a priority customer product.
- Product B has a customer delivery window that allows a short delay.
- Expedited raw materials are expensive.
- Subcontracting is not yet qualified.

The Supply Review team recommends Scenario 1 as the baseline supply plan, with a formal escalation for the 500-unit Product B backlog.

Why?

1. It uses confirmed resources.
2. It avoids relying on unconfirmed supplier recovery.
3. It avoids unapproved overtime.
4. It does not introduce unqualified external production.
5. It provides a realistic and executable production plan.

However, this is not a final customer commitment. The 500-unit backlog must be presented to Pre-S&OP for evaluation, including the commercial impact and potential recovery options.

> **Recommended November Supply Plan**
>
> | KPI | Proposed result |
> |---|---:|
> | Total demand | 10,000 units |
> | Internal production | 8,000 units |
> | Opening inventory | 1,500 units |
> | Total supply | 9,500 units |
> | Fulfilled demand | 9,500 units |
> | Unfulfilled demand | 500 units |
> | Capacity utilization | 98.96% |
> | Material utilization | 100% |
> | Ending inventory | 0 units |
>
> **Operationally feasible baseline**
>
> **Management attention required:** Resolve the 500-unit Product B shortage and assess whether the cost of recovering the demand is justified.

### 6.9 What should the S&OP Analyst conclude?

The analyst should not simply report:

"Production cannot meet demand."

A professional analysis should explain:

**What is the problem?**

The original production requirement exceeds available capacity by 325 hours and raw material availability by 1,000 kg.

**What is the business impact?**

Without additional resources, the company expects 500 units of Product B to remain unfulfilled.

**What are the available alternatives?**

Overtime and expedited materials, subcontracting, or accepting a temporary backlog.

**What is the recommendation?**

Use the confirmed-resource production plan as the baseline while escalating the Product B shortage and evaluating recovery alternatives.

**What decisions are required?**

Management must determine whether additional expenditure is justified to recover the 500 units within November.

That is the difference between simply preparing reports and performing actual S&OP analysis.

## 7. Supply Review Outputs

After the Supply Review Meeting, the team should have documented outputs that will serve as inputs for Pre-S&OP.

Hindi sapat na nag-meeting lang at nag-discuss ng problems. Dapat may consolidated supply position, proposed actions, and unresolved decisions.

### 7.1 Main Outputs

| Output | Description |
|---|---|
| Proposed Supply Plan | Agreed proposed production and supply quantities |
| Capacity Assessment | Available capacity, utilization, and capacity gaps |
| Material Assessment | Material requirements, availability, and shortages |
| Inventory Projection | Expected inventory and potential stockouts |
| Supply-Demand Gap | Products and quantities that cannot be supplied |
| Supply Alternatives | Different options to resolve supply constraints |
| Operational Risks | Downtime, supplier delays, manpower limitations, etc. |
| Escalation Items | Decisions that require higher management authority |
| Action Items | Assigned corrective actions and deadlines |

### 7.2 Sample Supply Review Summary

This is an example of how an S&OP Analyst can summarize the results.

| Area | Finding | Required action |
|---|---|---|
| Product A | Demand fully supported | Proceed with production plan |
| Product B | 500-unit supply shortage | Evaluate recovery options |
| Capacity | 325-hour shortage against original plan | Assess additional capacity |
| Materials | 1,000 kg shortage against original requirement | Coordinate with Procurement |
| Inventory | No projected ending inventory | Monitor stock and replenishment |
| Supplier | Delayed material delivery | Confirm recovery schedule |

### 7.3 What gets transferred to Pre-S&OP?

Pre-S&OP needs a clear understanding of the supply situation.

The Supply Review provides:

1. The proposed supply plan.
2. The demand quantities that can be fulfilled.
3. The demand quantities that cannot be fulfilled.
4. The reasons for supply constraints.
5. The financial and operational implications of alternatives.
6. Decisions that require management involvement.

The important principle:

**Supply Review identifies and analyzes the problem. Pre-S&OP reconciles the competing business requirements and develops an integrated recommendation.**

For example:

Supply Review:

"We can only fulfill 9,500 out of 10,000 units using currently confirmed resources."

Pre-S&OP:

"Should we spend additional money to recover the remaining 500 units, prioritize certain customers, or accept delayed fulfillment?"

That is the transition between the two meetings.

## 8. Supply Review in Professional S&OP Practice

Let's now discuss how you would actually perform this responsibility as an S&OP Analyst.

This section is especially important for your professional development.

### 8.1 Preparing Supply Review Reports

Before the meeting, an analyst should prepare a consolidated report.

A practical report structure would include:

**A. Demand and Supply Summary**

| Product | Demand | Available supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 6,000 | 6,000 | 0 |
| B | 4,000 | 3,500 | -500 |
| Total | 10,000 | 9,500 | -500 |

**B. Capacity Summary**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Available hours | 4,800 |
| Required hours | 5,125 |
| Proposed production hours | 4,750 |
| Capacity utilization | 98.96% |
| Original capacity shortage | 325 hours |

**C. Material Summary**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Required materials for original plan | 12,000 kg |
| Available materials | 11,000 kg |
| Material shortage | 1,000 kg |
| Material utilization under proposed plan | 100% |

**D. Inventory and Backlog**

Show:

- Beginning inventory
- Planned production
- Projected ending inventory
- Expected stockouts
- Backlog
- Safety stock deviations

**E. Risks and Recommendations**

Highlight the constraints that could affect customer fulfillment and business performance.

### 8.2 How to Present Capacity, Inventory, and Material Risks

A common mistake is presenting only numerical values without explaining their implications.

For example:

Weak presentation:

"Capacity utilization is 98.96%."

Better presentation:

"Proposed production will consume 98.96% of available capacity. Although the current plan is feasible, only 50 production hours remain available. Any unplanned downtime or production inefficiency could affect the November supply commitment."

Notice the difference?

The second statement connects the number to an operational risk.

Another example:

Weak presentation:

"Material availability is 91.67%."

Better presentation:

"Raw material availability is only 91.67% of the original production requirement due to a supplier delay. This creates a 1,000 kg shortage and limits the company's ability to fulfill Product B demand without additional material supply."

An S&OP Analyst must communicate three things:

1. What happened?
2. Why does it matter?
3. What action is needed?

### 8.3 How to Challenge Unrealistic Production Assumptions

One of the most valuable responsibilities of an S&OP Analyst is questioning assumptions.

Let's say Operations proposes:

"We can increase production by 20% next month."

Instead of accepting that immediately, ask:

| Area | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Capacity | Are enough production hours available? |
| Equipment | Can the machines sustain the higher production rate? |
| Manpower | Are additional operators required? |
| Materials | Can Procurement provide sufficient raw materials? |
| Supplier | Are the delivery commitments confirmed? |
| Yield | Will increased speed affect product quality? |
| Maintenance | Is additional downtime expected? |
| Inventory | Will the additional output create excess stocks? |

A production plan must be supported by realistic assumptions.

For example, a company may have sufficient machine capacity but insufficient raw materials.

Or it may have sufficient materials but insufficient manpower.

The analyst should identify these contradictions before the plan reaches executive management.

### 8.4 How to Coordinate with Other Departments

Supply Review requires continuous communication.

Consider this example:

1. **Supply Planning**  
   Identifies that Product B requires 3,500 units of production.

2. **Production / Operations**  
   Confirms that available capacity supports only 3,000 units under the current resource plan.

3. **Procurement**  
   Confirms that the supplier has delayed the delivery of 1,000 kg of raw material.

4. **Inventory Management**  
   Confirms that only 500 units of Product B are available in opening inventory.

5. **Sales / Customer Service**  
   Evaluates whether the 500-unit shortage can be accommodated through revised delivery commitments.

6. **Finance**  
   Evaluates the financial implications of overtime, expedited materials, and subcontracting.

7. **S&OP Analyst**  
   Consolidates the information, identifies the supply-demand gap, evaluates alternatives, and prepares the recommendation for Pre-S&OP.

The S&OP Analyst acts as a connector between departments.

Hindi kailangang siya ang gumawa ng lahat ng operational decisions. But the analyst should understand how departmental decisions affect the overall business plan.

### 8.5 Documenting Decisions, Risks, and Action Items

Every significant issue should have an owner and expected completion date.

Example:

| Issue | Action | Responsible department | Due date |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 kg material shortage | Confirm revised supplier delivery | Procurement | Nov. 18 |
| 325-hour capacity shortage | Evaluate overtime availability | Operations | Nov. 17 |
| 500-unit backlog | Assess customer delivery impact | Sales | Nov. 19 |
| Subcontracting option | Evaluate qualification and cost | Procurement / Quality | Nov. 20 |

These are illustrative dates.

A useful action tracker should contain:

- Issue description
- Root cause
- Business impact
- Agreed action
- Responsible owner
- Due date
- Current status
- Escalation requirement

**Important professional practice:** An action item is not complete just because somebody says they will investigate it.

The action must have an owner, deadline, and measurable expected outcome.

### 8.6 Recommended Supply Review Report Structure

For your future S&OP work, remember this report architecture.

> **Supply Review Report**
>
> 1. **Executive Summary**  
>    Overall supply position, major risks, and critical findings.
>
> 2. **Demand vs. Supply**  
>    Demand plan, available supply, and supply-demand gaps.
>
> 3. **Production and Capacity**  
>    Required hours, available capacity, utilization, and constraints.
>
> 4. **Inventory Position**  
>    Opening stock, projected ending inventory, coverage, and backlog.
>
> 5. **Material Availability**  
>    Material requirements, shortages, and supplier commitments.
>
> 6. **Supply Scenarios**  
>    Alternative production and procurement strategies with trade-offs.
>
> 7. **Risks and Recommendations**  
>    Proposed actions, decisions required, and management escalations.
>
> 8. **Action Tracker**  
>    Responsible owners, due dates, and status.

## 9. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate the important lessons.

### 9.1 Concepts you must understand

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Supply Review | Evaluates whether the company can support agreed demand |
| Consensus Demand Plan | Agreed customer demand from Demand Review |
| Supply Plan | Proposed method of fulfilling demand |
| Production Capacity | Available production capability within a period |
| Capacity Utilization | Percentage of available capacity being used |
| Material Availability | Whether sufficient raw materials are available |
| Projected Ending Inventory | Expected stock remaining after supply and demand |
| Supply-Demand Gap | Difference between available supply and demand |
| Backlog | Unfulfilled customer demand |
| Supply Constraint | Limitation preventing the fulfillment of demand |

### 9.2 Five principles to remember

**1. Demand does not automatically equal supply.**

A company can have 10,000 units of demand but only enough resources to supply 9,000.

**2. Production capacity and material availability must both be evaluated.**

Sufficient capacity does not guarantee sufficient materials.

**3. Inventory is part of supply.**

The company does not always need to manufacture the entire demand quantity.

**4. A feasible plan is more valuable than an unrealistic plan.**

Do not assume production can exceed available resources without a documented recovery strategy.

**5. Supply Review is about business decisions, not just calculations.**

The numbers identify the problem. The analysis determines the implications and possible solutions.
