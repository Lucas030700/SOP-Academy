# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What are Developing Recommendations?

Imagine that you are an S&OP Analyst in a manufacturing company.

Your company receives an unexpected increase in customer demand. However, the production facility is already operating near maximum capacity, and one critical raw material is experiencing a supply shortage.

You analyze the situation and discover:

- Demand increased by 20%.
- Available production capacity cannot accommodate the entire increase.
- A critical raw material has a four-week supplier lead time.
- Some customer orders may be delayed.
- Expediting materials and overtime production are possible, but both increase costs.

You have already completed your analysis.

But management asks:

**"Based on your analysis, what should we do?"**

This is where Developing Recommendations comes in.

Hindi sapat na sabihin mo lang kung ano ang problema. Kailangan mong sabihin kung ano ang dapat gawin, bakit iyon ang pinakamagandang option, magkano ang magiging impact, at ano ang risks na kailangan nilang tanggapin.

In simple terms:

**Developing Recommendations = Converting business analysis into proposed actions supported by evidence, calculations, and sound business judgment.**

## 1.2 Why is this important in S&OP?

Remember that S&OP is not simply a process for reviewing reports and discussing operational performance.

Its ultimate purpose is to align business functions and support decisions that balance:

- Customer demand
- Production and supply capability
- Inventory availability
- Operational capacity
- Financial objectives
- Business priorities

An S&OP analyst must therefore go beyond identifying problems.

The analyst must help management understand the available choices and their consequences.

For example:

| Analysis result | Management recommendation |
|---|---|
| Production capacity is insufficient | Implement overtime or allocate production to another facility |
| Raw material shortage | Expedite critical materials or qualify an alternative supplier |
| Inventory is excessive | Reduce replenishment or redirect inventory |
| Customer demand exceeds supply | Prioritize orders based on business criteria |
| Production cost is increasing | Evaluate alternative production schedules |

Notice the difference: the first column tells management what is happening. The second tells management what can be done.

## 1.3 Why is this included in Module 6?

This is the concluding integration lesson because all the previous topics contribute to recommendation development.

Let's connect them.

| Previous Module 6 topic | Contribution to developing recommendations |
|---|---|
| Demand-Supply Matching | Identifies whether demand and supply are aligned |
| Supply Gap Analysis | Quantifies how much supply is missing |
| Balancing Strategies | Identifies possible ways to resolve the imbalance |
| Demand Prioritization | Determines which customers or orders should receive priority |
| Scenario Planning | Evaluates different possible business situations |
| Trade-off Analysis | Evaluates the advantages and disadvantages of competing alternatives |
| **Developing Recommendations** | **Combines the analysis and proposes the best course of action** |

Think of the previous topics as individual analytical tools.

Topic 6.7 teaches you how to combine those tools into one coherent business decision.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Reporting vs. Recommending

Let's use a simple example.

You are managing a bakery.

Your customers normally purchase 1,000 loaves of bread daily. Suddenly, demand increases to 1,300 loaves.

However, your bakery can only produce 1,100 loaves per day.

You have a shortage of 200 loaves.

Different ways of communicating this situation produce different levels of business value.

**Level 1: Reporting Findings**

"Our daily demand is 1,300 loaves, while our production capacity is only 1,100."

You are reporting the numbers.

**Level 2: Identifying the Problem**

"We have a daily supply gap of 200 loaves because our production capacity cannot accommodate the increased demand."

You have identified the problem.

**Level 3: Evaluating Alternatives**

"We can introduce overtime, purchase additional equipment, or outsource part of our production."

You are identifying possible solutions and evaluating them.

**Level 4: Developing a Recommendation**

"I recommend introducing overtime for the next two weeks because it can increase production by 200 loaves daily without requiring additional capital investment. However, we should monitor overtime costs and production efficiency. If demand remains elevated beyond two weeks, we should evaluate additional equipment."

Now you are helping management make a decision.

The progression from data to decision:

1. **DATA:** Demand: 1,300 | Capacity: 1,100
2. **FINDING:** Supply gap: 200 units
3. **ANALYSIS:** Capacity and resource constraints identified
4. **ALTERNATIVES:** Overtime, outsourcing, equipment investment
5. **RECOMMENDATION:** Implement overtime for two weeks and reassess

The important lesson:

**An analyst does not simply explain what happened. An analyst helps the business determine what should happen next.**

## 2.2 A Simple Analogy: Doctor and Patient

Imagine visiting a doctor.

The doctor performs several activities:

1. Collects information about your condition.
2. Examines the symptoms.
3. Identifies the possible cause.
4. Evaluates available treatments.
5. Recommends the most appropriate treatment.
6. Explains the benefits and possible risks.

An S&OP analyst follows a similar reasoning process.

| Medical process | S&OP process |
|---|---|
| Patient symptoms | Business performance problems |
| Medical examination | Data analysis |
| Diagnosis | Root cause identification |
| Treatment options | Business alternatives |
| Treatment recommendation | Recommended business action |
| Recovery monitoring | KPI monitoring |

A doctor who only tells you that you have a fever but never recommends what to do is not completing the job.

Likewise, an analyst who only presents a supply gap without proposing an appropriate action is providing incomplete decision support.

# 3. Technical Explanation

## 3.1 Formal Definition

In professional business management, a recommendation is a proposed course of action developed through systematic analysis of business conditions, constraints, objectives, alternatives, risks, and expected outcomes.

In S&OP, recommendations are developed to resolve demand-supply imbalances while considering operational feasibility, customer requirements, inventory implications, and financial performance.

A good recommendation should answer five fundamental questions:

| Question | What management needs to understand |
|---|---|
| What is happening? | Current business situation |
| Why is it happening? | Root cause and constraints |
| What can we do? | Available alternatives |
| What should we do? | Recommended course of action |
| What will happen if we do it? | Expected impact, cost, and risks |

## 3.2 Four Different Analytical Activities

These four activities are related, but they are not interchangeable.

| Activity | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting Findings | Describe what the data shows | Demand increased by 20% |
| Problem Identification | Explain the business issue | Capacity cannot support the new demand |
| Evaluation of Alternatives | Compare possible solutions | Overtime vs. outsourcing vs. subcontracting |
| Recommendation | Propose the preferred action | Use overtime and selectively outsource the remaining gap |

One common mistake among beginner analysts is jumping directly from findings to recommendations.

For example:

"Demand increased by 20%, therefore we should increase production."

This is incomplete reasoning.

Why?

Because increasing production may not be possible if:
- Machines are already at maximum capacity.
- Raw materials are insufficient.
- Labor availability is limited.
- Production costs exceed the expected benefit.

A recommendation must be supported by a complete understanding of the business situation.

## 3.3 Characteristics of a Strong Recommendation

A professional recommendation should possess the following characteristics.

| Characteristic | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Evidence-based | Supported by reliable business data |
| Actionable | Clearly states what needs to happen |
| Measurable | Includes expected results or KPIs |
| Feasible | Can realistically be implemented |
| Financially justified | Considers costs, savings, and benefits |
| Risk-aware | Identifies possible negative consequences |
| Time-bound | Defines when the action should happen |
| Accountable | Identifies who is responsible |

Consider these two recommendations:

**Weak recommendation:**

"We should improve production capacity to address the increasing demand."

Problems:
- No specific action.
- No quantified impact.
- No implementation timeline.
- No identified owner.
- No cost or feasibility assessment.

**Strong recommendation:**

"Implement a temporary second shift on Line 2 for the next four weeks to increase weekly production capacity by 800 units. This will reduce the projected supply gap by 80%. Production and HR will coordinate manpower requirements, while Finance will monitor incremental labor costs. Review the results weekly."

This is significantly more useful because management can evaluate and authorize the proposed action.

# 4. Detailed Discussion: From Analysis to Recommendation

This is the most important part of the lesson.

We will establish a systematic process that you can eventually use in your actual S&OP analyst role.

## 4.1 The Seven-Step Recommendation Development Process

1. **Understand the Business Problem:** Determine what is happening, how significant it is, and which business objectives are affected.
2. **Identify Root Causes and Constraints:** Determine why the problem exists and what prevents the business from achieving its objectives.
3. **Establish Objectives and Decision Criteria:** Define what the business wants to achieve and how alternatives will be evaluated.
4. **Develop Possible Solutions:** Identify different actions that could resolve the problem.
5. **Evaluate Alternatives:** Compare operational feasibility, financial impact, customer impact, and risks.
6. **Select and Justify the Recommended Option:** Choose the most appropriate alternative and explain why it is preferred.
7. **Define Implementation and Monitoring:** Assign owners, deadlines, expected outcomes, and performance measures.

Let's understand each step.

### Step 1: Understand the Business Problem

Before recommending anything, you must understand the actual problem.

For example:

A manufacturing company receives a demand forecast of 12,000 units for next month.

Its available supply is only 10,000 units.

Initial finding:

[
Supply\ Gap = Demand - Supply
]

[
=12,000-10,000=2,000\ units
]

You now know that the company has a projected shortage of 2,000 units.

However, several questions remain:

- Is the demand increase temporary or permanent?
- Is the shortage caused by production capacity?
- Are materials unavailable?
- Is the production schedule inefficient?
- Is existing inventory allocated to other customers?
- Are there confirmed customer orders or only forecast demand?

**Why does this matter?**

Because the correct recommendation depends on the nature of the problem.

If the demand increase is temporary, overtime may be appropriate.

If demand is expected to remain elevated for the next 12 months, additional capacity investment might be more appropriate.

Do not recommend a permanent solution to a temporary problem without evaluating its consequences.

### Step 2: Identify Root Causes and Constraints

A problem describes what is happening.

A root cause explains why it is happening.

Let's use the 2,000-unit supply gap.

Possible causes:

| Potential cause | Operational explanation |
|---|---|
| Insufficient capacity | Machines cannot produce the required quantity |
| Material shortage | Required raw materials are unavailable |
| Supplier delay | Materials will arrive later than required |
| Labor shortage | Insufficient manpower for planned production |
| Production inefficiency | Actual output is lower than planned |
| Inventory allocation | Available stock is reserved for other orders |

You should distinguish between root causes and symptoms.

Example:

- Symptom: Production output is lower than demand.
- Possible root cause: Machine downtime is reducing available production hours.
- Underlying cause: Preventive maintenance is inadequate.

If you recommend overtime without addressing recurring machine downtime, the problem may continue.

**Useful analytical technique: 5 Whys**

The 5 Whys method involves repeatedly asking why a problem occurs until you identify a meaningful underlying cause.

Example:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why is production below plan? | Machine output is lower than expected |
| Why is output lower? | Frequent machine stoppages |
| Why are stoppages occurring? | Equipment overheating |
| Why is overheating occurring? | Cooling system performance is inadequate |
| Why is cooling inadequate? | Maintenance and inspection procedures are insufficient |

The fifth answer provides a potential root cause to investigate.

Important: Five questions are not a mandatory limit. Some problems require fewer or more investigations. Validate the identified cause with actual evidence.

### Step 3: Establish Objectives and Decision Criteria

Once you understand the problem, define what a successful solution should accomplish.

For example, management wants to resolve a 2,000-unit shortage.

Possible objectives:

1. Fulfill at least 95% of confirmed customer demand.
2. Minimize additional production costs.
3. Avoid excessive finished goods inventory.
4. Prevent disruption to existing customer commitments.
5. Maintain product quality.

These objectives become your decision criteria.

You cannot properly evaluate alternatives if you do not know what the business considers important.

Consider this:

| Decision criterion | Business priority |
|---|---|
| Customer service | Very high |
| Additional cost | High |
| Implementation time | High |
| Inventory impact | Medium |
| Long-term capacity | Medium |

This also explains why the cheapest solution is not necessarily the best solution.

A low-cost alternative that causes major customer delays may create a much larger financial loss.

### Step 4: Develop Possible Solutions

Now identify alternative courses of action.

For a supply shortage, possible solutions include:

- Increase production through overtime.
- Add an additional production shift.
- Expedite raw material deliveries.
- Purchase materials from an alternative supplier.
- Outsource some production.
- Transfer inventory from another facility.
- Prioritize high-value or critical customer orders.
- Reschedule lower-priority production.

At this stage, avoid selecting your preferred solution too early.

The objective is to identify realistic alternatives before evaluating them.

A useful practice is to develop at least three alternatives whenever the situation permits.

### Step 5: Evaluate Alternatives

This is where your analytical skills become particularly important.

Evaluate each alternative using several dimensions.

**Operational evaluation:**

- Can the production line support the proposed increase?
- Are materials available?
- Is manpower available?
- Can the alternative meet the required delivery date?
- Will product quality be affected?

**Financial evaluation:**

- What is the incremental cost?
- What is the expected revenue protected?
- What savings can be generated?
- Will inventory carrying costs increase?
- Is capital investment required?

**Customer evaluation:**

- Which customer commitments will be protected?
- Will any orders be delayed?
- Will the solution improve or reduce service levels?

**Risk evaluation:**

- What could go wrong?
- How likely is it?
- What happens if the alternative fails?

You will learn to quantify these considerations in Section 6.

### Step 6: Select and Justify the Recommended Option

After comparing alternatives, select the option that best supports the established business objectives.

Remember:

**Best alternative ≠ Cheapest alternative.**

The preferred alternative is the one that delivers the most appropriate overall business outcome within the company's constraints.

Your justification should explain:

1. Why the alternative is preferred.
2. What business problem it resolves.
3. What benefits it creates.
4. What additional costs it requires.
5. What risks management must accept.
6. Why the other alternatives are less favorable.

### Step 7: Define Implementation and Monitoring

A recommendation is incomplete if nobody knows who will execute it.

For example:

| Action | Owner | Deadline | Success measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Confirm material availability | Procurement | Within 2 days | Materials confirmed |
| Approve overtime schedule | Operations | Within 3 days | Additional capacity available |
| Prioritize customer orders | Supply Planning | Within 2 days | Priority orders protected |
| Monitor production output | Production | Daily | Planned output achieved |
| Review incremental costs | Finance | Weekly | Costs within approved budget |

This converts your recommendation into an executable business action plan.

## 4.2 Building Evidence-Based Recommendations

A recommendation must be supported by evidence.

Hindi sapat ang personal opinion, assumptions, or statements such as:

"I think we should increase production."

Instead, use relevant S&OP information.

| Information | What it tells you | How it supports recommendations |
|---|---|---|
| Demand forecast | Expected customer requirements | Determines required supply |
| Customer orders | Actual confirmed commitments | Helps establish priorities |
| Production capacity | Maximum achievable output | Determines production feasibility |
| Production schedule | Planned output and timing | Identifies scheduling opportunities |
| Raw material inventory | Available materials | Determines material constraints |
| Supplier lead time | Material replenishment timing | Determines when supply can become available |
| Finished goods inventory | Available stock | Identifies immediate fulfillment opportunities |
| Financial data | Costs, revenue, margins | Evaluates economic impact |

### Facts, Assumptions, Estimates, and Recommendations

This distinction is especially important when presenting to management.

| Classification | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | Verified information | Current production capacity is 10,000 units |
| Assumption | Condition accepted for analysis but not yet verified | Supplier can deliver the additional material next week |
| Estimate | Calculated or projected value | Overtime may increase capacity by 1,500 units |
| Recommendation | Proposed business action | Approve temporary overtime production |

Never present an assumption as an established fact.

For example:

Incorrect:

"Additional materials will arrive next week."

Correct:

"Additional materials are expected next week, based on the supplier's preliminary confirmation. Final delivery remains subject to supplier verification."

This protects the credibility of your analysis.

## 4.3 Quantifying Business Impact

A strong recommendation explains the expected impact in measurable terms.

For example:

Instead of saying:

"Overtime will improve customer fulfillment."

Say:

"Overtime is expected to increase weekly output by 1,500 units, reducing the projected supply gap from 2,000 units to 500 units and improving demand fulfillment from 83.3% to 95.8%, assuming the required materials are available."

Notice how much more useful the second statement is.

It tells management:

- What will change.
- How much it will change.
- What the expected outcome is.
- Which assumption must hold true.

### Relevant impact dimensions

| Dimension | Example measurement |
|---|---|
| Demand | Forecast demand, confirmed orders |
| Supply | Available supply, supply gap |
| Capacity | Units per week, utilization |
| Inventory | Ending inventory, days of supply |
| Customer service | Fill rate, on-time delivery |
| Financial | Incremental cost, savings, margin |
| Risk | Probability and impact of disruption |

# 5. Evaluating and Prioritizing Actions

Not every possible action deserves equal attention.

You may identify ten potential solutions, but the company may only have enough resources to implement three.

You therefore need to prioritize.

## 5.1 Business Impact vs. Implementation Effort

One useful method is the Impact-Effort Matrix.

|  | **LOW EFFORT** | **HIGH EFFORT** |
|---|---|---|
| **HIGH IMPACT** | **Quick Wins:** Prioritize and implement quickly | **Major Projects:** Plan and allocate resources |
| **LOW IMPACT** | **Fill-in Tasks:** Implement when resources are available | **Low Priority:** Usually defer or reconsider |

Conceptual classification. Actual priority should also consider urgency, feasibility, dependencies, and business risk.

Example:

| Proposed action | Impact | Effort | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reallocate existing inventory | High | Low | High |
| Approve overtime | High | Medium | High |
| Construct a new production facility | High | Very high | Long-term |
| Change report formatting | Low | Low | Low |
| Qualify a new supplier | High | High | Strategic |

## 5.2 Urgency and Operational Feasibility

An action can have high business impact but still be unsuitable if it cannot be implemented in time.

Example:

A customer needs 5,000 units within seven days.

You identify two alternatives:

- Alternative A: Build additional capacity requiring three months.
- Alternative B: Transfer 3,000 available units from another facility within four days.

Alternative A may improve long-term capacity, but it cannot solve the immediate customer shortage.

This is why recommendations should distinguish between immediate corrective actions and long-term improvements.

## 5.3 Short-Term vs. Long-Term Actions

| Short-term corrective action | Long-term improvement |
|---|---|
| Expedite raw materials | Develop alternative suppliers |
| Introduce temporary overtime | Improve production capacity |
| Transfer available inventory | Optimize network inventory |
| Prioritize customer orders | Improve demand planning accuracy |
| Use subcontracting | Develop strategic manufacturing partnerships |

An effective recommendation may combine both.

For example:

- Immediate: Expedite materials and introduce temporary overtime.
- Medium-term: Improve supplier delivery reliability.
- Long-term: Increase capacity or qualify an alternative source.

## 5.4 Assigning Ownership and Accountability

Every recommended action should have an owner.

In business management, this is often supported by a RACI framework.

| Role | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Responsible | Person or team performing the task |
| Accountable | Person ultimately answerable for the outcome |
| Consulted | People providing expertise or input |
| Informed | People who need updates |

Example:

For a material shortage:

- Procurement: Responsible for supplier coordination.
- Supply Chain Manager: Accountable for resolving the material availability issue.
- Production: Consulted regarding production requirements.
- Sales: Informed about potential customer delivery changes.

This prevents the common situation where everyone agrees that an action is necessary, but nobody actually executes it.

# 6. Formulas and Analytical Tools

Now let's move from conceptual understanding to technical application.

We will use manufacturing and supply chain examples.

All values below are illustrative assumptions, not universal industry standards.

## 6.1 Expected Financial Benefit

Expected Financial Benefit measures the financial value generated by a proposed action.

A simplified formula is:

[
Net\ Benefit = Financial\ Benefits - Incremental\ Costs
]

Example:

A company considers overtime production.

Expected benefits:
- Additional revenue protected: ₱500,000
- Avoided customer penalties: ₱50,000

Additional costs:
- Overtime labor: ₱80,000
- Additional utilities: ₱15,000
- Additional logistics: ₱10,000

Calculation:

[
Total\ Benefits=500,000+50,000
]

[
=₱550,000
]

[
Total\ Costs=80,000+15,000+10,000
]

[
=₱105,000
]

[
Net\ Benefit=550,000-105,000
]

**₱445,000 Net Benefit**

Interpretation:

The proposed action generates an estimated net financial benefit of ₱445,000.

Important limitation: Additional revenue should not automatically be treated as profit. Ideally, use incremental contribution margin rather than gross sales revenue, and avoid double-counting avoided penalties or savings.

## 6.2 Incremental Cost and Savings

**Incremental Cost** is the additional expense incurred when implementing a proposed action compared with the baseline plan.

[
Incremental\ Cost=New\ Cost-Baseline\ Cost
]

Example:

| Expense | Normal production | Overtime production |
|---|---:|---:|
| Labor | ₱100,000 | ₱140,000 |
| Utilities | ₱30,000 | ₱38,000 |
| Logistics | ₱10,000 | ₱15,000 |
| Total | ₱140,000 | ₱193,000 |

[
Incremental\ Cost=193,000-140,000
]

[
=₱53,000
]

The overtime strategy costs an additional ₱53,000.

Now assume outsourcing the same production volume costs ₱90,000 more than the baseline.

| Alternative | Incremental cost |
|---|---:|
| Overtime | ₱53,000 |
| Outsourcing | ₱90,000 |

Overtime is cheaper by ₱37,000, assuming both alternatives provide equivalent usable output and delivery performance.

## 6.3 Supply Gap Reduction

This measures how much a proposed action reduces the difference between demand and available supply.

First:

[
Supply\ Gap=Demand-Available\ Supply
]

Assume:

- Demand = 12,000 units
- Initial supply = 10,000 units
- Additional supply from overtime = 1,500 units

Initial supply gap:

[
12,000-10,000=2,000
]

New supply:

[
10,000+1,500=11,500
]

Remaining supply gap:

[
12,000-11,500=500
]

Supply gap reduction:

[
2,000-500=1,500\ units
]

Percentage reduction:

[
\frac{Initial\ Gap-Remaining\ Gap}{Initial\ Gap}\times100
]

[
=\frac{2,000-500}{2,000}\times100
]

**75% Supply Gap Reduction**

Interpretation:

The overtime proposal resolves 75% of the original supply shortage.

## 6.4 Demand Fulfillment Improvement

Demand fulfillment measures the proportion of demand that can be supplied.

For a simplified aggregate planning calculation:

[
Fulfillment\ Rate=
\frac{Available\ Supply}{Demand}\times100
]

Initial fulfillment:

[
\frac{10,000}{12,000}\times100=83.33\%
]

After overtime:

[
\frac{11,500}{12,000}\times100=95.83\%
]

Improvement:

[
95.83\%-83.33\%=12.50\ percentage\ points
]

**12.50 percentage-point improvement**

Important distinction:

This is a simplified demand coverage calculation. Actual customer order fill rate or order fulfillment performance may use quantities shipped, orders fulfilled, or order lines completed, depending on the company's KPI definition.

## 6.5 Inventory Impact

Recommendations can affect both current and future inventory.

The basic inventory balance is:

[
Ending\ Inventory =
Beginning\ Inventory + Receipts - Demand
]

Example:

- Beginning inventory = 3,000 units
- Planned receipts = 8,000 units
- Demand = 9,000 units

[
Ending\ Inventory=3,000+8,000-9,000
]

[
=2,000\ units
]

Now assume management approves an additional 1,500-unit production quantity.

[
New\ Ending\ Inventory=3,000+9,500-9,000
]

[
=3,500\ units
]

The recommendation increases projected ending inventory by 1,500 units.

However, this assumes the additional production is not immediately consumed by unmet demand. If all 1,500 units are allocated to existing backorders, ending inventory may remain unchanged.

**Business interpretation:**

Additional production is not automatically beneficial. You must determine whether it creates customer fulfillment, inventory replenishment, or excess stock.

## 6.6 Return on Investment (ROI)

ROI measures the financial return relative to the investment or cost required.

A common simplified formula is:

[
ROI=\frac{Net\ Benefit}{Investment\ Cost}\times100
]

Example:

A company spends ₱200,000 on a temporary capacity improvement.

Expected incremental financial benefit = ₱300,000.

[
Net\ Benefit=300,000-200,000
]

[
=₱100,000
]

[
ROI=\frac{100,000}{200,000}\times100
]

**50% ROI**

Interpretation:

For every ₱100 invested, the company generates ₱50 in net benefit under the stated assumptions.

Be careful with terminology. Some companies calculate ROI using net operating benefit, some use annualized returns, and others include a broader set of investment costs. Always clarify the organization's definition.

Also, ROI does not consider the timing of cash flows. For larger or long-term projects, NPV and payback period may provide additional insight.

## 6.7 Weighted Decision Matrix

This is one of the most useful tools when comparing multiple alternatives with different business advantages.

Imagine three alternatives:

- A: Overtime production
- B: Outsourcing
- C: Additional production equipment

Management evaluates them using four criteria:

1. Customer service impact
2. Financial cost
3. Implementation speed
4. Operational feasibility

Each criterion receives a weight.

**Illustrative weighting**

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Customer service | 35% |
| Financial cost | 30% |
| Implementation speed | 20% |
| Operational feasibility | 15% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

Each alternative receives a score from 1 to 5.

- 1 = Very poor
- 2 = Poor
- 3 = Average
- 4 = Good
- 5 = Excellent

The formula:

[
Weighted\ Score=\sum(Weight\times Rating)
]

Let's compare.

| Criteria | Weight | Overtime | Outsourcing | Equipment |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Customer service | 0.35 | 4 | 5 | 3 |
| Financial cost | 0.30 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| Implementation speed | 0.20 | 5 | 4 | 1 |
| Feasibility | 0.15 | 4 | 4 | 3 |

**Overtime calculation:**

[
(0.35\times4)+(0.30\times4)
]

[
+(0.20\times5)+(0.15\times4)
]

[
=1.40+1.20+1.00+0.60
]

[
=4.20
]

**Outsourcing calculation:**

[
(0.35\times5)+(0.30\times3)
]

[
+(0.20\times4)+(0.15\times4)
]

[
=4.00
]

**Additional equipment calculation:**

[
(0.35\times3)+(0.30\times2)
]

[
+(0.20\times1)+(0.15\times3)
]

[
=2.30
]

**Weighted Decision Matrix Results**

| Alternative | Weighted score |
|---|---:|
| Overtime | 4.20 / 5 |
| Outsourcing | 4.00 / 5 |
| Additional equipment | 2.30 / 5 |

**Highest score: Overtime**

**Interpretation:**

Overtime ranks first because it provides a strong balance of customer service, cost, speed, and feasibility.

However, a weighted score is a decision-support tool, not an automatic decision-maker.

For example, overtime may score well overall but still be impossible if there is no available workforce or sufficient raw material.

A critical constraint can override the numerical ranking.

### 6.8 When Quantitative Analysis Is Insufficient

Hindi lahat ng business considerations ay madaling lagyan ng formula.

Consider the following:

| Qualitative consideration | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Customer relationship | Losing a strategic customer may have long-term consequences |
| Product quality | Rushed production may increase defect risks |
| Supplier reliability | A cheaper supplier may have inconsistent delivery |
| Regulatory compliance | Some actions are not permissible regardless of financial benefit |
| Employee fatigue | Excessive overtime can affect safety and productivity |
| Strategic importance | A product may be important for future market expansion |

For example, outsourcing might be the cheapest alternative.

But if the external manufacturer cannot meet quality requirements, the financial calculation alone is insufficient.

**Professional principle: Quantitative analysis supports business judgment. It does not replace business judgment.**

# 7. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's combine everything into a realistic manufacturing S&OP case.

This is the main demonstration of Topic 6.7.

## 7.1 Business Scenario: Unexpected Demand Increase

A manufacturing company produces an industrial electronic component.

A major customer increases its forecast for the coming month because of stronger market demand.

The company must determine how to fulfill the additional demand without excessively increasing costs or disrupting other customers.

**Business Situation: Next Month**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Demand | 12,000 units |
| Available supply | 9,000 units |
| Supply gap | 3,000 units |
| Current capacity utilization | 95% |

**Additional constraints**

- Raw material inventory can support only 9,500 units of production.
- Normal supplier replenishment lead time is four weeks.
- Customer requires 12,000 units within the month.
- Overtime and outsourcing are possible.
- Other customer commitments must also be protected.

### Step 1: Analyze the Situation

First, calculate the supply gap.

[
12,000-9,000=3,000\ units
]

Demand fulfillment:

[
\frac{9,000}{12,000}\times100=75\%
]

Therefore:

- The company can currently supply 75% of demand.
- The remaining 25% is not covered by available supply.
- The company needs an additional 3,000 units to meet the full requirement.

But there is another constraint.

Raw materials can support only 9,500 units of production.

Even if the company has enough machine capacity, it cannot produce the required quantity without additional materials.

This is an important S&OP lesson:

**Production capacity does not automatically translate into available supply. Material availability must also support the production plan.**

### Step 2: Identify Root Causes and Constraints

Let's separate the problem into categories.

| Category | Finding | Business implication |
|---|---|---|
| Demand | Demand increased to 12,000 units | Higher supply requirement |
| Capacity | Normal utilization is 95% | Limited available capacity |
| Materials | Materials support 9,500 units | Production is material-constrained |
| Procurement | Four-week lead time | Replenishment may arrive too late |
| Customer | 12,000 units required this month | Delivery risk |

Root cause assessment:

1. Unexpected demand increase created additional requirements.
2. Normal production capacity has limited flexibility.
3. Material inventory is insufficient for the entire demand.
4. Supplier lead time restricts immediate replenishment.

There is no single solution that automatically addresses all four issues.

### Step 3: Establish Business Objectives

For this example, management establishes the following objectives:

1. Achieve the highest feasible customer fulfillment.
2. Protect existing confirmed customer commitments.
3. Minimize incremental production and procurement costs.
4. Avoid excessive finished goods inventory.
5. Maintain product quality and delivery reliability.

These objectives will guide the comparison.

### Step 4: Develop Three Alternatives

**Alternative A: Temporary Overtime Production**

- Increase production through overtime.
- Requires additional raw materials.
- Lower additional cost than outsourcing.
- Subject to manpower and equipment availability.

**Alternative B: Outsource Part of Production**

- Transfer part of the requirement to an approved external manufacturer.
- Requires supplier capacity and material confirmation.
- Higher unit cost.
- May improve short-term supply availability.

**Alternative C: Customer Prioritization and Rescheduling**

- Allocate available production to the most critical customer orders.
- Defer lower-priority orders.
- Avoids major incremental production expenditure.
- Does not eliminate the total supply shortage.

### Step 5: Evaluate Operational and Financial Impacts

For demonstration, assume the following data:

| Metric | Alternative A | Alternative B | Alternative C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Additional usable supply | 1,500 | 2,500 | 0 |
| Incremental cost | ₱75,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱10,000 |
| Estimated implementation time | 5 days | 10 days | 2 days |
| Customer fulfillment potential | Higher | Highest | Priority orders only |

Important assumptions:

- Additional supply is incremental to the original 9,000 units.
- Alternative A requires additional materials, which must be secured.
- Alternative B requires an approved supplier with verified quality and capacity.
- Alternative C changes allocation, not total physical supply.
- Costs are illustrative and exclude any unlisted penalties or financial effects.

Let's calculate.

**Alternative A: Overtime**

New available supply:

[
9,000+1,500=10,500
]

Remaining supply gap:

[
12,000-10,500=1,500
]

Demand fulfillment:

[
\frac{10,500}{12,000}\times100=87.5\%
]

**Alternative B: Outsourcing**

New available supply:

[
9,000+2,500=11,500
]

Remaining supply gap:

[
12,000-11,500=500
]

Demand fulfillment:

[
\frac{11,500}{12,000}\times100=95.83\%
]

**Alternative C: Customer Prioritization**

Total available supply remains:

[
9,000\ units
]

Demand fulfillment:

[
\frac{9,000}{12,000}\times100=75\%
]

However, allocation can improve the fulfillment of critical customer orders.

This is an important distinction: prioritization improves who receives the available supply, but it does not create additional supply.

### Step 6: Compare the Alternatives

| Evaluation | A: Overtime | B: Outsourcing | C: Prioritization |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supply gap remaining | 1,500 | 500 | 3,000 |
| Incremental cost | ₱75,000 | ₱200,000 | ₱10,000 |
| Implementation speed | Fast | Moderate | Fast |
| Total fulfillment | 87.5% | 95.83% | 75% |
| Main concern | Material availability | Higher cost and quality validation | Unfulfilled demand |

Which alternative should management choose?

It depends on the business objectives and the available resources.

Outsourcing provides the highest fulfillment among the three alternatives, but it is also the most expensive.

Overtime is cheaper, but it leaves a larger supply gap.

Prioritization is inexpensive, but it does not resolve the total shortage.

### Step 7: Develop the Recommendation

A possible recommendation is a combination of alternatives rather than selecting only one.

**Recommended approach: Combine overtime production with selective outsourcing and customer prioritization.**

Proposed actions:

1. Prioritize confirmed and strategically important customer orders.
2. Implement overtime to generate an additional 1,500 units.
3. Outsource the remaining 1,500 units, subject to approved supplier capacity and material availability.
4. Expedite the required raw materials.
5. Monitor actual production output and supplier delivery performance daily.

Expected supply:

[
9,000+1,500+1,500=12,000
]

Expected demand fulfillment:

[
\frac{12,000}{12,000}\times100=100\%
]

Illustrative incremental cost:

[
75,000+\frac{200,000}{2}+10,000
]

[
=₱185,000
]

The outsourcing cost assumes that the ₱200,000 quoted cost is proportional to the 2,500-unit alternative, and the company outsources 1,500 units instead. This proportional cost assumption must be verified because fixed setup costs may make the actual cost different.

**Target: 100% Demand Coverage**

**Important qualification:** This recommendation is conditional. The company must confirm that the additional materials can be secured, overtime can produce the required quantity, and the external supplier can meet quality and delivery requirements. Otherwise, the 100% target is not operationally achievable.

### Step 8: Implementation Plan

| Action | Owner | Timing | KPI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Confirm raw material availability | Procurement | Day 1–2 | Material coverage |
| Confirm outsourcing capacity | Procurement / Supplier Quality | Day 1–3 | Approved supply quantity |
| Approve overtime | Operations | Day 2 | Additional capacity |
| Finalize customer allocation | Supply Planning / Sales | Day 2 | Priority order fulfillment |
| Monitor production | Production | Daily | Actual vs. planned output |
| Track incremental costs | Finance | Weekly | Actual vs. approved cost |
| Review fulfillment | S&OP Team | Weekly | Demand fulfillment rate |

### Step 9: Identify Risks and Contingencies

| Risk | Potential impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Raw materials arrive late | Overtime production cannot proceed | Expedite supply and identify alternate approved sources |
| Supplier misses delivery | Outsourced supply becomes unavailable | Establish backup supply arrangement |
| Quality issues | Additional units cannot be released | Require qualification and inspection |
| Overtime productivity is lower than expected | Production target missed | Monitor daily output and adjust allocation |
| Customer demand changes again | Supply plan becomes outdated | Refresh forecast and reassess weekly |

This completes the recommendation development process.

Notice what we did:

We did not simply calculate the shortage.

We connected demand, supply, materials, capacity, cost, customer commitments, and operational risk to determine a practical course of action.

# 8. Communicating Recommendations to Management

A technically correct recommendation can still fail if it is poorly communicated.

Imagine presenting 20 Excel tables, 15 charts, and several pages of calculations to management.

The analysis may be correct, but management may struggle to identify the actual decision being requested.

Your job is to simplify the decision without oversimplifying the analysis.

## 8.1 Professional Business Recommendation Structure

Use the following structure when preparing an S&OP recommendation.

| Section | What to present |
|---|---|
| 1. Executive Summary | What decision is required? |
| 2. Business Situation | What is happening? |
| 3. Key Findings | What does the analysis reveal? |
| 4. Root Causes | Why is the problem occurring? |
| 5. Alternatives | What options are available? |
| 6. Comparative Analysis | How do the options differ? |
| 7. Recommendation | What should management approve? |
| 8. Business Impact | What are the expected results? |
| 9. Risks and Mitigation | What could go wrong? |
| 10. Action Plan | Who will do what and when? |

## 8.2 The Executive Summary

The executive summary should communicate the most important information immediately.

For our manufacturing scenario, an example would be:

**EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

**Issue:** Forecast demand increased to 12,000 units, while available supply is limited to 9,000 units, creating a 3,000-unit supply gap.

**Key constraints:** Production capacity is operating at 95% utilization, while available raw materials support only 9,500 units of production.

**Recommendation:** Implement temporary overtime production, selectively outsource the remaining requirement, and prioritize customer commitments.

**Expected outcome:** Target 100% demand coverage, subject to material availability and supplier confirmation.

**Estimated incremental cost:** ₱185,000 under the illustrative assumptions.

**Decision required:** Management approval of overtime, outsourcing expenditure, and expedited material procurement.

This is more effective than beginning your presentation with multiple detailed tables.

Management immediately understands the problem, proposed action, expected outcome, and required approval.

## 8.3 Communicating Risks and Trade-offs

Never present a recommendation as if it has no disadvantages.

For example:

"Our recommendation is to implement overtime and outsourcing to meet the demand requirement."

This is incomplete.

A better explanation:

"Although the combined approach can potentially achieve full demand coverage, it increases incremental operating costs and depends on supplier delivery confirmation. We recommend proceeding with overtime immediately while securing outsourced capacity as a contingency. If the supplier cannot meet the required delivery date, customer allocation will be adjusted based on approved business priorities."

This demonstrates that you understand both the opportunity and the risk.

## 8.4 Pre-S&OP vs. Executive S&OP Recommendations

This is particularly important because not every recommendation is presented to the same audience.

### Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where cross-functional teams review the integrated demand and supply plan, resolve issues, evaluate alternatives, and prepare recommendations for leadership.

Your presentation should be analytical and detailed enough for the relevant functions to validate the proposal.

Typical participants:

- Demand Planning
- Supply Planning
- Operations
- Procurement
- Finance
- Sales
- Other relevant functions

As an S&OP analyst, you may be expected to:

1. Present the projected supply gap.
2. Explain the underlying constraints.
3. Compare possible balancing strategies.
4. Quantify financial and operational impacts.
5. Identify unresolved issues.
6. Develop a proposed action plan.

**Pre-S&OP question:**

"Have we analyzed the problem thoroughly, and are the proposed solutions operationally and financially feasible?"

### Executive S&OP

Executive S&OP focuses on management decisions and business-level alignment.

The discussion generally emphasizes:

- Major demand-supply imbalances.
- Strategic customer commitments.
- Financial implications.
- Significant operational constraints.
- Resource requirements.
- Business risks.
- Decisions requiring executive approval.

Your presentation should be concise and decision-oriented.

**Executive S&OP question:**

"What decision does management need to make, and what are the consequences of that decision?"

| Dimension | Pre-S&OP | Executive S&OP |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Develop and validate recommendations | Decide and approve |
| Detail | More analytical | More summarized |
| Focus | Operational and cross-functional feasibility | Strategic and financial implications |
| Participants | Functional managers and planning teams | Senior management |
| Expected output | Agreed recommendations and unresolved issues | Decisions, approvals, and direction |

### 8.5 How to Present Like an S&OP Analyst

Practice this five-part communication sequence:

1. **Situation:** "We are projecting a 3,000-unit supply gap next month."
2. **Cause:** "The gap is driven by increased demand, limited capacity, and material availability."
3. **Alternatives:** "We evaluated overtime, outsourcing, and customer prioritization."
4. **Recommendation:** "We recommend combining overtime and selective outsourcing."
5. **Decision:** "We request approval of the proposed ₱185,000 incremental expenditure, subject to supplier and material confirmation."

This is a useful communication framework for management meetings.
