# Module 6 – Supply Planning and Demand-Supply Balancing
## Topic 6.2: Identifying Supply Gaps

**Main learning objective:** Matutunan mong matukoy kung kailan hindi sapat ang available and planned supply para matugunan ang expected customer demand, gaano kalaki ang shortage, bakit ito nangyayari, at ano ang magiging epekto nito sa business.

Sa topic na ito, lilipat tayo mula sa simpleng comparison ng demand at supply papunta sa actual investigation ng supply problems.

# 1. Topic Overview

### 1.1 What are we going to discuss?

Sa previous topic, **Topic 6.1: Demand-Supply Matching**, natutunan natin kung paano ikinukumpara ang demand requirements against available and planned supply.

Ngayon, mas specific ang focus natin.

Imagine na ang company ay may customer demand na 10,000 units next month, pero ang available and planned supply ay 8,000 units lamang.

**Illustrative example: Demand vs. Supply**

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 10,000 units |
| Available supply | 8,000 units |
| **Supply gap** | **2,000 units** |

Ang 2,000 units na difference ang tinatawag nating **Supply Gap**.

Pero hindi sapat na malaman lang na may shortage.

Kailangan nating sagutin ang mga sumusunod:

1. Bakit 2,000 units ang kulang?
2. Production capacity ba ang problema?
3. May raw material shortage ba?
4. Delayed ba ang supplier?
5. May production downtime ba?
6. May available inventory ba sa ibang warehouse?
7. Temporary lang ba ang shortage, or magiging recurring problem?

Ito ang purpose ng Supply Gap Analysis.

### 1.2 Why is this important?

Mahalaga ito dahil hindi lahat ng customer demand ay automatically kayang i-fulfill ng company.

Kung hindi ma-identify nang maaga ang supply gap, possible consequences include:

- Missed customer delivery commitments.
- Backorders and delayed shipments.
- Lost sales and revenue.
- Emergency procurement costs.
- Expedited transportation costs.
- Production schedule disruptions.
- Reduced customer satisfaction.
- Increased inventory imbalance.

**The earlier a supply gap is identified, the more options the company has to resolve it.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

### 2.1 Understanding supply gaps through a manufacturing example

Imagine na may manufacturing company na gumagawa ng three products:

- Product A
- Product B
- Product C

May customer orders sila for December.

However, may limitations ang factory:

- Limited production machines.
- Limited raw materials.
- Delayed component deliveries.
- Existing finished goods inventory.

Kahit may customer demand, hindi ibig sabihin na kaya itong i-produce at i-deliver ng company.

Parang isang restaurant:

- 100 customers ang gustong kumain.
- Kaya lang mag-serve ng kitchen ay 80 meals.
- May ingredients lamang para sa 70 meals.

May dalawang magkaibang limitations:

1. Kitchen capacity: 80 meals.
2. Ingredient availability: 70 meals.

Kahit may capacity ang kitchen para sa 80 meals, 70 meals lang ang possible dahil kulang ang ingredients.

This illustrates an important concept:

**Available production capacity is not necessarily equal to available supply.**

Kailangan available ang lahat ng necessary resources bago maging actual deliverable supply ang planned production.

### 2.2 Supply gap versus supply problem

Magkaiba ang dalawang bagay:

- **Supply gap:** The measurable difference between required demand and available supply.
- **Supply problem:** The underlying condition that causes the gap.

Example:

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 10,000 |
| Available supply | 8,000 |
| Supply gap | 2,000 |
| Root cause | Delayed raw material delivery |

Ang 2,000 units ang supply gap.

Ang delayed raw material delivery naman ang possible root cause.

Hindi dapat paghaluin ang dalawang ito. One tells us how much is missing; the other explains why it is missing.

# 3. Technical Explanation

### 3.1 Formal definition

A **Supply Gap** is the difference between the quantity of demand that must be fulfilled within a specified time period and the quantity of supply that can realistically be made available within that same period.

Important words:

- **Demand requirement:** Quantity of products required by customers.
- **Available supply:** Quantity of products that can be supplied within the required period.
- **Time period:** The specific period when demand must be fulfilled, such as a week or month.
- **Supply constraint:** A limitation that prevents the company from making sufficient supply available.

Notice the phrase *within the same period*.

This is extremely important.

For example:

| Month | Demand | Supply |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 8,000 |
| February | 10,000 | 12,000 |

January has a shortage of 2,000 units.

February has a surplus of 2,000 units.

Hindi automatically ibig sabihin na resolved na ang January shortage dahil may surplus sa February.

Why?

Because customer demand has timing requirements. If the customer needs the product in January, February supply may already be too late.

This leads us to an important distinction: quantity availability versus timing availability.

### 3.2 Four related supply concepts

Hindi pare-pareho ang supply shortage, capacity shortage, material shortage, at delivery shortfall.

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Supply shortage | Overall supply is insufficient to meet demand | Demand = 10,000; supply = 8,000 |
| Capacity shortage | Production resources cannot produce the required quantity | Required output = 10,000; capacity = 8,000 |
| Material shortage | Insufficient raw materials or components are available | Production needs 10,000 kg; only 8,000 kg available |
| Delivery shortfall | Actual delivered quantity is below the committed quantity | Customer ordered 1,000; delivered 800 |

Let's examine their relationships.

**Capacity shortage**

The company has insufficient machine hours, labor, production lines, or other resources.

**Material shortage**

The company has insufficient inputs to manufacture the products.

**Delivery shortfall**

The company fails to deliver the required quantity on time, regardless of whether the original reason was capacity, material, transportation, or another issue.

**Supply shortage**

This is the broader condition where the total supply available cannot meet the required demand.

A company can experience several of these simultaneously.

### 3.3 Gross supply gap versus net supply gap

This distinction is especially important in inventory and supply planning.

**Gross supply gap**

The difference between demand and planned production or incoming supply, before considering existing inventory.

**Net supply gap**

The remaining shortage after considering usable existing inventory and other eligible supply sources.

Example:

| Component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 10,000 |
| Planned production | 8,000 |
| Gross supply gap | 2,000 |
| Existing usable inventory | 1,500 |
| Net supply gap | 500 |

The company has a gross gap of 2,000 units, but only a net shortage of 500 units after using inventory.

**Visualizing gross versus net shortage**

| Measure | Quantity | Share of gross gap |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross gap | 2,000 units | 100% |
| Inventory coverage | 1,500 units | 75% |
| Remaining net gap | 500 units | 25% |

**Important:** Existing inventory must be usable and available in the correct location and time period. Blocked, defective, reserved, or inaccessible stock should not automatically be counted as available supply.

### 3.4 Relationship between demand-supply matching and supply gap analysis

Magkaugnay sila, pero magkaiba ang purpose.

| Activity | Main question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Demand-Supply Matching | Does supply meet demand? | Demand versus supply comparison |
| Supply Gap Identification | Where and when is supply insufficient? | Identified supply gaps |
| Supply Gap Analysis | How large is the shortage and why does it exist? | Quantified gaps and root causes |
| Supply-Demand Balancing | What actions can close the gap? | Recommended corrective actions |

Example:

1. Demand-Supply Matching identifies a shortage of 2,000 units.
2. Supply Gap Identification determines that the shortage affects Product A in January.
3. Supply Gap Analysis determines that a supplier delay caused the shortage.
4. Supply-Demand Balancing evaluates alternative suppliers, production rescheduling, and inventory transfers.

This is the logical progression of Module 6.

# 4. Detailed Discussion

## 4.1 Types and causes of supply gaps

Let's examine the common causes and how they affect manufacturing operations.

### A. Insufficient production capacity

Occurs when the company does not have enough production resources to manufacture the required quantity.

Possible limitations:

- Insufficient machine availability.
- Limited production hours.
- Labor shortages.
- Equipment bottlenecks.
- Excessive changeover time.
- Maintenance downtime.
- Production scheduling conflicts.

**Example:**

A factory needs to manufacture 12,000 units per month.

However, its maximum available production capacity is only 10,000 units.

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Required production | 12,000 units |
| Available capacity | 10,000 units |
| **Capacity shortage** | **2,000 units** |

Possible solutions include overtime, additional shifts, subcontracting, or improving production efficiency.

These are possible balancing strategies, which we will study in Topic 6.3.

### B. Raw material and component shortages

Occurs when the company does not have enough materials to support the planned production schedule.

Example:

A product requires:

- 2 kg of Raw Material X per finished unit.
- Planned production: 5,000 units.
- Available Raw Material X: 8,000 kg.

Material requirement:

\[
5,000 \times 2 = 10,000\text{ kg}
\]

Material shortage:

\[
10,000-8,000=2,000\text{ kg}
\]

Even if the production line has enough capacity, production cannot reach its target without the missing material.

### C. Supplier delays and procurement constraints

A company may have already purchased materials, but the supplier cannot deliver them according to the required schedule.

Possible causes:

- Supplier production problems.
- Supplier capacity constraints.
- Transportation delays.
- Customs clearance.
- Quality rejection.
- Procurement processing delays.
- Long supplier lead times.

**Important distinction:**

A purchase order is not the same as available inventory.

For example, a company orders 5,000 components with a delivery date of January 10.

If the supplier confirms that delivery will happen on February 5, those 5,000 components cannot support January production.

They are expected receipts, but they are not January available supply.

### D. Production downtime and low yield

Production capacity on paper may not equal actual usable output.

Consider:

- Planned production: 10,000 units.
- Equipment downtime reduces production.
- Defective units are rejected during quality inspection.

Suppose actual production is 9,000 units, but only 8,500 units pass quality inspection.

The company has produced 9,000 units, but only 8,500 units are good finished goods.

For supply planning, the 8,500 good units are generally more relevant.

This connects directly to manufacturing yield.

### E. Inventory shortages and inaccurate inventory records

Sometimes the company appears to have sufficient inventory in its system, but actual physical inventory is insufficient.

Example:

| Inventory information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| ERP inventory | 5,000 |
| Actual physical inventory | 4,200 |
| Inventory discrepancy | 800 |

Possible reasons:

- Unrecorded material consumption.
- Delayed system transactions.
- Incorrect inventory counting.
- Scrap not properly recorded.
- Quality-hold inventory included in available stock.
- Inventory located in another warehouse.

This is an important planning issue because inaccurate inventory information can create false confidence in supply availability.

### F. Lead time and transportation issues

Even when materials exist, they may not arrive before the production requirement date.

Example:

- Material required: January 15.
- Supplier shipment: January 10.
- Transportation delay: 7 days.
- Actual arrival: January 17.

The company may experience a two-day production material gap.

The issue is not necessarily insufficient total material quantity. It is a timing problem.

### G. Unexpected increases in customer demand

Supply gaps can also occur because demand increases unexpectedly.

Example:

| Item | Original forecast | Updated demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| Product A | 8,000 | 10,000 |
| Product B | 5,000 | 5,500 |
| Product C | 4,000 | 4,000 |

If supply plans were based on the original forecast, the updated demand creates additional requirements.

The company may have sufficient supply for its original forecast but insufficient supply for the latest demand.

This is why demand changes must be communicated to supply planning.

## 4.2 Supply Gap Identification

Now that we understand the possible causes, let's discuss how an S&OP analyst actually identifies supply gaps.

The process generally follows these steps:

**Supply Gap Identification Workflow**

1. **Collect demand requirements.** Gather customer orders, forecasts, and relevant demand commitments.
2. **Determine available inventory.** Identify usable finished goods inventory and other eligible supply.
3. **Review incoming supply.** Check confirmed purchase orders, scheduled receipts, and planned production.
4. **Check operational constraints.** Validate production capacity, materials, suppliers, and lead times.
5. **Compare demand and supply.** Identify shortages and surpluses by product and time period.
6. **Quantify and classify gaps.** Determine the size, timing, location, and possible cause of each gap.

### A. Identify gaps by product

A company might have enough supply overall, but certain products may still have shortages.

Example:

| Product | Demand | Supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10,000 | 8,000 | -2,000 |
| B | 5,000 | 6,000 | +1,000 |
| C | 4,000 | 4,000 | 0 |
| **Total** | **19,000** | **18,000** | **-1,000** |

The overall shortage is 1,000 units.

However, Product A actually has a shortage of 2,000 units.

Product B has a surplus of 1,000 units.

The surplus of Product B cannot automatically solve the shortage of Product A because they are different products.

This is an important S&OP principle:

**Aggregate supply can hide product-level shortages.**

### B. Identify gaps by location

Suppose the company has two distribution centers.

| Location | Demand | Available supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Manila DC | 5,000 | 3,000 | -2,000 |
| Cebu DC | 3,000 | 4,000 | +1,000 |
| **Total** | **8,000** | **7,000** | **-1,000** |

Although the overall shortage is 1,000 units, Manila has a 2,000-unit shortage.

The Cebu surplus may help if inventory can be transferred in time.

But transportation time, cost, and stock ownership must be considered.

### C. Identify gaps by time period

A company can have sufficient supply across an entire quarter but still experience shortages in individual months.

| Month | Demand | Supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 8,000 | -2,000 |
| February | 10,000 | 12,000 | +2,000 |
| March | 10,000 | 10,000 | 0 |
| **Total** | **30,000** | **30,000** | **0** |

At first glance, total quarterly supply equals total quarterly demand.

However, January still has a shortage of 2,000 units.

February's surplus cannot automatically fulfill January's missed customer commitments.

This is called a **time-phased supply gap**.

### D. Temporary timing gap versus structural shortage

This is another important distinction.

**Temporary timing gap**

A shortage caused by a timing mismatch between demand and supply.

Example:

- Demand is required in January.
- Material arrives in February.
- Supply is available, but too late.

**Structural shortage**

A recurring or persistent shortage caused by an underlying limitation in supply capability.

Example:

- Monthly demand: 15,000 units.
- Sustainable production capacity: 12,000 units.
- Monthly shortage: 3,000 units.

The company continuously lacks sufficient production capacity.

| Characteristic | Temporary timing gap | Structural shortage |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Usually short-term | Persistent or recurring |
| Main issue | Timing mismatch | Insufficient capability |
| Example | Delayed supplier delivery | Insufficient monthly capacity |
| Business response | Reschedule or expedite | Capacity or sourcing changes |

Why is this important?

Because the corrective action must match the type of problem.

A temporary supplier delay may be resolved through expedited delivery.

A structural capacity shortage may require additional equipment, another production line, or a long-term sourcing arrangement.

### E. Apparent shortage versus actual shortage

Sometimes a supply gap appears in the planning report even though the company has enough supply.

This is called an apparent shortage.

Possible reasons:

1. Inventory is recorded in the wrong location.
2. Production orders are not reflected in the planning system.
3. Purchase orders are missing from the supply plan.
4. Incorrect product codes are used.
5. Inventory is incorrectly classified as unavailable.
6. Supply is allocated to the wrong customer.
7. Demand is duplicated.

Example:

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Demand | 10,000 |
| Reported supply | 8,000 |
| Reported shortage | 2,000 |
| Unrecorded confirmed receipt | 2,000 |
| Actual supply | 10,000 |

The apparent shortage disappears once the confirmed receipt is correctly reflected.

**Lesson:** Before escalating a shortage, validate the accuracy and completeness of the underlying data.

# 5. Formulas and Calculations

Ito ang pinaka-importanteng technical portion ng topic.

Bago tayo mag-compute, kailangan muna nating maintindihan kung ano ang dapat isama sa supply calculation.

## 5.1 Understanding the supply components

For a particular product and period, supply planning may consider:

| Supply component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Beginning inventory | Usable inventory available at the start of the period |
| Scheduled receipts | Confirmed incoming products or materials expected during the period |
| Planned production | Expected good finished goods output |
| Transfers in | Eligible inventory transferred from another location |
| Transfers out | Inventory transferred to another location |
| Allocated or reserved stock | Inventory committed to specific requirements |

Important: Hindi lahat ng supply component ay automatically additive.

For example, kung ang scheduled receipt ay already included sa planned production output, hindi mo dapat bilangin nang dalawang beses.

Similarly, inventory reserved for another customer should not be treated as freely available.

For our initial calculations, we will use a simplified time-phased model.

## 5.2 Formula 1: Supply-Demand Gap

The basic formula is:

\[
\text{Supply-Demand Gap}=\text{Supply}-\text{Demand}
\]

Where:

- Supply = available supply quantity (units).
- Demand = required demand quantity (units).

Interpretation:

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positive | Supply surplus |
| Zero | Demand and supply are balanced |
| Negative | Supply shortage |

**Example:**

Given:

- Demand = 12,000 units.
- Supply = 9,000 units.

Step 1: Apply the formula.

\[
9,000-12,000=-3,000
\]

Step 2: Interpret the result.

The company has a supply gap of negative 3,000 units.

In business reporting, we normally express this as:

**Supply shortage = 3,000 units.**

Remember that the negative sign indicates the direction of the imbalance.

## 5.3 Formula 2: Net Supply Shortage

The net supply shortage considers existing usable inventory.

For a simplified single-period calculation:

\[
\text{Net Supply Shortage}=\max(0,D-P-I)
\]

Where:

- \(D\) = demand (units).
- \(P\) = planned production or other eligible incoming supply (units).
- \(I\) = usable inventory available (units).

**Example:**

| Component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 12,000 |
| Planned production | 9,000 |
| Existing inventory | 2,000 |

Step 1: Calculate total supply.

\[
9,000+2,000=11,000
\]

Step 2: Calculate the shortage.

\[
12,000-11,000=1,000
\]

Step 3: Apply the non-negative shortage convention.

\[
\max(0,1,000)=1,000
\]

**Net shortage: 1,000 units**

Interpretation:

The company needs another 1,000 units to fulfill the required demand.

**Important:** If you are using beginning inventory, scheduled receipts, and planned production together, make sure the same inventory or receipt is not counted twice.

## 5.4 Formula 3: Projected Inventory Shortfall

This formula is useful when examining inventory over multiple periods.

The basic inventory balance is:

\[
EI=BI+R+P-D
\]

Where:

- \(EI\) = ending inventory.
- \(BI\) = beginning inventory.
- \(R\) = scheduled receipts.
- \(P\) = planned good production.
- \(D\) = demand.

For the simplified example, assume no transfers, scrap adjustments, or other inventory movements.

**Example:**

- Beginning inventory = 2,000 units.
- Scheduled receipts = 1,000 units.
- Planned production = 5,000 units.
- Demand = 7,000 units.

Step 1: Calculate total available inventory and supply.

\[
2,000+1,000+5,000=8,000
\]

Step 2: Calculate ending inventory.

\[
EI=8,000-7,000
\]

\[
EI=1,000
\]

The company finishes the period with 1,000 units.

Now let's change planned production to 3,000 units.

\[
EI=2,000+1,000+3,000-7,000
\]

\[
EI=-1,000
\]

**Projected shortfall: 1,000 units**

A negative projected ending inventory indicates that the company cannot fulfill all planned demand under the assumed timing and allocation rules.

For reporting purposes:

\[
\text{Inventory Shortfall}=\max(0,-EI)
\]

A positive ending inventory indicates remaining stock, while a negative ending inventory indicates unmet requirements.

## 5.5 Formula 4: Cumulative Supply Gap Across Multiple Periods

This is particularly important in monthly S&OP planning.

The cumulative gap helps us understand how shortages or surpluses affect inventory over time.

The formula for cumulative projected inventory is:

\[
EI_t=BI_t+R_t+P_t-D_t
\]

And:

\[
BI_{t+1}=EI_t
\]

Where:

- \(t\) = current period.
- \(BI_t\) = beginning inventory for the current period.
- \(R_t\) = scheduled receipts during the period.
- \(P_t\) = planned production during the period.
- \(D_t\) = demand during the period.
- \(EI_t\) = ending inventory for the period.

**Example:**

Assume the company starts January with 1,000 units.

| Month | Beginning inventory | Production | Demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 1,000 | 4,000 | 5,000 |
| February | 0 | 4,000 | 5,000 |
| March | 0 | 6,000 | 5,000 |

Assume there are no scheduled receipts or other inventory movements.

January:

\[
EI=1,000+4,000-5,000=0
\]

February:

\[
EI=0+4,000-5,000=-1,000
\]

March:

\[
EI=-1,000+6,000-5,000=0
\]

**Projected inventory position**

| Month | Projected ending inventory |
|---|---:|
| January | 0 |
| February | -1,000 |
| March | 0 |

February has a projected shortage of 1,000 units. March's additional production can recover the inventory position, but it does not automatically mean February's customer deliveries were fulfilled.

A useful alternative is to calculate the cumulative net supply-demand difference:

\[
CG_t=\sum_{i=1}^{t}(S_i-D_i)
\]

Where:

- \(CG_t\) = cumulative supply-demand gap.
- \(S_i\) = supply during period \(i\).
- \(D_i\) = demand during period \(i\).

Using the example, January has a zero net gap, February has -1,000, and March has +1,000.

The cumulative balance becomes:

| Month | Monthly net gap | Cumulative gap |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 0 | 0 |
| February | -1,000 | -1,000 |
| March | +1,000 | 0 |

**Important technical distinction:** The cumulative net gap is a mathematical balance. It is not necessarily the same as actual backlog. Once demand is missed, it must be tracked as a customer commitment and carried forward only if it remains unfulfilled.

## 5.6 Formula 5: Percentage of Demand Fulfilled

This measures how much of the required demand can be fulfilled.

\[
\text{Demand Fulfillment \%}
=
\frac{\text{Demand Fulfilled}}{\text{Total Demand}}
\times100
\]

Where:

- Demand fulfilled = quantity actually allocated or delivered according to the defined measurement.
- Total demand = total demand requirement.
- Unit = percentage.

**Example:**

- Total demand = 10,000 units.
- Demand fulfilled = 8,500 units.

\[
\frac{8,500}{10,000}\times100
=85\%
\]

**Demand fulfillment: 85%**

Interpretation:

The company can fulfill 85% of its demand, leaving 15% unfulfilled.

**Important:** Demand fulfillment is not automatically equivalent to On-Time In-Full (OTIF).

A customer may receive all 1,000 units eventually, but if delivery happens after the agreed date, OTIF performance may still be affected.

## 5.7 Formula 6: Unfulfilled Demand and Backlog

Unfulfilled demand is the portion of demand that cannot be fulfilled within the required period.

\[
\text{Unfulfilled Demand}=D-F
\]

Where:

- \(D\) = total demand.
- \(F\) = demand fulfilled.

Example:

- Demand = 10,000.
- Fulfilled demand = 8,500.

\[
10,000-8,500=1,500
\]

The company has 1,500 units of unfulfilled demand.

However, there is an important distinction between unfulfilled demand and backlog.

**Unfulfilled demand** refers to demand not fulfilled within the defined period.

**Backlog** refers to outstanding customer demand that remains open and is carried forward into future periods.

For example:

| Month | New demand | Fulfilled | Ending backlog |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 8,500 | 1,500 |
| February | 8,000 | 9,000 | 500 |
| March | 9,000 | 8,500 | 1,000 |

Assume all fulfilled quantities are applied first to the oldest outstanding customer requirements.

January:

\[
10,000-8,500=1,500
\]

February:

\[
1,500+8,000-9,000=500
\]

March:

\[
500+9,000-8,500=1,000
\]

This illustrates how backlog carries forward between periods.

The company does not start every month with zero unmet demand.

It must consider existing outstanding commitments.

### 5.8 Important calculation considerations

Before using these formulas in a real company, remember the following:

| Consideration | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Inventory availability | Only usable stock should be counted. |
| Scheduled receipts | Must arrive within the required period. |
| Production timing | Production must finish before the demand requirement date. |
| Production yield | Use expected good output, not simply gross production quantity. |
| Customer allocation | Available supply may already be committed to another customer. |
| Lead time | Supply arriving after the demand date cannot fulfill that earlier requirement. |
| Backlog | Unfulfilled prior demand may become part of future requirements. |
| Double counting | Avoid counting the same supply in inventory, receipts, and production. |

One more technical distinction: In real planning systems, the projected available balance is often calculated using time-phased inventory transactions, not simply total monthly quantities. A shipment arriving on the last day of the month may be too late for an order due on the first day.

That is why weekly or daily buckets can be necessary for critical shortages.

# 6. Root Cause Analysis

Identifying a shortage tells us WHAT happened.

Root cause analysis tells us WHY it happened.

This is an essential responsibility of an S&OP analyst.

## 6.1 Symptoms versus root causes

Let's use a manufacturing example.

The company has a shortage of 2,000 units.

The initial report states:

**Symptom:** Product A cannot meet January demand.

But why?

- Production output was below plan.
- Why was output below plan?
- A machine was frequently stopped.
- Why was the machine frequently stopped?
- A critical component was failing.
- Why was the component failing?
- The supplier delivered components outside the required specification.

Now we are closer to the underlying cause.

| Level | Finding |
|---|---|
| Business symptom | Customer demand cannot be fulfilled |
| Operational symptom | Production output below plan |
| Immediate cause | Machine downtime |
| Underlying cause | Component failure |
| Potential root cause | Supplier quality-control problem |

A key lesson:

**Do not stop investigating at the first explanation.**

A supplier delay may be the immediate cause, but the deeper cause might be poor supplier planning, inadequate safety stock, or inaccurate lead-time assumptions.

## 6.2 Introduction to the 5 Whys

The 5 Whys is a root cause analysis technique where you repeatedly ask "Why?" until you identify an actionable underlying cause.

Example:

**5 Whys: Production shortage**

1. **Why 1: Why was customer demand not fulfilled?** Production output was insufficient.
2. **Why 2: Why was production output insufficient?** The production machine experienced frequent downtime.
3. **Why 3: Why did the machine experience frequent downtime?** A critical component repeatedly failed.
4. **Why 4: Why did the component repeatedly fail?** Incoming components did not consistently meet specifications.
5. **Why 5: Why were nonconforming components repeatedly received?** The supplier's quality-control process did not consistently detect the defect.

**Potential root cause:** Supplier quality-control weakness.

Notice that the solution should not simply be "increase production."

The company may need supplier corrective action, incoming inspection, or an alternative supplier.

Also, five questions are not a mandatory limit. Sometimes three are sufficient; sometimes more are necessary. The objective is to find a supported, actionable cause.

## 6.3 Introduction to Pareto Analysis

Pareto Analysis helps identify which problems contribute most significantly to the total shortage.

The principle is commonly associated with the 80/20 rule.

It does not mean that every business problem follows an exact 80/20 distribution.

It means that a relatively small number of causes may account for a large portion of the impact.

Example:

A factory has 10,000 units of total identified supply gaps.

| Root cause | Shortage contribution |
|---|---:|
| Supplier delays | 5,000 |
| Machine downtime | 2,500 |
| Material quality rejection | 1,500 |
| Labor shortage | 600 |
| Planning errors | 400 |
| **Total** | **10,000** |

Calculate each cause's contribution:

\[
\text{Contribution \%}
=
\frac{\text{Cause Shortage}}{\text{Total Shortage}}\times100
\]

For supplier delays:

\[
\frac{5,000}{10,000}\times100=50\%
\]

For machine downtime:

\[
\frac{2,500}{10,000}\times100=25\%
\]

For material quality rejection:

\[
\frac{1,500}{10,000}\times100=15\%
\]

The cumulative result:

| Root cause | Gap quantity | Contribution | Cumulative |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Supplier delays | 5,000 | 50% | 50% |
| Machine downtime | 2,500 | 25% | 75% |
| Quality rejection | 1,500 | 15% | 90% |
| Labor shortage | 600 | 6% | 96% |
| Planning errors | 400 | 4% | 100% |

**Illustrative shortage contribution by root cause:**

| Root cause | Shortage contribution |
|---|---:|
| Supplier delays | 5,000 units |
| Machine downtime | 2,500 units |
| Quality rejection | 1,500 units |
| Labor shortage | 600 units |
| Planning errors | 400 units |

Interpretation:

Supplier delays and machine downtime account for 75% of the total shortage.

Therefore, these two causes deserve priority in the investigation.

This is useful because companies have limited resources. They should prioritize the problems with the largest operational and business impact.

## 6.4 Classifying the root cause

For S&OP reporting, you can group supply gaps into five major categories.

| Category | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|
| Demand-related | Did demand increase beyond the agreed plan? |
| Capacity-related | Can the factory produce the required quantity? |
| Material-related | Are sufficient raw materials available? |
| Supplier-related | Can suppliers deliver the required quantities on time? |
| Planning-related | Are the planning assumptions and system data accurate? |

You can use this classification when consolidating shortages across multiple products and manufacturing sites.

# 7. Practical Manufacturing Application

Now let's combine everything into one realistic manufacturing scenario.

This is where we move from understanding formulas individually to using them together as an S&OP analyst.

## 7.1 Business scenario: Three-product manufacturing company

Imagine a manufacturing company producing three finished products:

- Product A
- Product B
- Product C

The company is preparing its January Supply Review.

The demand planning team has submitted the following requirements:

| Product | January demand |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 units |
| Product B | 3,000 units |
| Product C | 2,000 units |
| **Total** | **10,000 units** |

The supply planning team reviews its available inventory, incoming receipts, and planned production.

### Available inventory

| Product | Beginning inventory |
|---|---:|
| A | 500 |
| B | 400 |
| C | 200 |
| **Total** | **1,100** |

### Scheduled receipts

| Product | Scheduled receipts |
|---|---:|
| A | 0 |
| B | 500 |
| C | 0 |
| **Total** | **500** |

Product B has an incoming supplier delivery of 500 units, confirmed to arrive within January and before its customer requirement dates.

### Planned production

| Product | Planned production |
|---|---:|
| A | 4,000 |
| B | 2,200 |
| C | 1,500 |
| **Total** | **7,700** |

These are expected good finished goods, not gross production starts.

## 7.2 Step 1: Calculate available supply

Formula:

\[
S=BI+R+P
\]

Where:

- \(S\) = total available supply.
- \(BI\) = beginning inventory.
- \(R\) = scheduled receipts.
- \(P\) = planned production.

Calculate each product:

**Product A**

\[
500+0+4,000=4,500
\]

**Product B**

\[
400+500+2,200=3,100
\]

**Product C**

\[
200+0+1,500=1,700
\]

Consolidated results:

| Product | Inventory | Receipts | Production | Available supply |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 500 | 0 | 4,000 | 4,500 |
| B | 400 | 500 | 2,200 | 3,100 |
| C | 200 | 0 | 1,500 | 1,700 |
| **Total** | **1,100** | **500** | **7,700** | **9,300** |

The company has 9,300 units of available supply against 10,000 units of demand.

## 7.3 Step 2: Identify supply gaps by product

Apply:

\[
Gap=Supply-Demand
\]

| Product | Demand | Supply | Supply-demand gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 4,500 | -500 |
| B | 3,000 | 3,100 | +100 |
| C | 2,000 | 1,700 | -300 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **9,300** | **-700** |

**January supply gap summary**

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Total demand | 10,000 |
| Available supply | 9,300 |
| Gross product shortages | 800 |
| Product B surplus | 100 |
| Net aggregate shortage | 700 |

Notice an important detail.

There are two ways of looking at the shortage:

**Gross product shortages:**

\[
500+300=800
\]

**Net aggregate shortage:**

\[
800-100=700
\]

The 100-unit surplus of Product B reduces the aggregate imbalance mathematically, but it does not eliminate the actual shortages of Products A and C.

For operational reporting, we should preserve the product-level shortages instead of hiding them through aggregation.

## 7.4 Step 3: Investigate the root causes

The supply planning team now investigates why planned production is insufficient.

The initial operational findings are:

| Product | Supply gap | Initial finding |
|---|---:|---|
| A | 500 | Supplier delay affecting critical raw material |
| B | +100 | Supply exceeds demand |
| C | 300 | Production downtime reduced available output |

Let's investigate each.

**Product A: Supplier delay**

The factory needs a critical raw material to produce 4,500 units.

However, only enough material is available for 4,000 units of production.

The remaining 500 units cannot be produced because the supplier's next delivery will arrive in February.

This is a material availability and supplier timing problem.

**Product C: Production downtime**

Product C's production line experienced unexpected downtime.

The original production plan was higher, but the available machine time was insufficient to achieve the planned good output.

This is a capacity availability problem.

**Product B: Surplus**

Product B has 100 units more than its January demand.

The surplus may remain in inventory, subject to the company's inventory policy and future demand requirements.

It cannot be assumed to be interchangeable with A or C.

## 7.5 Step 4: Examine the business impact

Assume the company prioritizes its available supply to fulfill the January requirements.

| Product | Demand | Fulfilled | Unfulfilled |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 5,000 | 4,500 | 500 |
| B | 3,000 | 3,000 | 0 |
| C | 2,000 | 1,700 | 300 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **9,200** | **800** |

Why is the fulfilled quantity only 9,200 when available supply is 9,300?

Because Product B has 100 surplus units that are not needed to satisfy its January demand.

Calculate demand fulfillment:

\[
\frac{9,200}{10,000}\times100
=92\%
\]

**Demand fulfillment: 92%**

The company can fulfill 92% of its January demand, leaving 800 units unfulfilled.

This is the operational result.

But an S&OP analyst must also understand the financial impact.

## 7.6 Step 5: Quantify the potential revenue impact

Assume the following illustrative selling prices:

| Product | Selling price | Unfulfilled units |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | ₱500 | 500 |
| B | ₱300 | 0 |
| C | ₱800 | 300 |

Potential delayed sales value:

Product A:

\[
500\times ₱500=₱250,000
\]

Product C:

\[
300\times ₱800=₱240,000
\]

Total:

\[
₱250,000+₱240,000=₱490,000
\]

**Potential delayed sales value: ₱490,000**

Illustrative sales value associated with 800 unfulfilled units, assuming all units are eventually sold at the stated prices.

Important distinction:

This is not necessarily lost revenue.

If the company delivers the products in February and customers still accept them, the amount may represent delayed sales rather than permanently lost sales.

We must distinguish:

- Delayed revenue.
- Lost sales.
- Backlog.
- Customer cancellation.

These have different financial implications.

## 7.7 Step 6: Investigate material requirements

Let's go one level deeper.

Suppose Product A requires 2 kg of a critical raw material per finished unit.

The company has enough material for 4,000 units, but demand requires 5,000 units.

Additional material requirement:

\[
(5,000-4,000)\times2
\]

\[
=2,000\text{ kg}
\]

The company needs another 2,000 kg of material to support the additional production.

However, we must also verify whether the factory has sufficient remaining production capacity.

Material availability alone does not guarantee that the extra units can be produced.

This is where the concepts from Topic 4.3 and Topic 4.5 become important:

- Capacity Planning determines whether additional production is possible.
- Lead Time Management determines whether the material can arrive in time.

## 7.8 Step 7: Prepare the supply gap investigation report

An S&OP analyst can summarize the findings as follows:

| Product | Gap | Primary issue | Classification |
|---|---:|---|---|
| A | 500 | Delayed raw material | Supplier/material |
| B | 0 | No shortage | Balanced |
| C | 300 | Production downtime | Capacity |
| **Total** | **800** | **Two main constraints** | |

The analyst should also identify:

1. When will the shortage occur?
2. How many customers will be affected?
3. Which customer orders have priority?
4. Is the shortage temporary or recurring?
5. Can inventory transfers help?
6. Can production be rescheduled?
7. What is the potential revenue impact?
8. When can the shortage be recovered?

These questions prepare the information needed for Topic 6.3.

# 8. Supply Gap Analysis in S&OP

Now let's look at how this information becomes part of the actual S&OP process.

## 8.1 Role of the S&OP Analyst

An S&OP analyst is not necessarily the person who operates the production line or negotiates with suppliers.

Instead, the analyst consolidates information from different functions and translates it into actionable business insights.

Typical information sources include:

| Department | Information provided |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer orders, forecasts, priority accounts |
| Demand Planning | Consensus demand forecast |
| Production | Production plan, output, capacity constraints |
| Procurement | Purchase orders, supplier commitments, material availability |
| Warehouse | Inventory balances and stock availability |
| Logistics | Transportation schedules and delivery constraints |
| Finance | Revenue, costs, financial exposure |

The analyst connects these information sources to determine the actual supply position.

## 8.2 How to report supply gaps

A useful supply gap report should not simply show demand and supply totals.

It should communicate the problem, its timing, its cause, and its impact.

A recommended structure:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Product | Affected product or SKU |
| Location | Manufacturing site or distribution center |
| Period | Week or month of shortage |
| Demand | Required quantity |
| Available supply | Usable supply quantity |
| Supply gap | Shortage quantity |
| Root cause | Main identified constraint |
| Business impact | Revenue, customers, or production impact |
| Recovery date | Expected date supply becomes available |
| Owner | Responsible department or person |
| Status | Open, under investigation, or resolved |

### Example of a management-level report

**January Supply Risk Summary**

| Measure | Result |
|---|---:|
| Products with shortages | 2 of 3 |
| Total unfulfilled demand | 800 |
| Demand fulfillment | 92% |
| Potential delayed sales | ₱490K |
| Identified main causes | 2 |

**Priority issues**

- **High: Product A, 500 units.** Critical material shortage caused by supplier delay.
- **Medium: Product C, 300 units.** Reduced output caused by production downtime.

The severity labels above are illustrative. Actual classifications should follow company-defined criteria.

## 8.3 Critical shortages versus manageable gaps

Not every supply gap deserves the same level of attention.

For example:

- A shortage of 100 units may be critical if it affects a major customer shipment.
- A shortage of 1,000 units may be manageable if the affected demand is flexible and can be fulfilled next month.

Therefore, shortage quantity alone is not enough to determine priority.

Consider these factors:

| Evaluation factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Shortage quantity | Measures the physical gap |
| Customer importance | Identifies strategic or high-priority customers |
| Delivery commitment | Determines urgency |
| Revenue exposure | Measures financial significance |
| Inventory coverage | Shows how long current stock can support demand |
| Recovery time | Determines how long the shortage will persist |
| Production criticality | Identifies bottleneck products or processes |
| Regulatory or contractual requirements | May increase the severity of missed commitments |

An S&OP analyst should distinguish between a large but manageable gap and a small but critical gap.

## 8.4 How supply gap analysis supports Supply Review

During Supply Review, the supply planning team evaluates whether the proposed supply plan can support the demand plan.

The discussion typically includes:

1. Available production capacity.
2. Planned production quantities.
3. Material availability.
4. Supplier delivery commitments.
5. Inventory coverage.
6. Expected production output.
7. Identified supply shortages.
8. Recovery possibilities.

The objective is to determine whether the supply plan is operationally achievable.

For example:

The Demand Plan requires 50,000 units.

The Supply Plan can only provide 45,000 units.

The supply team identifies:

- 3,000 units constrained by material shortages.
- 2,000 units constrained by machine capacity.

These findings become important discussion points during Supply Review.

## 8.5 How supply gap analysis supports Pre-S&OP

Supply Review identifies and investigates operational constraints.

Pre-S&OP brings the cross-functional teams together to evaluate the overall demand-supply imbalance and possible responses.

For example:

| Identified issue | Information required for Pre-S&OP |
|---|---|
| Material shortage | Quantity, supplier recovery date, alternative sourcing |
| Capacity shortage | Additional hours, shifts, outsourcing options |
| Delivery shortfall | Affected customer orders and recovery dates |
| Inventory shortage | Available stock and transfer opportunities |
| Revenue exposure | Delayed or potentially lost sales |

The objective is to present management with a clear picture of the remaining gaps, their business consequences, and the decisions required.

The supply gap analysis becomes the factual foundation for the next topic: **Supply-Demand Balancing Strategies**.

# 9. Key Takeaways

Bago tayo mag-proceed sa next topic, ito ang mga concepts na dapat mong maintindihan at maalala.

## 9.1 Essential concepts

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Supply Gap | Difference between required demand and available supply |
| Capacity Shortage | Insufficient production resources |
| Material Shortage | Insufficient raw materials or components |
| Delivery Shortfall | Actual delivery is below the committed quantity |
| Gross Supply Gap | Shortage before considering existing inventory |
| Net Supply Gap | Remaining shortage after considering eligible inventory and supply |
| Temporary Gap | Timing-related shortage |
| Structural Shortage | Persistent supply capability problem |
| Root Cause Analysis | Investigation of the underlying reason for a shortage |
| Pareto Analysis | Identifies the causes contributing most to the problem |
| 5 Whys | Repeated questioning to identify underlying causes |
| Backlog | Outstanding customer demand carried forward |

## 9.2 Essential formulas

These are the formulas worth keeping in your S&OP reviewer.

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Supply-demand gap | Supply − Demand |
| Net shortage | MAX(0, Demand − Eligible Supply) |
| Ending inventory | Beginning Inventory + Receipts + Production − Demand |
| Inventory shortfall | MAX(0, −Ending Inventory) |
| Cumulative gap | Cumulative Supply − Cumulative Demand |
| Demand fulfillment % | Fulfilled Demand ÷ Total Demand × 100 |
| Unfulfilled demand | Total Demand − Fulfilled Demand |
| Backlog | Previous Backlog + New Demand − Fulfilled Demand |

Remember that these are simplified planning formulas. Actual company calculations may include allocations, transfers, safety stock policies, quality holds, and time-phased inventory transactions.

## 9.3 Three important lessons

**Lesson 1: Supply availability is not the same as production capacity.**

A factory can have enough machine capacity but insufficient raw materials.

**Lesson 2: Total supply can hide product-level shortages.**

An overall balanced supply-demand position does not guarantee that every product, location, or customer requirement can be fulfilled.

**Lesson 3: Identifying the gap is not enough.**

An S&OP analyst must determine the quantity, timing, root cause, business impact, and recovery requirements.
