# Module 6 – Topic 6.6: Trade-off Analysis

**Main learning objective:** Matutunan mong mag-evaluate ng different business alternatives at pumili ng solution na nagbibigay ng pinakamagandang overall business outcome, kahit may compromises sa cost, capacity, inventory, customer service, at profitability.

# 1. Topic Overview

### 1.1 What is Trade-off Analysis?

Imagine na ang isang manufacturing company ay may customer demand na 10,000 units, pero ang available production capacity ay 8,000 units lamang.

May shortage na 2,000 units.

Ano ang puwedeng gawin ng company?

1. Mag-overtime para madagdagan ang production.
2. Mag-subcontract sa external manufacturer.
3. Gumamit ng existing inventory.
4. Makipag-negotiate sa customer para i-reschedule ang delivery.
5. Mag-combine ng multiple solutions.

Ang problema, bawat solution ay may kapalit.

| Alternative | Potential benefit | Possible disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime | Faster production | Higher labor cost |
| Subcontracting | Additional capacity | Higher purchasing cost |
| Inventory utilization | Immediate fulfillment | Lower inventory buffer |
| Demand rescheduling | Avoid additional production cost | Delayed customer delivery |
| Combined approach | Balanced solution | More complex coordination |

Dito pumapasok ang **Trade-off Analysis**.

Trade-off analysis is the process of evaluating competing alternatives by examining their benefits, costs, risks, and operational consequences to determine which option provides the best overall business outcome.

Sa madaling salita:

**Hindi lang natin tinatanong kung alin ang pinakamurang solution. Tinatanong natin kung alin ang pinakamagandang decision para sa buong business.**

### 1.2 Why is it important in S&OP?

Ang S&OP ay hindi simpleng process ng pag-match ng demand at supply.

May financial at operational consequences ang bawat decision.

Halimbawa:

- Increasing inventory can improve customer service but increase working capital.
- Increasing production can reduce backlog but increase operating expenses.
- Using cheaper suppliers can reduce procurement costs but potentially increase lead time.
- Reducing production can lower costs but potentially reduce revenue.

Kaya kailangan ng trade-off analysis para hindi isolated ang decision ng isang department.

### 1.3 Connection to previous topics

| Previous topic | Connection to Trade-off Analysis |
|---|---|
| 6.3 Supply-Demand Balancing Strategies | Identifies possible solutions to supply-demand gaps. |
| 6.4 Demand Prioritization | Determines which demand should receive limited supply. |

| **6.6 Trade-off Analysis** | Evaluates which alternative provides the best overall outcome. |

Think of the sequence:

**Identify the problem → Develop alternatives → Evaluate trade-offs → Recommend a decision.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

### 2.1 Understanding trade-offs through a simple example

Imagine may ₱100,000 budget ka para sa isang manufacturing project.

May dalawang available options:

- Option A: Purchase a cheaper machine for ₱80,000.
- Option B: Purchase a more expensive machine for ₱100,000.

Initially, mukhang mas attractive ang Option A dahil mas mura.

Pero after evaluating the machines:

| Consideration | Option A | Option B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Purchase cost | ₱80,000 | ₱100,000 |
| Production capacity | 500 units/day | 800 units/day |
| Maintenance cost/month | ₱8,000 | ₱4,000 |
| Expected downtime | Higher | Lower |

Now, which is better?

Hindi automatic na Option A dahil mas mura. Hindi rin automatic na Option B dahil mas productive.

Kailangan nating malaman:

- Gaano karaming production ang kailangan?
- Gaano kalaki ang impact ng downtime?
- Kaya ba ng budget ang Option B?
- Gaano katagal bago mabawi ang additional investment?

Ito ang essence ng trade-off analysis.

### 2.2 The central principle

Sa business, bihirang magkaroon ng solution na perfect sa lahat ng aspects.

Usually, kapag may improvement sa isang area, may corresponding cost or disadvantage sa ibang area.

For example:

**Customer service versus inventory**

Higher inventory provides benefits:
- Higher product availability.
- Faster order fulfillment.
- Lower stockout risk.

But it also creates trade-offs:
- Higher storage costs.
- More working capital required.
- Higher risk of obsolete inventory.

Ang challenge ng S&OP professional ay hanapin ang appropriate balance.

Hindi necessarily maximum customer service, minimum inventory, or maximum production ang goal.

Ang goal ay **optimal overall business performance within the company's constraints and priorities.**

# 3. Technical Explanation

### 3.1 Trade-off Analysis: Formal Definition

Trade-off analysis is a structured decision-making approach used to compare alternatives with competing objectives by evaluating their incremental financial impact, operational performance, risks, and constraints.

Important terms:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Alternative | A possible solution or course of action. |
| Evaluation criteria | Factors used to compare alternatives. |
| Incremental cost | Additional cost caused by choosing an alternative. |
| Opportunity cost | Value of the benefit sacrificed when choosing one option instead of another. |
| Constraint | A limitation that restricts available decisions. |
| Trade-off | A compromise between competing objectives. |
| Optimization | Finding the best outcome given defined objectives and constraints. |

### 3.2 Why do competing objectives exist?

Different departments have different responsibilities.

For example:

| Department | Primary concern |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer demand and revenue |
| Operations | Production efficiency and capacity |
| Supply Chain | Material availability and delivery |
| Finance | Costs, profitability, and cash flow |
| Procurement | Supplier pricing and reliability |
| Inventory Management | Availability and inventory investment |

Consider this situation:

Sales wants 100% customer fulfillment.

Operations wants stable production schedules.

Finance wants lower operating costs.

Supply Chain wants sufficient inventory.

All these objectives are reasonable, but they may conflict.

For example, maintaining enough inventory for 100% availability may require significant working capital. Finance may therefore challenge the inventory target.

**Trade-off analysis creates a common basis for evaluating these competing objectives.**

### 3.3 Difference between Trade-off Analysis, Scenario Planning, and Cost-Benefit Analysis

These three concepts are related, but they answer different questions.

| Method | Main question | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario Planning | What could happen? | Develop possible future situations. |
| Trade-off Analysis | Which alternative should we choose? | Evaluate competing objectives and compromises. |
| Cost-Benefit Analysis | Are the benefits worth the costs? | Determine whether expected benefits justify the required investment. |

Let's connect them through one manufacturing problem.

**Situation:** Forecast demand is expected to increase by 20%, but current production capacity is insufficient.

1. Scenario planning develops alternatives:
   - Demand increases by 10%.
   - Demand increases by 20%.
   - Demand increases by 30%.

2. Trade-off analysis compares possible responses:
   - Overtime.
   - Subcontracting.
   - Additional inventory.
   - Capacity expansion.

3. Cost-benefit analysis evaluates whether the expected financial benefits justify the cost of each investment.

Notice that they complement each other rather than replace each other.

# 4. Detailed Discussion: Common S&OP Trade-offs

Now let's discuss the trade-offs that frequently appear in manufacturing and supply chain operations.

## 4.1 Customer Service versus Inventory Investment

This is one of the most common S&OP trade-offs.

A company wants to fulfill customer orders immediately. To achieve this, it maintains sufficient inventory.

However, maintaining more inventory requires more money.

**Example:**

A manufacturing company wants to increase its customer service level from 90% to 98%.

To achieve this, it increases safety stock.

| Metric | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Safety stock | 1,000 units | 2,500 units |
| Average inventory | 5,000 units | 6,500 units |
| Customer service level | 90% | 98% |
| Inventory investment | ₱500,000 | ₱650,000 |

The improvement comes with an additional ₱150,000 inventory investment.

**Business interpretation:**

The company must determine whether the additional customer service improvement justifies the additional working capital and inventory carrying costs.

Important consideration: Higher inventory does not automatically guarantee higher service levels. Demand variability, replenishment lead time, and inventory positioning also matter.

## 4.2 Production Capacity versus Operating Costs

Increasing production capacity can improve fulfillment, but it may require additional resources.

Common options include:

- Adding production shifts.
- Increasing machine operating hours.
- Hiring additional operators.
- Purchasing additional equipment.
- Improving production efficiency.

For example:

| Metric | Normal operation | Extended operation |
|---|---:|---:|
| Daily capacity | 8,000 units | 10,000 units |
| Operating hours | 8 hours | 10 hours |
| Daily labor cost | ₱40,000 | ₱55,000 |
| Production increase | Baseline | +2,000 units |

The company gains 2,000 additional units but spends another ₱15,000 per day.

The important question:

Is the additional production worth the additional operating expense?

## 4.3 Overtime versus Subcontracting

Suppose the company needs an additional 1,000 units.

It can either:

- Produce internally through overtime.
- Outsource production to another manufacturer.

| Consideration | Overtime | Subcontracting |
|---|---|---|
| Production cost | Usually lower than outsourcing | May be higher |
| Capacity | Limited by internal resources | Access to external capacity |
| Quality control | More direct control | Requires supplier monitoring |
| Lead time | Depends on internal readiness | Depends on supplier availability |
| Flexibility | Limited by labor and equipment | Depends on supplier agreement |
| Operational risk | Employee fatigue, equipment wear | Supplier quality and delivery risk |

Neither option is universally better.

Overtime may be cheaper, but internal capacity might already be constrained.

Subcontracting may be more expensive, but it can prevent production bottlenecks and protect customer commitments.

## 4.4 Inventory Build-Ahead versus Production Flexibility

**Inventory build-ahead** means producing goods earlier than the actual customer requirement.

For example, if demand is expected to increase next month, the company produces additional finished goods this month.

Benefits:

- Less pressure during peak demand.
- Better utilization of available capacity.
- Potentially fewer overtime requirements.
- Improved readiness for customer orders.

Disadvantages:

- Higher inventory holding costs.
- Increased working capital.
- Risk of forecast error.
- Possible obsolescence.
- Less flexibility if customer requirements change.

Consider two situations:

**Situation A:** Demand is stable and predictable.

Build-ahead inventory may be a good strategy.

**Situation B:** Demand is highly uncertain and product specifications frequently change.

Producing too early may create excess or obsolete inventory.

The decision depends on demand predictability, product shelf life, production lead time, and available capacity.

## 4.5 Demand Fulfillment versus Profitability

Not every customer order should automatically be fulfilled regardless of cost.

Imagine:

- Selling price: ₱100/unit.
- Normal variable cost: ₱70/unit.
- Additional emergency production cost: ₱40/unit.

Normal contribution margin:

\[
100-70=₱30
\]

But if emergency production is required:

\[
100-70-40=-₱10
\]

The company loses ₱10 per additional unit on a contribution basis.

This does not necessarily mean the company should reject the order.

Other considerations include:

- Strategic customer relationships.
- Contractual commitments.
- Future business opportunities.
- Customer retention.
- Penalties for non-fulfillment.

However, the financial impact must be visible before management approves the decision.

## 4.6 Supplier Cost versus Lead Time and Reliability

A supplier offering the lowest price may not provide the best overall value.

| Consideration | Supplier A | Supplier B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unit price | ₱100 | ₱110 |
| Lead time | 30 days | 15 days |
| On-time delivery | 85% | 98% |
| Quality performance | Moderate | High |

Supplier A has a lower purchase price.

Supplier B has a higher purchase price but provides shorter lead time and better delivery reliability.

The additional ₱10 per unit may be justified if it reduces:

- Safety stock requirements.
- Production interruptions.
- Expediting expenses.
- Customer delivery delays.

This is an example of evaluating total cost rather than purchase price alone.

## 4.7 Short-Term Solutions versus Long-Term Improvements

Sometimes, the best immediate solution is not the best long-term solution.

Example: A manufacturing line frequently experiences capacity shortages.

| Short-term solution | Long-term solution |
|---|---|
| Overtime | Process improvement |
| Subcontracting | Additional production capacity |
| Expedited procurement | Supplier development |
| Inventory build-ahead | Better demand and production planning |
| Temporary manpower | Workforce capability development |

A short-term solution addresses the immediate problem.

A long-term improvement addresses the underlying cause.

**Important S&OP principle:** Do not repeatedly use expensive short-term solutions without evaluating whether a structural improvement would provide better long-term financial and operational results.

# 5. Evaluating Business Alternatives

Before calculating anything, we need a structured evaluation process.

Hindi magandang approach na agad mag-compute without identifying what we are trying to solve.

Use the following sequence.

## Trade-off Analysis Framework

1. **Define the business problem.** Identify the demand-supply gap, operational issue, or financial objective.
2. **Identify available alternatives.** Determine the feasible solutions.
3. **Establish evaluation criteria.** Decide what factors will determine whether an option is attractive.
4. **Quantify the impacts.** Calculate costs, savings, production output, inventory, revenue, and profitability.
5. **Evaluate risks and constraints.** Determine what could prevent an option from succeeding.
6. **Compare alternatives.** Use consistent assumptions and a common evaluation period.
7. **Identify the preferred alternative.** Select the option that best supports the agreed business priorities.

## 5.1 Establishing Evaluation Criteria

The criteria depend on the business problem.

For a manufacturing supply shortage, we might use:

| Category | Evaluation criteria | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Incremental cost | ₱ |
| Financial | Contribution margin | ₱ |
| Financial | Working capital | ₱ |
| Operational | Additional production | Units |
| Operational | Capacity utilization | % |
| Inventory | Inventory consumption | Units |
| Customer | On-time fulfillment | % |
| Customer | Backlog | Units |
| Risk | Quality and delivery risk | Low / Medium / High |

Notice that not all criteria are expressed in pesos.

Some are quantitative operational measures, while others are qualitative assessments.

We should not force every business consideration into a monetary value if reliable valuation is unavailable.

## 5.2 Quantitative versus Qualitative Considerations

**Quantitative considerations** are measurable using numerical values.

Examples:
- Additional production cost: ₱50,000.
- Additional capacity: 1,500 units.
- Inventory reduction: 500 units.
- Customer fulfillment: 95%.

**Qualitative considerations** require judgment or descriptive evaluation.

Examples:
- Supplier relationship.
- Customer importance.
- Operational complexity.
- Employee fatigue.
- Long-term strategic value.

A good trade-off analysis combines both.

## 5.3 Consistent Assumptions

This is extremely important.

Imagine comparing overtime and subcontracting.

If the overtime option assumes a 10-day production period while subcontracting assumes a 30-day period, the comparison may not be fair.

Use consistent assumptions whenever possible:

- Same demand quantity.
- Same planning horizon.
- Same product specifications.
- Same customer requirements.
- Same financial period.
- Same cost treatment.
- Same inventory availability.

Otherwise, the comparison can lead to an incorrect recommendation.

# 6. Financial and Operational Considerations

Before introducing the formulas, we need to understand the business terms involved.

## 6.1 Revenue, Profit, and Contribution Margin

These three are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Consider a company selling one product.

| Financial item | Amount per unit |
|---|---:|
| Selling price | ₱160 |
| Variable manufacturing cost | ₱100 |
| Variable selling and distribution cost | ₱10 |
| Fixed operating expenses | Depends on total operations |

### A. Revenue

Revenue is the amount generated from selling products or services.

\[
Revenue=Selling\ Price\times Units\ Sold
\]

If the company sells 1,000 units:

\[
₱160\times1,000=₱160,000
\]

Revenue is not the same as profit.

### B. Contribution Margin

Contribution margin is the amount remaining after deducting variable costs from revenue.

\[
CM=Revenue-Variable\ Costs
\]

Per-unit contribution margin:

\[
CM/unit=Selling\ Price-Variable\ Cost/unit
\]

Using our example:

\[
CM/unit=160-100-10
\]

\[
\boxed{CM/unit=₱50}
\]

For 1,000 units:

\[
₱50\times1,000=₱50,000
\]

The ₱50,000 contribution margin is available to cover fixed costs and then contribute to operating profit.

### C. Profit

Profit is what remains after deducting all relevant expenses.

Simplified operating profit:

\[
Operating\ Profit=Revenue-Variable\ Costs-Fixed\ Operating\ Expenses
\]

Suppose fixed operating expenses are ₱30,000.

\[
Operating\ Profit=160,000-110,000-30,000
\]

\[
\boxed{Operating\ Profit=₱20,000}
\]

The relationship:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱160,000 |
| Variable costs | ₱110,000 |
| Contribution margin | ₱50,000 |
| Fixed operating expenses | ₱30,000 |
| **Operating profit** | **₱20,000** |

**Remember:**

- Revenue tells us how much we generate from sales.
- Contribution margin tells us how much remains after variable costs.
- Profit tells us what remains after all applicable expenses.

For S&OP decisions, contribution margin is particularly useful when evaluating incremental demand, because it helps identify the financial contribution of additional units.

## 6.2 Production and Procurement Costs

Manufacturing costs can include:

- Direct materials.
- Direct labor.
- Variable manufacturing overhead.
- Fixed manufacturing overhead.
- Overtime premiums.
- Subcontracting charges.
- Expediting costs.

Procurement costs can include:

- Purchase price.
- Freight.
- Duties and taxes where applicable.
- Inspection costs.
- Supplier-related quality costs.

Important distinction:

**Relevant cost** is a cost that changes depending on which alternative is selected.

For example, if the company already pays the same monthly factory rent regardless of whether it uses overtime or subcontracting, that rent may not be relevant to the incremental decision.

## 6.3 Inventory Holding Costs

Inventory requires money to purchase, store, insure, and manage.

Inventory carrying cost may include:

- Cost of capital.
- Warehousing.
- Insurance.
- Handling.
- Obsolescence.
- Deterioration.

A company holding more inventory may increase its operating expenses and working capital requirements.

## 6.4 Working Capital

Working capital is a measure of short-term financial resources available to support business operations.

\[
Working\ Capital=Current\ Assets-Current\ Liabilities
\]

Inventory is part of current assets.

If inventory increases without a corresponding increase in current liabilities, more funds are tied up in operations.

This is why Finance may challenge a proposal to build additional inventory even if Operations believes it improves production stability.

## 6.5 Capacity Utilization

Capacity utilization measures how much of the available production capacity is being used.

\[
Capacity\ Utilization=
\frac{Actual\ Production}{Available\ Capacity}\times100
\]

Example:

- Actual production: 8,000 units.
- Available capacity: 10,000 units.

\[
\frac{8,000}{10,000}\times100=80\%
\]

Interpretation: The company is using 80% of its available production capacity.

However, 100% utilization is not automatically desirable. It may reduce flexibility, increase bottleneck risk, and make the operation less capable of absorbing unexpected demand.

## 6.6 Customer Service and Fulfillment

Two useful metrics:

**Demand fulfillment rate**

\[
Fulfillment\ Rate=
\frac{Units\ Fulfilled}{Total\ Demand}\times100
\]

**Backlog**

\[
Backlog=Unfulfilled\ Customer\ Demand
\]

Example:

- Demand: 10,000 units.
- Fulfilled: 9,000 units.

Fulfillment rate = 90%.

Backlog = 1,000 units, assuming all unfulfilled demand remains open.

Note that fulfillment rate and On-Time In-Full (OTIF) are not identical. OTIF also considers whether orders were delivered on time and in full.

# 7. Formulas and Calculations

Ito na ang quantitative foundation ng trade-off analysis.

We'll use illustrative figures in Philippine pesos and manufacturing units.

## 7.1 Incremental Cost and Savings

**Incremental cost** refers to the additional expense incurred when choosing an alternative compared with the baseline.

Formula:

\[
Incremental\ Cost=Alternative\ Cost-Baseline\ Cost
\]

Example:

- Baseline production cost: ₱100,000.
- Production with overtime: ₱130,000.

\[
Incremental\ Cost=130,000-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱30,000}
\]

The overtime decision requires an additional ₱30,000.

For savings:

\[
Savings=Baseline\ Cost-Alternative\ Cost
\]

If a new supplier reduces procurement cost from ₱200,000 to ₱180,000:

\[
Savings=200,000-180,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱20,000}
\]

**Interpretation:** Positive savings mean the alternative costs less than the baseline.

## 7.2 Contribution Margin

Formula:

\[
CM=Revenue-Variable\ Costs
\]

Or:

\[
CM/unit=Selling\ Price-Variable\ Cost/unit
\]

Example:

- Selling price: ₱160/unit.
- Variable cost: ₱110/unit.
- Additional sales: 500 units.

Step 1: Calculate contribution margin per unit.

\[
160-110=₱50
\]

Step 2: Calculate total contribution margin.

\[
500\times50=₱25,000
\]

Additional contribution margin = **₱25,000**.

Important: If emergency production requires an additional ₱15/unit overtime premium, the incremental contribution after that premium becomes:

\[
500\times(50-15)=₱17,500
\]

Do not deduct the same cost twice.

## 7.3 Cost per Additional Unit Produced

This determines how much additional cost the company incurs for every additional unit produced.

\[
Cost/Additional\ Unit=
\frac{Incremental\ Cost}{Additional\ Units}
\]

Example:

- Additional overtime expense: ₱24,000.
- Additional production: 1,200 units.

\[
\frac{24,000}{1,200}
=\boxed{₱20/unit}
\]

Interpretation: The company spends an additional ₱20 to produce each unit through overtime, relative to its baseline production plan.

## 7.4 Inventory Carrying Cost

Inventory carrying cost estimates the expense associated with maintaining inventory over a period.

Formula:

\[
Annual\ Carrying\ Cost=
Average\ Inventory\ Value\times Carrying\ Cost\ Rate
\]

Example:

- Average inventory value: ₱2,000,000.
- Annual carrying cost rate: 20%.

\[
2,000,000\times0.20
\]

\[
\boxed{₱400,000/year}
\]

Monthly equivalent, assuming an evenly distributed annual cost:

\[
400,000/12=\boxed{₱33,333.33/month}
\]

The 20% is an illustrative assumption, not a universal industry standard.

The carrying cost rate may include financing, storage, insurance, obsolescence, and other relevant expenses. Avoid counting components twice if they are already included separately in your financial model.

## 7.5 Revenue Impact of Unfulfilled Demand

Formula:

\[
Revenue\ at\ Risk=
Unfulfilled\ Units\times Selling\ Price
\]

Example:

- Customer demand: 10,000 units.
- Fulfilled demand: 8,000 units.
- Selling price: ₱160/unit.

Unfulfilled demand:

\[
10,000-8,000=2,000
\]

Revenue at risk:

\[
2,000\times160
\]

\[
\boxed{₱320,000}
\]

But take note!

**Revenue at risk is not automatically lost revenue or lost profit.**

There are three possibilities:

1. Customer cancels the order: potential lost revenue.
2. Customer accepts delayed delivery: revenue is deferred, not necessarily lost.
3. Customer accepts partial fulfillment: only the unfulfilled portion remains exposed.

If all 2,000 units are permanently lost, the lost contribution margin is:

\[
2,000\times ₱50=\boxed{₱100,000}
\]

This is usually more useful than the revenue figure when evaluating the financial contribution sacrificed by losing those sales.

## 7.6 Total Relevant Cost Comparison

When comparing alternatives, consider only the costs that differ between them, while including all relevant consequences.

Simplified formula:

\[
Total\ Relevant\ Cost=
Production\ Cost+
Procurement\ Cost+
Inventory\ Cost+
Other\ Incremental\ Costs
\]

Example:

| Cost component | Option A | Option B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Overtime | ₱30,000 | ₱0 |
| Subcontracting premium | ₱0 | ₱45,000 |
| Expediting | ₱5,000 | ₱0 |
| Additional inventory holding | ₱0 | ₱8,000 |
| **Total** | **₱35,000** | **₱53,000** |

Option A has ₱18,000 lower relevant cost.

However, before selecting it, verify that both options achieve comparable customer fulfillment, quality, and delivery performance.

## 7.7 Net Financial Benefit Between Alternatives

This measures the financial advantage of one alternative over another.

\[
Net\ Financial\ Benefit=
Financial\ Impact\ of\ Alternative\ B-
Financial\ Impact\ of\ Alternative\ A
\]

For cost comparisons:

\[
Net\ Savings=Cost\ of\ A-Cost\ of\ B
\]

Example:

- Option A total relevant cost: ₱80,000.
- Option B total relevant cost: ₱65,000.

\[
80,000-65,000=\boxed{₱15,000}
\]

Option B provides ₱15,000 in savings compared with Option A.

For a broader business comparison, use:

\[
Net\ Benefit=
Incremental\ Benefits-Incremental\ Costs
\]

For example:

- Additional contribution margin: ₱100,000.
- Additional overtime and logistics expense: ₱35,000.

\[
100,000-35,000=\boxed{₱65,000}
\]

The alternative provides ₱65,000 in incremental net financial benefit, before considering any other unmodeled expenses or risks.

# 8. Decision Analysis Methods

Now that we understand the financial and operational metrics, paano natin gagamitin ang mga ito to make an actual business decision?

There are several decision analysis methods used in S&OP.

## 8.1 Cost-Benefit Analysis

Cost-benefit analysis compares the expected financial benefits against the costs of implementing an alternative.

Basic formula:

\[
Net\ Benefit=Total\ Benefits-Total\ Costs
\]

Example:

A company considers purchasing an additional machine.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Additional annual contribution | ₱800,000 |
| Annual operating expenses | ₱200,000 |
| Annual maintenance | ₱50,000 |
| Initial investment | ₱1,500,000 |

Annual net operating benefit:

\[
800,000-200,000-50,000
\]

\[
=₱550,000
\]

Simple payback period:

\[
Payback=\frac{Initial\ Investment}{Annual\ Net\ Benefit}
\]

\[
=\frac{1,500,000}{550,000}
\]

\[
=\boxed{2.73\ years}
\]

**When useful:**
- Evaluating equipment investments.
- Comparing additional production capacity against subcontracting.
- Evaluating inventory build-ahead programs.
- Assessing process improvement investments.

**Limitations:**

- Some benefits are difficult to quantify.
- Future costs and benefits may be uncertain.
- Simple payback does not account for the time value of money.
- It may overlook strategic and operational risks.

For major long-term investments, companies may also use Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), and discounted cash flow analysis.

## 8.2 Weighted Decision Matrix

This method is particularly useful when alternatives must be evaluated using multiple criteria.

Hindi lahat ng factors ay financial.

For example, when choosing between overtime and subcontracting, we might consider:

- Cost.
- Production capacity.
- Delivery speed.
- Quality.
- Operational risk.

But not all criteria have equal importance.

A weighted decision matrix assigns a weight to each criterion.

### Step 1: Establish evaluation criteria and weights

Illustrative example:

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Cost | 35% |
| Delivery performance | 30% |
| Quality | 20% |
| Operational risk | 15% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

### Step 2: Rate each alternative

Use a scale of 1 to 5:

- 1 = Very poor.
- 2 = Poor.
- 3 = Average.
- 4 = Good.
- 5 = Excellent.

Assume the following ratings:

| Criteria | Weight | Overtime | Subcontracting |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cost | 35% | 4 | 2 |
| Delivery | 30% | 4 | 3 |
| Quality | 20% | 5 | 3 |
| Risk | 15% | 3 | 2 |

### Step 3: Calculate weighted scores

Formula:

\[
Weighted\ Score=Weight\times Rating
\]

Overtime:

\[
(0.35\times4)+(0.30\times4)
\]

\[
+(0.20\times5)+(0.15\times3)
\]

\[
=1.40+1.20+1.00+0.45
\]

\[
\boxed{4.05}
\]

Subcontracting:

\[
(0.35\times2)+(0.30\times3)
\]

\[
+(0.20\times3)+(0.15\times2)
\]

\[
=0.70+0.90+0.60+0.30
\]

\[
\boxed{2.50}
\]

**Weighted decision matrix results**

| Alternative | Weighted score |
|---|---:|
| Overtime | **4.05 / 5** |
| Subcontracting | 2.50 / 5 |

**Initial result:** Overtime receives the higher weighted score.

**When useful:**
- Comparing suppliers.
- Selecting capacity solutions.
- Evaluating alternatives with financial and non-financial criteria.
- Aligning departmental priorities.

**Limitations:**

- Weights can be subjective.
- Ratings may depend on individual judgment.
- A high score does not guarantee financial feasibility.
- Different criteria may overlap or double-count the same benefit.

Important: A weighted score should support a business decision, not replace financial analysis or management judgment.

## 8.3 Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how changes in assumptions affect the outcome of a decision.

For example:

What happens if demand increases?

What if overtime costs increase?

What if a supplier cannot deliver on time?

Let's consider an overtime alternative.

| Scenario | Overtime cost/unit | Additional units | Total cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Low-cost case | ₱15 | 1,000 | ₱15,000 |
| Base case | ₱20 | 1,000 | ₱20,000 |
| High-cost case | ₱30 | 1,000 | ₱30,000 |

The same production requirement produces different financial outcomes depending on the overtime cost.

We can also test changes in the number of additional units.

\[
Total\ Overtime\ Cost=
Overtime\ Cost/Unit\times Additional\ Units
\]

For a fixed overtime rate of ₱20:

| Additional units | Total overtime cost |
|---:|---:|
| 500 | ₱10,000 |
| 1,000 | ₱20,000 |
| 1,500 | ₱30,000 |
| 2,000 | ₱40,000 |

**When useful:**
- Demand uncertainty.
- Material price changes.
- Overtime cost fluctuations.
- Supplier lead time changes.
- Inventory carrying cost evaluation.

**Limitations:**

Sensitivity analysis normally changes selected assumptions while holding other assumptions constant. It does not automatically predict which scenario will actually happen.

## 8.4 Risk versus Impact Assessment

Sometimes an alternative has a low financial cost but a high operational risk.

Example:

A supplier offers an attractive price but has a history of delayed deliveries.

We can assess the risk using:

\[
Risk\ Score=Probability\times Impact
\]

For a simple illustrative 1-to-5 rating:

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Risk score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Supplier delay | 4 | 5 | 20 |
| Quality failure | 2 | 5 | 10 |
| Minor scheduling issue | 3 | 2 | 6 |

A score of 20 indicates a higher-priority risk under this assessment method.

However, the scoring thresholds should be established by the company.

**When useful:**
- Supplier selection.
- Subcontracting decisions.
- Production recovery planning.
- Inventory depletion decisions.
- Customer delivery commitments.

**Limitation:** Risk scores simplify uncertainty. A numerical score does not capture every possible consequence or interaction between risks.

## 8.5 Introduction to Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM)

Multi-Criteria Decision-Making is a broader family of methods used to evaluate alternatives against multiple, potentially conflicting objectives.

The weighted decision matrix we discussed is one relatively simple MCDM technique.

Other methods include:

- Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP).
- TOPSIS.
- ELECTRE.
- PROMETHEE.

For now, focus on understanding the principle rather than memorizing advanced methodologies.

In S&OP, MCDM is useful because the best alternative may need to balance:

- Financial performance.
- Customer service.
- Capacity.
- Inventory.
- Risk.
- Strategic importance.

**Professional insight:** The lowest-cost option is not necessarily the best option, and the highest-scoring option is not necessarily financially feasible.

The decision must satisfy the company's non-negotiable constraints first, then optimize the remaining objectives.

# 9. Practical Manufacturing Application

This is the most important part of the lesson.

Let's simulate an actual S&OP situation where several departments need to agree on how to resolve a supply shortage.

## 9.1 Business Situation

A manufacturing company produces electronic components.

During the monthly S&OP review, Sales reports an increase in customer demand.

However, Operations reports that available production capacity is insufficient to fulfill all customer requirements.

The company needs to evaluate several alternatives.

### Demand and supply information

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 10,000 units |
| Regular production capacity | 8,000 units |
| Available excess inventory | 500 units |
| Selling price | ₱160/unit |
| Normal variable manufacturing cost | ₱110/unit |
| Contribution margin | ₱50/unit |
| Maximum additional overtime production | 1,500 units |

Important assumptions:

1. The 500 inventory units are above the required safety stock.
2. Normal production capacity of 8,000 units is already included in the baseline production plan.
3. The maximum additional overtime capacity is 1,500 units.
4. Subcontracting can provide the required additional units.
5. All alternatives meet the same product specifications, assuming supplier quality approval.
6. All incremental costs are evaluated over the same planning period.

### Step 1: Identify the supply-demand gap

\[
Supply\ Gap=Demand-Available\ Supply
\]

Available supply:

\[
8,000+500=8,500
\]

Supply-demand gap:

\[
10,000-8,500
\]

\[
\boxed{1,500\ units}
\]

Wait, let's distinguish two different quantities:

- The gap after regular production alone is 2,000 units.
- The gap after using available excess inventory is 1,500 units.

This distinction matters because inventory is already available to support fulfillment.

**Demand and supply position**

| Supply component | Units |
|---|---:|
| Regular production | 8,000 |
| Excess inventory | 500 |
| Remaining gap | 1,500 |
| **Total demand** | **10,000** |

## 9.2 Establish the Alternatives

We will evaluate five possible solutions.

| Option | Proposed action |
|---|---|
| A | Produce 1,500 additional units through overtime and reschedule 500 units. |
| B | Subcontract all 2,000 units needed beyond regular production. |
| C | Use 500 excess inventory units and produce 1,500 units through overtime. |
| D | Produce 1,200 units through overtime and reschedule 800 units. |
| E | Use 500 excess inventory units, produce 1,000 through overtime, and subcontract 500 units. |

Let's establish the additional cost assumptions.

| Cost assumption | Value |
|---|---:|
| Overtime premium | ₱18/additional unit |
| Subcontracting premium | ₱30/unit |
| Late-delivery penalty | ₱10/rescheduled unit |

These are illustrative incremental costs, not universal industry rates.

For this example, the late-delivery penalty is assumed to apply to every rescheduled unit.

## 9.3 Calculate Each Alternative

### Option A: Overtime + Demand Rescheduling

Production:

- Overtime: 1,500 units.
- Rescheduled demand: 500 units.

Overtime cost:

\[
1,500\times18=₱27,000
\]

Late-delivery penalty:

\[
500\times10=₱5,000
\]

Total relevant cost:

\[
27,000+5,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱32,000}
\]

Customer fulfillment by original due date:

\[
\frac{9,500}{10,000}\times100
=\boxed{95\%}
\]

### Option B: Full Subcontracting

The company produces the regular 8,000 units internally and outsources the remaining 2,000 units.

Subcontracting premium:

\[
2,000\times30
\]

\[
\boxed{₱60,000}
\]

On-time fulfillment:

\[
\boxed{100\%}
\]

### Option C: Inventory Utilization + Overtime

The company uses 500 units of available excess inventory and produces 1,500 additional units through overtime.

Overtime cost:

\[
1,500\times18
\]

\[
\boxed{₱27,000}
\]

On-time fulfillment:

\[
\boxed{100\%}
\]

Inventory remaining:

\[
500-500=0
\]

This uses all available excess inventory, but it does not consume the designated safety stock.

### Option D: Overtime + Demand Rescheduling

The company produces 1,200 additional units through overtime and delays 800 units.

Overtime cost:

\[
1,200\times18=₱21,600
\]

Late-delivery penalty:

\[
800\times10=₱8,000
\]

Total relevant cost:

\[
21,600+8,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱29,600}
\]

On-time fulfillment:

\[
\frac{9,200}{10,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{92\%}
\]

### Option E: Inventory + Overtime + Subcontracting

The company:

- Uses 500 inventory units.
- Produces 1,000 additional units through overtime.
- Subcontracts 500 units.

Overtime cost:

\[
1,000\times18=₱18,000
\]

Subcontracting premium:

\[
500\times30=₱15,000
\]

Total relevant cost:

\[
18,000+15,000
\]

\[
\boxed{₱33,000}
\]

On-time fulfillment:

\[
\boxed{100\%}
\]

Inventory remaining:

\[
\boxed{0\ units}
\]

## 9.4 Compare the Alternatives

Now let's consolidate the results.

| Metric | A | B | C | D | E |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Overtime units | 1,500 | 0 | 1,500 | 1,200 | 1,000 |
| Subcontract units | 0 | 2,000 | 0 | 0 | 500 |
| Inventory used | 0 | 0 | 500 | 0 | 500 |
| Delayed units | 500 | 0 | 0 | 800 | 0 |
| Incremental cost | ₱32k | ₱60k | ₱27k | ₱29.6k | ₱33k |
| On-time fulfillment | 95% | 100% | 100% | 92% | 100% |

| Option | Cost |
|---|---:|
| A | 32.0 |
| B | 60.0 |
| C | 27.0 |
| D | 29.6 |
| E | 33.0 |

### Initial Financial Interpretation

Option C has the lowest incremental cost at ₱27,000 while achieving 100% on-time fulfillment.

Option B has the highest cost at ₱60,000.

Option D is relatively inexpensive, but only 92% of demand is fulfilled on time.

Option E provides full fulfillment but costs more than Option C.

**Based purely on the stated incremental costs and service outcomes, Option C is initially the most attractive.**

But we are not finished.

A professional S&OP analyst should also examine the consequences of each alternative.

## 9.5 Examine the Financial Impact

Let's calculate the contribution margin and potential financial exposure.

Selling price:

\[
₱160/unit
\]

Variable cost:

\[
₱110/unit
\]

Contribution margin:

\[
160-110=₱50/unit
\]

Total potential revenue:

\[
10,000\times160=₱1,600,000
\]

Potential contribution margin:

\[
10,000\times50=₱500,000
\]

For Option D, 800 units are delayed.

Revenue associated with delayed units:

\[
800\times160=₱128,000
\]

Contribution margin associated with delayed units:

\[
800\times50=₱40,000
\]

However, because these orders are assumed to be delayed rather than canceled, the ₱128,000 is deferred revenue exposure, not necessarily lost revenue.

This is an important distinction in financial reporting and decision-making.

## 9.6 How Does the Preferred Alternative Change?

This is where actual trade-off analysis becomes interesting.

Different business priorities can lead to different decisions.

### Priority 1: Minimize incremental cost

**Preferred: Option C**

- Cost: ₱27,000.
- On-time fulfillment: 100%.
- Uses 500 units of excess inventory.
- Requires maximum available overtime.

Best when the company is comfortable consuming its excess inventory buffer.

### Priority 2: Preserve inventory

**Alternative: Option A**

- Cost: ₱32,000.
- On-time fulfillment: 95%.
- Does not consume excess inventory.
- Requires 500 units to be rescheduled.

Best when preserving inventory for uncertain future demand is more important than fulfilling every order immediately.

### Priority 3: Protect customer commitments

**Preferred: Option C**

- Cost: ₱27,000.
- On-time fulfillment: 100%.
- No rescheduled orders.

Best when inventory consumption is acceptable and internal production capacity is available.

### Priority 4: Reduce internal overtime

**Alternative: Option E**

- Cost: ₱33,000.
- On-time fulfillment: 100%.
- Overtime requirement reduced to 1,000 units.
- Requires external subcontracting.

Best when employee fatigue, equipment utilization, or internal capacity constraints make maximum overtime undesirable.

Notice what happened?

The preferred option changed depending on the business objective.

That is precisely why trade-off analysis is important.

## 9.7 Introduce Inventory Risk into the Decision

Let's assume the next month's demand forecast is uncertain.

The company expects another possible increase in demand, and the 500 units of excess inventory could provide protection.

If Option C consumes all 500 units, the company loses that additional inventory buffer.

This creates an opportunity cost that is not automatically reflected in the ₱27,000 incremental cost.

The analyst should investigate:

- How likely is another demand increase?
- How long does replenishment take?
- Can the supplier replenish the inventory before the next demand peak?
- What is the financial consequence of a future stockout?
- Is the existing safety stock sufficient without the excess inventory?

If future demand risk is significant, preserving some inventory may be more valuable than achieving the lowest immediate cost.

This is an example of why short-term cost comparisons should not be isolated from the broader S&OP horizon.

## 9.8 What Should the Company Actually Choose?

Based on the illustrative assumptions, Option C is the strongest initial recommendation.

However, I would not recommend approving it automatically.

Before approval, I would validate:

1. Whether the 500 units are genuinely available above safety stock.
2. Whether the maximum overtime capacity of 1,500 units is achievable.
3. Whether production quality will remain stable under extended operating hours.
4. Whether using the inventory creates a shortage risk in the next planning period.
5. Whether the required overtime can be completed within the customer delivery schedule.

If these conditions are satisfied, Option C provides full customer fulfillment at the lowest incremental cost among the five alternatives.

If inventory preservation or employee capacity becomes a higher priority, Option E or Option A may become more appropriate.

**The best decision is not simply the cheapest alternative. It is the alternative that provides the best overall business outcome while respecting operational constraints.**

# 10. Trade-off Analysis in S&OP Decisions

Now let's connect everything to the actual responsibilities of an S&OP analyst.

The analyst is not simply expected to calculate costs. The analyst must translate operational information into business implications that management can understand and act upon.

## 10.1 How an S&OP Analyst Evaluates Trade-offs

A typical workflow looks like this:

| Step | Analyst's responsibility | Expected output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify the gap | Compare demand against supply | Demand-supply gap |
| 2. Investigate constraints | Review capacity, inventory, materials, and lead times | Identified constraints |
| 3. Develop alternatives | Identify feasible solutions | Alternative scenarios |
| 4. Quantify impacts | Calculate costs, capacity, inventory, and fulfillment | Financial and operational comparison |
| 5. Assess risks | Evaluate quality, supplier, and delivery risks | Risk assessment |
| 6. Compare alternatives | Evaluate benefits and disadvantages | Trade-off analysis |
| 7. Recommend an option | Identify the preferred business solution | Management recommendation |

One important responsibility is to challenge assumptions.

For example:

If Operations says it can produce an additional 2,000 units through overtime, the analyst should ask:

- Is the required material available?
- Is machine capacity sufficient?
- Is additional manpower required?
- Is the production schedule achievable?
- Will quality performance be affected?

A proposed alternative is not feasible simply because its financial calculation looks attractive.

## 10.2 Presenting Trade-offs During Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where the cross-functional team reviews the demand and supply plans, identifies unresolved gaps, and evaluates potential solutions before executive decision-making.

The analyst should present:

**A. Business problem**

Example: Forecast demand exceeds available supply by 2,000 units.

**B. Available alternatives**

- Overtime.
- Subcontracting.
- Inventory utilization.
- Demand rescheduling.
- Combination of solutions.

**C. Quantified impacts**

Present costs, capacity, inventory, customer fulfillment, and financial implications.

**D. Risks and constraints**

Identify what could prevent each alternative from succeeding.

**E. Recommended alternative**

Explain which option provides the best overall result and why.

A useful Pre-S&OP comparison table:

| Evaluation | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Incremental cost | ₱32,000 | ₱60,000 | ₱27,000 |
| On-time fulfillment | 95% | 100% | 100% |
| Inventory consumption | 0 | 0 | 500 |
| Operational risk | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Overall assessment | Moderate | Expensive | Preferred initially |

The objective is not to overwhelm management with calculations.

It is to make the consequences of each alternative visible.

## 10.3 Roles of Different Departments

Trade-off analysis requires cross-functional participation.

| Department | Contribution to trade-off analysis |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer priorities, demand changes, delivery commitments |
| Operations | Production capacity, overtime availability, production constraints |
| Supply Chain | Inventory availability, replenishment, logistics, supply continuity |
| Procurement | Supplier quotations, lead times, subcontracting options |
| Finance | Cost validation, contribution margin, working capital, profitability |
| S&OP Analyst | Consolidates information, quantifies alternatives, evaluates trade-offs |

### Example: Departmental disagreement

Suppose:

- Sales wants 100% fulfillment.
- Operations prefers overtime.
- Procurement prefers subcontracting because internal capacity is constrained.
- Finance wants the lowest incremental cost.
- Supply Chain wants to preserve inventory.

The S&OP analyst should not simply support whichever department has the strongest argument.

Instead, the analyst provides a common quantitative comparison.

That allows the team to discuss the actual business consequences rather than defend departmental preferences.

## 10.4 How to Present a Recommendation Using Quantitative Evidence

A good recommendation should answer five questions:

1. What is the business problem?
2. What alternatives were evaluated?
3. What are the financial and operational impacts?
4. What risks and assumptions were considered?
5. Why is the recommended option better?

For our manufacturing case, a professional recommendation could be structured as follows:

### Illustrative Pre-S&OP Recommendation

**Business issue**

Customer demand of 10,000 units exceeds regular production capacity of 8,000 units, resulting in a 2,000-unit supply gap.

**Recommended action**

Utilize 500 units of excess inventory and produce an additional 1,500 units through overtime.

**Financial impact**

The proposed solution requires ₱27,000 in incremental overtime expenses, which is lower than the other evaluated alternatives.

**Operational impact**

The solution achieves 100% on-time customer fulfillment while maintaining the designated safety stock.

**Key risk**

Consuming the excess inventory reduces the available buffer for unexpected demand increases in the succeeding planning period.

**Required validation**

Confirm overtime feasibility, material availability, production quality, and the next-period inventory replenishment plan.

**Recommendation**

Proceed with the proposed inventory utilization and overtime strategy, subject to operational feasibility confirmation and inventory risk assessment.

This is the type of analysis that transforms raw operational data into a business decision.

# 11. Important Concepts to Remember

Before moving to the exercises, let's consolidate the professional concepts.

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Trade-off analysis | Evaluates competing alternatives and their compromises. |
| Incremental cost | Additional expense compared with a baseline. |
| Opportunity cost | Value sacrificed by selecting one alternative over another. |
| Contribution margin | Revenue less variable costs. |
| Revenue at risk | Potential revenue associated with unfulfilled demand. |
| Relevant cost | Cost that changes depending on the decision. |
| Inventory carrying cost | Cost associated with holding inventory. |
| Working capital | Funds tied up in day-to-day business operations. |
| Weighted decision matrix | Compares alternatives using weighted criteria. |
| Sensitivity analysis | Tests how changing assumptions affects results. |
| Risk assessment | Evaluates potential events and their consequences. |
| Multi-criteria decision-making | Evaluates alternatives against multiple objectives. |

## 11.1 Five Critical Lessons

1. **The cheapest alternative is not always the best alternative.** Consider customer service, capacity, quality, and future demand.
2. **Revenue is not profit.** Always distinguish revenue, contribution margin, and operating profit.
3. **Inventory has an opportunity cost.** Using available inventory today may affect future supply flexibility.
4. **Financial and operational feasibility must be evaluated together.** A financially attractive alternative may not be operationally achievable.
5. **Trade-off analysis supports decisions, not automatic decisions.** Management must consider business priorities, risk tolerance, and strategic objectives.
