# Module 6 – Topic 6.5: Scenario Planning

**Main learning objective:** Learn how to prepare for different possible business situations by developing alternative demand, supply, production, inventory, and financial plans.

## Topic Overview

### 1. What is Scenario Planning?

Imagine that you are an S&OP Analyst in a manufacturing company.

Your company expects to receive customer orders worth 10,000 units next month. Based on the current production plan, available inventory, and supplier commitments, the company believes it can fulfill those orders.

Everything looks good.

But what if something changes?

- What if customer demand suddenly increases by 20%?
- What if demand decreases by 15%?
- What if a major supplier delays raw material deliveries by two weeks?
- What if one production line experiences unexpected downtime?
- What if material prices increase by 10%?
- What if demand increases at the same time that material availability decreases?

Your original plan might no longer work.

**This is where Scenario Planning becomes important.**

Scenario Planning is the process of developing and evaluating multiple possible business situations to understand their potential impacts and prepare appropriate response plans.

In simple terms:

> Instead of asking, "What do we expect to happen?", Scenario Planning also asks, "What if something different happens?"

The objective is not to predict the future perfectly. It is to make sure the company is prepared to respond when actual business conditions differ from expectations.

### Why is this important in S&OP?

S&OP decisions are interconnected.

For example:

1. Higher demand increases the required production volume.
2. Higher production increases raw material consumption.
3. Increased material consumption may create shortages.
4. Material shortages may limit production output.
5. Lower production output may affect customer fulfillment.
6. Expediting materials or transportation may increase operating costs.

A seemingly small change in one business variable can create several consequences throughout the supply chain.

Scenario Planning helps management understand these relationships before committing to a business decision.

## 2. Layman's Explanation

### 2.1 Scenario Planning using an everyday example

Imagine that you are planning a road trip.

Your original plan is:

- Travel on Saturday.
- Budget: ₱5,000.
- Expected travel time: 4 hours.
- Fuel requirement: 20 liters.

You have a base plan.

However, you also consider other possibilities.

| Situation | What might happen? | Your preparation |
|---|---|---|
| Base case | Normal traffic | Follow the original schedule. |
| Best case | Light traffic | Arrive earlier. |
| Worst case | Heavy traffic and rain | Prepare extra fuel, money, and travel time. |

Notice something important.

You are not saying that heavy traffic will definitely happen.

You are simply preparing for the possibility.

This is exactly how manufacturing companies use Scenario Planning.

They establish an expected business plan, then develop alternative plans for different possible situations.

### 2.2 Forecasting vs. Scenario Planning vs. Contingency Planning

These three concepts are related, but they serve different purposes.

| Concept | Main question | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Forecasting | What do we expect to happen? | Estimate future demand or business conditions. |
| Scenario Planning | What could happen under different conditions? | Evaluate alternative future situations. |
| Contingency Planning | What will we do if a particular situation happens? | Prepare specific response actions. |

Let's use a manufacturing example.

A company forecasts 10,000 units of customer demand next month.

**Forecasting:**

Expected demand = 10,000 units.

**Scenario Planning:**

- Base case: 10,000 units.
- Upside case: 12,000 units.
- Downside case: 8,500 units.

**Contingency Planning:**

If demand reaches 12,000 units and production capacity is insufficient:

- Add overtime.
- Activate an alternative production line.
- Expedite critical raw materials.
- Reallocate available inventory.

The distinction is important:

- Forecasting establishes expectations.
- Scenario Planning evaluates possibilities.
- Contingency Planning prepares actions.

They work together, rather than replacing one another.

## 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the professional concepts used in actual S&OP operations.

### 3.1 Types of Business Scenarios

Companies develop scenarios based on different assumptions about future business conditions.

The most common starting point is the three-scenario framework.

### Base Case

**Normal conditions**

The most reasonable planning assumption based on available information, current forecasts, and known business conditions.

Example: Demand remains at 10,000 units and suppliers deliver as committed.

### Upside Case

**Better-than-expected conditions**

Business performs better than the base-case assumptions.

Example: Demand increases by 20%, customer orders accelerate, or additional capacity becomes available.

### Downside Case

**Worse-than-expected conditions**

Business conditions deteriorate and create operational or financial risks.

Example: Demand declines, a supplier delays delivery, or production experiences unexpected downtime.

Important: Best-case and upside-case are often used interchangeably. The same applies to worst-case and downside-case. However, companies may define them differently depending on their planning methodology.

The three-scenario framework is a starting point, not a requirement to limit analysis to exactly three scenarios.

### 3.2 Other Types of Business Scenarios

Beyond the three general categories, companies can develop scenarios around specific business drivers.

| Scenario type | Possible business situation | Potential consequence |
|---|---|---|
| High demand | Orders increase by 20%. | Capacity shortage |
| Low demand | Orders decrease by 15%. | Excess inventory |
| Supplier disruption | Supplier delays delivery by 2 weeks. | Material shortage |
| Capacity shortage | Production capacity decreases by 15%. | Lower production output |
| Production downtime | Machine stops for 3 days. | Lost production |
| Material cost increase | Raw material prices increase by 10%. | Higher manufacturing costs |
| Transportation disruption | Shipping costs increase by 25%. | Higher logistics expenses |
| Combined disruption | Demand increases while material supply decreases. | Severe supply-demand imbalance |

Let's understand some of these more deeply.

**A. Demand scenarios**

These focus on changes in customer requirements.

Examples:

- A customer launches a new product, increasing demand.
- A customer reduces its purchasing volume.
- A major customer postpones orders.
- A seasonal demand peak occurs earlier than expected.

**B. Supply scenarios**

These focus on the company's ability to obtain materials or products.

Examples:

- Supplier delivery is delayed.
- Supplier allocation is reduced.
- Raw materials become unavailable.
- Imported materials experience customs delays.

**C. Capacity scenarios**

These focus on the company's ability to manufacture products.

Examples:

- A production machine experiences downtime.
- Available manpower decreases.
- A production line is under maintenance.
- Equipment efficiency decreases.

**D. Financial scenarios**

These focus on changes in the financial assumptions supporting the plan.

Examples:

- Raw material prices increase.
- Transportation costs increase.
- Currency exchange rates change.
- Production overtime increases labor expenses.

**E. Combined scenarios**

These are particularly important because real business problems do not always happen independently.

For example:

A manufacturing company experiences:

1. Customer demand increases by 20%.
2. A major supplier delivers only 70% of its committed materials.
3. Production capacity remains unchanged.

Even if the factory has sufficient machine capacity, it may not have sufficient materials to manufacture the required quantity.

This is why S&OP scenario analysis must consider relationships between demand, supply, capacity, and financial conditions.

### 3.3 Scenario Planning vs. Sensitivity Analysis

You may encounter another term: **Sensitivity Analysis**.

These concepts are related but different.

| Scenario Planning | Sensitivity Analysis |
|---|---|
| Evaluates alternative combinations of business conditions. | Measures how changes in individual assumptions affect results. |
| Can change several variables simultaneously. | Often changes one variable while holding others constant. |
| Produces alternative business plans. | Measures the degree of impact from a variable. |

Example:

A company wants to understand the impact of changing demand.

Sensitivity analysis:

- Demand at 9,000 units.
- Demand at 10,000 units.
- Demand at 11,000 units.

Only demand changes. Other assumptions remain constant.

Scenario planning:

- Demand increases by 10%.
- Supplier lead time increases by 2 weeks.
- Material costs increase by 5%.

Multiple variables may change together to represent a realistic business situation.

**Remember:** Sensitivity analysis can be used as an analytical tool within Scenario Planning.

## 4. Detailed Discussion: Scenario Development Process

This is the actual process an S&OP Analyst can follow when developing scenarios.

### Step 1: Establish the Baseline or Base-Case Plan

First, establish what the business currently expects to happen.

The baseline should contain:

- Demand forecast.
- Beginning inventory.
- Planned production.
- Available production capacity.
- Raw material availability.
- Supplier commitments.
- Expected revenue.
- Manufacturing and procurement costs.

Example:

A manufacturing company expects the following next month:

| Business variable | Baseline assumption |
|---|---:|
| Demand forecast | 9,000 units |
| Beginning inventory | 1,500 units |
| Planned production | 8,000 units |
| Available capacity | 9,000 units |
| Supplier material support | 8,000 units |
| Selling price | ₱100/unit |
| Variable manufacturing cost | ₱60/unit |

These values form the starting point for scenario development.

The base case should be internally consistent. For example, planned production must be supported by available materials and production capacity.

### Step 2: Identify Critical Assumptions and Business Drivers

A business driver is a variable that can significantly influence business performance.

Examples:

| Business driver | Possible change |
|---|---|
| Customer demand | +20% |
| Production capacity | -15% |
| Supplier delivery | 2-week delay |
| Material cost | +10% |
| Transportation cost | +25% |
| Machine availability | 90% uptime |

Not every variable deserves scenario analysis.

An S&OP Analyst should prioritize variables that can materially affect:

- Customer fulfillment.
- Inventory.
- Production.
- Revenue.
- Profitability.
- Working capital.

For example, a 1% increase in packaging cost might have little effect on overall profitability, while a 30% reduction in critical raw material availability could significantly affect customer service.

### Step 3: Define the Possible Changes

Now establish the alternative assumptions.

For illustration:

| Variable | Base case | Upside case | Downside case |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Demand | 9,000 | 10,800 | 7,650 |
| Production capacity | 9,000 | 9,000 | 9,000 |
| Material-supported production | 8,000 | 8,000 | 6,000 |
| Material cost/unit | ₱60 | ₱60 | ₱63.50 |

Notice that:

- Upside demand increases by 20%.
- Downside demand decreases by 15%.
- Downside material availability limits production to 6,000 units.
- Downside unit manufacturing cost increases.

These are assumptions, not calculated results.

### Step 4: Translate Assumptions into Operational Impacts

This is where calculations become important.

For each scenario, determine:

1. How much can we produce?
2. How much supply will be available?
3. How much demand can we fulfill?
4. How much inventory will remain?
5. Is there a supply gap?
6. Will we need overtime or additional procurement?

### Step 5: Translate Operational Impacts into Financial Impacts

Operational changes eventually affect financial performance.

For example:

- Lower production can reduce sales fulfillment.
- Higher material prices increase manufacturing costs.
- Excess production can increase inventory carrying costs.
- Overtime increases labor expenses.
- Expedited procurement increases purchasing and transportation costs.

The goal is to understand both operational feasibility and financial consequences.

### Step 6: Develop Alternative Plans and Response Actions

Finally, determine what the business should do under each scenario.

| Scenario | Potential response |
|---|---|
| Base case | Maintain the approved production plan. |
| Upside case | Evaluate overtime, material expediting, and production allocation. |
| Downside case | Adjust production, control procurement, and protect critical customer orders. |

One important principle:

**A scenario without an associated response plan is only an analysis.**

A useful S&OP scenario should help management understand what action may be required.

## 5. Scenario Variables and Business Impact

Let's examine the major variables that an S&OP Analyst should monitor.

| Variable | What changes? | What should we evaluate? |
|---|---|---|
| Demand volume | Number of units customers require | Demand-supply gap |
| Product mix | Distribution of demand across products | Capacity and material requirements |
| Production capacity | Available manufacturing output | Production feasibility |
| Capacity utilization | Percentage of capacity being used | Available production headroom |
| Material availability | Quantity of materials available | Material shortages |
| Supplier lead time | Time required for replenishment | Inventory coverage |
| Inventory | Available finished goods and raw materials | Stockout and excess inventory risk |
| Production cost | Cost of manufacturing | Profitability |
| Procurement cost | Cost of acquiring materials | Cost exposure |
| Revenue | Sales generated | Revenue opportunity or loss |
| Working capital | Money tied up in inventory and other operating assets | Cash availability |

### 5.1 Demand Volume and Product Mix

Demand volume measures how many units customers require.

Product mix describes the distribution of demand across different products.

Example:

| Product | Base demand | Upside demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 | 7,000 |
| Product B | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| Product C | 1,000 | 800 |
| **Total** | **9,000** | **10,800** |

Notice that the total demand increases by 20%, but Product A experiences a 40% increase.

This matters because products may have different:

- Production cycle times.
- Material requirements.
- Machine assignments.
- Labor requirements.

A company might have sufficient total capacity but insufficient capacity for a particular product.

This is an important distinction between volume planning and product-mix planning.

### 5.2 Production Capacity and Utilization

Production capacity measures the maximum output that can be produced under defined operating assumptions.

Capacity utilization measures how much of that capacity is being used.

For example:

- Available capacity: 10,000 units.
- Planned production: 8,000 units.

Utilization is 80%.

The remaining 20% represents unused capacity under the same assumptions.

However, unused capacity does not automatically mean the company can produce any additional product. Material availability, machine compatibility, labor, and scheduling constraints must also be considered.

### 5.3 Material Availability and Supplier Lead Time

A production plan is only feasible when the required materials are available at the right time.

Suppose:

- Required raw material: 5,000 kg.
- Available raw material: 4,000 kg.
- Additional supplier delivery: delayed by 2 weeks.

The company has a material shortage of 1,000 kg.

Even if machine capacity is sufficient, the company cannot manufacture the full planned volume.

This illustrates why capacity and material availability must be evaluated together.

### 5.4 Inventory and Customer Service

Different scenarios create different inventory conditions.

| Scenario | Potential inventory outcome |
|---|---|
| Higher demand | Inventory depletion |
| Lower demand | Excess inventory |
| Supplier delay | Raw material shortage |
| Production downtime | Lower finished goods inventory |
| Demand cancellation | Unplanned finished goods accumulation |

Inventory acts as a buffer between demand and supply, but that buffer is limited.

Scenario Planning helps determine whether the existing inventory can protect customer fulfillment under alternative business conditions.

### 5.5 Financial Impact

The same operational scenario can produce different financial consequences.

For example:

A higher-demand scenario may increase revenue, but it can also require:

- Overtime.
- Premium freight.
- Additional procurement.
- Higher inventory investment.

Therefore, increased revenue does not automatically mean increased profitability.

This becomes especially important in Topic 6.6, where we will study how to evaluate competing business decisions.

## 6. Formulas and Calculations

Now let's establish the mathematical foundation.

We will use a consistent set of formulas throughout the practical manufacturing example.

### 6.1 Demand Change Under Different Scenarios

**Formula:**

\[
D_s=D_b(1+r)
\]

Where:

- \(D_s\) = Scenario demand (units)
- \(D_b\) = Base demand (units)
- \(r\) = Demand change rate (decimal)

Example:

Base demand = 9,000 units.

Upside demand increases by 20%.

\[
D_s=9,000(1+0.20)
\]

\[
\boxed{D_s=10,800\ units}
\]

For a downside scenario with a 15% reduction:

\[
D_s=9,000(1-0.15)
\]

\[
\boxed{D_s=7,650\ units}
\]

**Interpretation:** The company should prepare for demand ranging from 7,650 to 10,800 units under these illustrative assumptions.

### 6.2 Available Supply Under Capacity Constraints

Production cannot exceed the lowest relevant operational constraint.

A simplified formula is:

\[
P=\min(C,M,L)
\]

Where:

- \(P\) = Actual feasible production (units)
- \(C\) = Available production capacity (units)
- \(M\) = Material-supported production (units)
- \(L\) = Other operational limits, expressed as equivalent production units

Example:

| Constraint | Available production |
|---|---:|
| Machine capacity | 9,000 units |
| Material-supported production | 8,000 units |
| Labor-supported production | 8,500 units |

\[
P=\min(9,000,8,000,8,500)
\]

\[
\boxed{P=8,000\ units}
\]

Material availability is the limiting constraint.

Available supply:

\[
S=I_b+P
\]

Where:

- \(S\) = Available supply (units)
- \(I_b\) = Beginning finished goods inventory (units)
- \(P\) = Feasible production (units)

If beginning inventory is 1,500 units:

\[
S=1,500+8,000
\]

\[
\boxed{S=9,500\ units}
\]

For a simplified monthly planning model, this assumes no other receipts, transfers, or losses.

### 6.3 Projected Ending Inventory

\[
I_e=I_b+P-F
\]

Where:

- \(I_e\) = Ending inventory (units)
- \(I_b\) = Beginning inventory (units)
- \(P\) = Production (units)
- \(F\) = Customer fulfillment or shipments (units)

Example:

Beginning inventory = 1,500.

Production = 8,000.

Customer shipments = 9,000.

\[
I_e=1,500+8,000-9,000
\]

\[
\boxed{I_e=500\ units}
\]

The company finishes the month with 500 units.

If demand exceeds available supply, customer shipments must be limited to available supply unless additional supply is obtained.

### 6.4 Supply-Demand Gap

A supply-demand gap measures the difference between required demand and available supply.

\[
Gap=D_s-S
\]

Where:

- \(D_s\) = Scenario demand (units)
- \(S\) = Available supply (units)

Example:

Demand = 10,800 units.

Available supply = 9,500 units.

\[
Gap=10,800-9,500
\]

\[
\boxed{Gap=1,300\ units}
\]

A positive gap indicates a shortage.

A negative gap indicates that supply exceeds demand.

For reporting, it is also useful to separate the shortage from the surplus:

\[
Shortage=\max(0,D_s-S)
\]

\[
Surplus=\max(0,S-D_s)
\]

### 6.5 Capacity Utilization

\[
CU=\frac{P}{C}\times100
\]

Where:

- \(CU\) = Capacity utilization (%)
- \(P\) = Actual or planned production (units)
- \(C\) = Available capacity (units)

Example:

Production = 8,000 units.

Available capacity = 9,000 units.

\[
CU=\frac{8,000}{9,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CU=88.89\%}
\]

The remaining theoretical capacity is:

\[
9,000-8,000=1,000\ units
\]

However, remember that this remaining capacity is only usable if other constraints, particularly materials and labor, permit it.

### 6.6 Revenue Impact

\[
Revenue=F\times SP
\]

Where:

- \(F\) = Fulfilled units (units)
- \(SP\) = Selling price (₱/unit)

Example:

9,000 fulfilled units at ₱100 each:

\[
Revenue=9,000\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{Revenue=₱900,000}
\]

If demand increases but the company cannot fulfill all orders, actual revenue may be lower than potential revenue.

### 6.7 Manufacturing Cost

For simplified variable manufacturing cost:

\[
VC=F\times UC
\]

Where:

- \(VC\) = Variable cost of fulfilled units (₱)
- \(F\) = Fulfilled units
- \(UC\) = Variable manufacturing cost per unit (₱/unit)

Example:

\[
VC=9,000\times60
\]

\[
\boxed{VC=₱540,000}
\]

For this exercise, assume the unit cost is constant for each scenario, and inventory is valued consistently. Actual financial reporting may require more detailed production-cost and inventory-accounting treatment.

### 6.8 Profitability Comparison

A simplified contribution-based operating profit formula:

\[
OP=Revenue-VC-FC
\]

Where:

- \(OP\) = Simplified operating profit (₱)
- \(Revenue\) = Sales revenue (₱)
- \(VC\) = Variable cost of fulfilled units (₱)
- \(FC\) = Fixed operating costs (₱)

Example:

Revenue = ₱900,000.

Variable cost = ₱540,000.

Fixed cost = ₱100,000.

\[
OP=900,000-540,000-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{OP=₱260,000}
\]

This is a simplified planning measure, not a complete accounting income statement. It excludes items such as taxes, depreciation, and separate inventory adjustments.

### 6.9 Percentage Change Between Scenarios

This formula allows us to compare an alternative scenario against the base case.

\[
\%\ Change=\frac{Alternative-Base}{Base}\times100
\]

Example:

Base demand = 9,000 units.

Upside demand = 10,800 units.

\[
\%\ Change=\frac{10,800-9,000}{9,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{20\%}
\]

The upside scenario represents a 20% increase in demand.

### 6.10 Keeping Assumptions Consistent

This is one of the most important principles in scenario analysis.

When comparing scenarios, you must clearly identify which assumptions change and which remain constant.

For example:

| Variable | Base | Upside | Downside |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Selling price | ₱100 | ₱100 | ₱100 |
| Fixed cost | ₱100,000 | ₱100,000 | ₱100,000 |
| Beginning inventory | 1,500 | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Demand | 9,000 | 10,800 | 7,650 |
| Material-supported production | 8,000 | 8,000 | 6,000 |
| Unit cost | ₱60 | ₱60 | ₱63.50 |

We are intentionally holding selling price, fixed cost, and beginning inventory constant.

Demand, production feasibility, and unit manufacturing cost change according to the scenario assumptions.

If you change too many unrelated variables without documenting them, you will not know which assumptions caused the financial results to change.

**Professional practice:** Maintain a scenario assumption table, record the source and rationale for each assumption, and identify the planning horizon and units of measurement.

## 7. Practical Manufacturing Application

Let's now apply everything in a realistic manufacturing situation.

For this example, imagine that you are an S&OP Analyst in a company manufacturing electronic components.

The company needs to prepare its production and supply plan for December.

### 7.1 Establish the Base-Case Business Conditions

The company has the following information:

**December planning baseline**

| Variable | Value |
|---|---:|
| Demand forecast | 9,000 units |
| Beginning inventory | 1,500 units |
| Production capacity | 9,000 units/month |
| Material-supported production | 8,000 units |

**Financial assumptions**

| Variable | Value |
|---|---:|
| Selling price | ₱100/unit |
| Variable manufacturing cost | ₱60/unit |
| Fixed operating costs | ₱100,000 |

Additional assumptions:

- All finished goods inventory is available for customer shipment.
- All products are treated as one standard product for this example.
- Production and shipments occur within the same monthly planning period.
- No other inventory receipts, transfers, or losses occur.
- Production cannot exceed either machine capacity or material-supported production.
- All figures are illustrative, not industry standards.

### 7.2 Scenario A: Base Case

**Business assumption:** Expected business conditions remain unchanged.

- Demand: 9,000 units.
- Production capacity: 9,000 units.
- Material-supported production: 8,000 units.
- Beginning inventory: 1,500 units.

**Step 1: Calculate feasible production.**

\[
P=\min(9,000,8,000)=8,000
\]

**Step 2: Calculate available supply.**

\[
S=1,500+8,000=9,500
\]

**Step 3: Calculate customer fulfillment.**

\[
F=\min(9,000,9,500)=9,000
\]

**Step 4: Calculate ending inventory.**

\[
I_e=1,500+8,000-9,000
\]

\[
\boxed{I_e=500\ units}
\]

**Step 5: Calculate supply gap.**

\[
Gap=9,000-9,500=-500
\]

There is no shortage. Instead, the company has a 500-unit supply surplus relative to demand.

**Step 6: Calculate capacity utilization.**

\[
CU=\frac{8,000}{9,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CU=88.89\%}
\]

**Step 7: Calculate financial performance.**

Revenue:

\[
9,000\times ₱100=₱900,000
\]

Variable cost:

\[
9,000\times ₱60=₱540,000
\]

Simplified operating profit:

\[
900,000-540,000-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{OP=₱260,000}
\]

**Base case result**

The company can fulfill all expected customer demand, maintain 500 units of ending inventory, and generate ₱260,000 in simplified operating profit.

**Recommended action:** Maintain the production plan and monitor material deliveries and customer orders.

### 7.3 Scenario B: Upside Case

**Business assumption:** Customer demand increases by 20% because of stronger market demand and additional customer orders.

Demand:

\[
D=9,000(1+0.20)
\]

\[
\boxed{D=10,800\ units}
\]

Other conditions remain unchanged.

**Step 1: Calculate feasible production.**

\[
P=\min(9,000,8,000)=8,000
\]

**Step 2: Calculate available supply.**

\[
S=1,500+8,000=9,500
\]

**Step 3: Calculate supply-demand gap.**

\[
Gap=10,800-9,500
\]

\[
\boxed{Gap=1,300\ units}
\]

**Step 4: Calculate customer fulfillment.**

\[
F=\min(10,800,9,500)=9,500
\]

**Step 5: Calculate ending inventory.**

\[
I_e=1,500+8,000-9,500
\]

\[
\boxed{I_e=0}
\]

**Step 6: Calculate capacity utilization.**

\[
CU=\frac{8,000}{9,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CU=88.89\%}
\]

Notice something interesting.

The company has 11.11% unused machine capacity, but it cannot use that capacity because its available materials only support 8,000 units of production.

This is a material constraint, not a machine-capacity constraint.

**Step 7: Calculate financial performance.**

Revenue:

\[
9,500\times ₱100=₱950,000
\]

Variable cost:

\[
9,500\times ₱60=₱570,000
\]

Simplified operating profit:

\[
950,000-570,000-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{OP=₱280,000}
\]

**Upside case result**

The company can fulfill only 9,500 of the 10,800 units required.

- Supply shortage: 1,300 units.
- Customer fulfillment: 87.96%.
- Ending inventory: zero.
- Simplified operating profit: ₱280,000.

**Recommended actions:**

1. Expedite critical raw material deliveries.
2. Evaluate alternative qualified suppliers.
3. Consider additional procurement.
4. Evaluate overtime or additional production shifts.
5. Prioritize customers based on Topic 6.4.
6. Communicate realistic delivery commitments to customers.

#### What if management approves additional material procurement?

Suppose the company can expedite materials sufficient for another 1,000 units of production.

New production:

\[
P=9,000\ units
\]

The company reaches its machine capacity.

New available supply:

\[
S=1,500+9,000=10,500
\]

Remaining shortage:

\[
10,800-10,500=300\ units
\]

New fulfillment rate:

\[
\frac{10,500}{10,800}\times100
=97.22\%
\]

The additional procurement improves fulfillment from 87.96% to 97.22%.

However, management must evaluate the extra procurement and expediting costs before approving this response.

This is where Scenario Planning begins connecting with Trade-off Analysis.

### 7.4 Scenario C: Downside Case

**Business assumption:** Demand decreases by 15%, but a supplier also experiences a disruption that reduces material-supported production from 8,000 to 6,000 units.

Material cost also increases from ₱60 to ₱63.50 per unit.

Demand:

\[
D=9,000(1-0.15)
\]

\[
\boxed{D=7,650\ units}
\]

**Step 1: Calculate feasible production.**

\[
P=\min(9,000,6,000)
\]

\[
\boxed{P=6,000}
\]

**Step 2: Calculate available supply.**

\[
S=1,500+6,000
\]

\[
\boxed{S=7,500\ units}
\]

**Step 3: Calculate supply-demand gap.**

\[
Gap=7,650-7,500
\]

\[
\boxed{Gap=150\ units}
\]

**Step 4: Calculate customer fulfillment.**

\[
F=\min(7,650,7,500)
\]

\[
\boxed{F=7,500\ units}
\]

**Step 5: Calculate ending inventory.**

\[
I_e=1,500+6,000-7,500
\]

\[
\boxed{I_e=0}
\]

**Step 6: Calculate capacity utilization.**

\[
CU=\frac{6,000}{9,000}\times100
\]

\[
\boxed{CU=66.67\%}
\]

The company is using only 66.67% of its available production capacity.

However, this is not necessarily because demand is low. The supplier disruption is also preventing the company from utilizing its available capacity.

**Step 7: Calculate financial performance.**

Revenue:

\[
7,500\times ₱100=₱750,000
\]

Variable manufacturing cost:

\[
7,500\times ₱63.50=₱476,250
\]

Simplified operating profit:

\[
750,000-476,250-100,000
\]

\[
\boxed{OP=₱173,750}
\]

**Downside case result**

The company experiences:

- Lower customer demand.
- Reduced production output.
- Material shortage.
- Zero ending inventory.
- Higher manufacturing costs.
- Lower simplified operating profit.

**Recommended actions:**

1. Coordinate with the affected supplier.
2. Identify alternative qualified material sources.
3. Evaluate material allocation to critical customers.
4. Reduce or postpone nonessential procurement.
5. Adjust production schedules based on material availability.
6. Review the financial impact of increased material costs.
7. Protect customer delivery commitments where possible.

Notice that lower demand does not necessarily eliminate supply shortages. The company can still experience shortages when supply deteriorates faster than demand.

Now let's compare the three scenarios.

All figures are illustrative monthly planning results.

### 7.7 What Should Management Learn from These Results?

This is where we move beyond calculations and begin analytical thinking.

**Observation 1: Higher demand does not guarantee higher profitability.**

The upside scenario generates higher profit in our simplified model, but it also creates a 1,300-unit supply shortage.

The company may lose future customer confidence if it cannot meet delivery commitments.

**Observation 2: Available capacity is not the same as available supply.**

In the upside case, machine capacity is not fully utilized, but material availability prevents additional production.

An analyst must identify the actual constraint before recommending an action.

**Observation 3: Lower demand does not automatically mean lower supply risk.**

The downside case still has a 150-unit shortage because supplier disruption reduces production capability.

**Observation 4: Financial performance and operational performance must be evaluated together.**

The downside case has the lowest profit, while the upside case has the highest revenue.

However, the upside case also has the largest unfulfilled demand.

Management must understand both the financial opportunity and the operational risk.

## 8. Scenario Evaluation and Decision-Making

Developing scenarios is only half of the job.

An S&OP Analyst must also help management evaluate which scenarios deserve attention and what actions should be prepared.

### 8.1 Comparing Alternative Scenarios

There are several dimensions to consider.

| Evaluation criteria | Key management question |
|---|---|
| Demand | How much could customer requirements change? |
| Supply | Can suppliers support the required volume? |
| Capacity | Can production accommodate the change? |
| Inventory | Will stock be sufficient? |
| Customer service | Which customers could be affected? |
| Financial impact | How will revenue and costs change? |
| Risk exposure | How severe could the consequences become? |
| Feasibility | Can the proposed response actually be implemented? |

The objective is not simply to select the scenario with the highest profit.

The company must also consider feasibility, risk, and customer commitments.

### 8.2 Probability and Business Impact

Two important concepts in risk evaluation are:

- **Probability:** How likely is a particular event to occur?
- **Impact:** How significant would the consequences be if it occurred?

A simple risk assessment can use:

\[
Risk\ Score=Probability\times Impact
\]

For illustration, use a scale of 1 to 5.

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Risk score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Demand increase | 4 | 3 | 12 |
| Supplier disruption | 3 | 5 | 15 |
| Minor machine downtime | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| Major machine breakdown | 2 | 5 | 10 |

These scores are illustrative and depend on the company's defined risk-rating methodology.

The supplier disruption has the highest score, so it deserves significant management attention.

However, risk scores are not precise financial measurements. A high-impact, low-probability event may still deserve preparation if its consequences are severe.

### 8.3 Expected Financial Impact

Probability can also be used to estimate expected financial outcomes.

Suppose management assigns the following illustrative probabilities:

| Scenario | Probability | Operating profit |
|---|---:|---:|
| Base | 60% | ₱260,000 |
| Upside | 25% | ₱280,000 |
| Downside | 15% | ₱173,750 |

The expected operating profit is:

\[
E(OP)=\sum(P_i\times OP_i)
\]

Where:

- \(P_i\) = Probability of scenario \(i\)
- \(OP_i\) = Operating profit under scenario \(i\)

Calculation:

\[
E(OP)=
(0.60\times260,000)
\]

\[
+(0.25\times280,000)
\]

\[
+(0.15\times173,750)
\]

\[
=156,000+70,000+26,062.50
\]

\[
\boxed{E(OP)=₱252,062.50}
\]

This is the probability-weighted expected operating profit.

Important: This calculation is only meaningful if the scenarios are mutually exclusive, collectively cover the relevant outcomes, and their probabilities are reasonable. The probabilities are management assumptions, not facts.

It is also not a substitute for examining the downside risk separately.

### 8.4 Trigger Points and Warning Indicators

A trigger point is a predefined condition that signals when the company should activate a response plan or reassess its current assumptions.

Think of it as an early-warning system.

For example:

| Indicator | Illustrative trigger | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Customer orders | Demand exceeds forecast by 10% | Reassess production plan |
| Material availability | Less than 90% of required material confirmed | Escalate supplier coordination |
| Supplier lead time | Increases by 2 weeks | Evaluate alternate sourcing |
| Capacity utilization | Exceeds 95% | Evaluate overtime or additional shifts |
| Inventory | Falls below safety stock | Review replenishment and allocation |
| Material cost | Increases by 8% | Reevaluate procurement and financial plan |

These trigger values are illustrative. Actual thresholds should reflect company policies, product characteristics, lead times, and risk tolerance.

An important distinction:

A trigger point does not necessarily mean that a problem has already occurred.

It means that the company has reached a condition where preventive or corrective action should be considered.

### 8.5 Developing Contingency Actions

A contingency action is a specific response prepared for a possible business disruption.

Let's connect it to our manufacturing example.

| Trigger | Contingency action | Intended outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Demand exceeds forecast by 10% | Review overtime and additional shifts | Increase production |
| Supplier delivery is delayed | Activate qualified alternative supplier | Protect material availability |
| Machine downtime occurs | Transfer production to another qualified line | Recover output |
| Material prices increase | Negotiate pricing or evaluate alternative sourcing | Control costs |
| Demand decreases significantly | Adjust production and procurement | Avoid excess inventory |
| Supply remains insufficient | Prioritize critical customers | Protect important commitments |

The quality of a contingency plan depends on its practicality.

For example, recommending an alternative supplier is not enough.

The company must verify:

- Is the supplier qualified?
- Can it deliver the required quantity?
- How long will procurement take?
- Does the material meet specifications?
- What is the additional cost?

A response plan must be operationally feasible.

### 8.6 Why Scenario Planning Does Not Predict Exactly What Will Happen

This is a fundamental principle.

Scenario Planning is not designed to tell management exactly which scenario will occur.

Instead, it prepares management to respond to several plausible business conditions.

A company may prepare:

- An upside response.
- A downside response.
- A supplier disruption response.
- A capacity disruption response.

Actual business conditions may even fall between the scenarios or combine elements from several of them.

Therefore, scenario assumptions should be reviewed regularly as new information becomes available.

## 9. Scenario Planning in the S&OP Process

Now let's connect everything to the actual S&OP cycle.

### 9.1 Role of the S&OP Analyst

As an S&OP Analyst, your responsibility is to translate business assumptions into measurable operational and financial outcomes.

Typical responsibilities include:

1. Collecting the latest demand forecast.
2. Gathering production capacity information.
3. Reviewing inventory positions.
4. Confirming supplier commitments.
5. Identifying critical business assumptions.
6. Developing alternative scenarios.
7. Calculating supply-demand gaps.
8. Quantifying financial implications.
9. Identifying potential risks.
10. Preparing recommendations for management.

You are not necessarily the person who decides which scenario to implement.

Your job is to provide accurate analysis that enables decision-makers to choose intelligently.

### 9.2 Scenario Planning During Pre-S&OP

Pre-S&OP is where cross-functional teams review the demand and supply plans, resolve imbalances, and prepare recommendations for management.

A typical scenario analysis presentation might contain:

| Section | Information to present |
|---|---|
| Business baseline | Current demand and supply plan |
| Scenario assumptions | Changes in demand, capacity, and supply |
| Operational impact | Production, inventory, and shortages |
| Financial impact | Revenue, costs, and profitability |
| Risk assessment | Probability and potential consequences |
| Response options | Alternative actions |
| Recommendation | Proposed management decision |

Let's imagine presenting the upside scenario.

You might explain:

"The upside scenario indicates a 20% increase in demand, bringing the requirement to 10,800 units. Based on current supplier commitments, feasible production remains at 8,000 units. Together with beginning inventory, available supply is 9,500 units, resulting in a potential shortage of 1,300 units. We recommend evaluating expedited procurement to increase production by 1,000 units, which would improve the projected fulfillment rate from 87.96% to 97.22%, subject to additional procurement and expediting costs."

That is an example of professional S&OP communication.

Notice that the explanation includes:

- What changed.
- What the numbers indicate.
- What the business impact is.
- What action is recommended.
- What additional consideration management must evaluate.

### 9.3 Management Evaluation

During management review, decision-makers may consider:

- Which scenario is most plausible?
- What is the financial exposure?
- What customer commitments are at risk?
- How much additional capacity or procurement is required?
- What is the cost of taking preventive action?
- What happens if the company does nothing?
- Which risks require immediate escalation?

Management may decide to:

- Approve additional procurement.
- Authorize overtime.
- Increase safety stock.
- Reallocate inventory.
- Accept a controlled customer backlog.
- Adjust the financial forecast.

The approved decisions should then be reflected in the relevant operational plans.

### 9.4 How Scenario Planning Improves S&OP

| Without Scenario Planning | With Scenario Planning |
|---|---|
| Relies heavily on one forecast | Evaluates multiple possible conditions |
| Reacts after problems occur | Prepares responses in advance |
| May overlook hidden constraints | Identifies operational dependencies |
| Limited visibility of financial risks | Quantifies alternative financial outcomes |
| Unprepared for disruptions | Establishes contingency actions |
| Management decisions may be delayed | Provides structured alternatives |

Ultimately, Scenario Planning helps transform S&OP from a process focused only on preparing a plan into a process that prepares the business to respond to uncertainty.

Here are the most important concepts you should retain from Topic 6.5.

### A. Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Scenario Planning | Developing alternative business situations and evaluating their impacts. |
| Base Case | Expected business conditions. |
| Upside Case | More favorable business conditions. |
| Downside Case | Less favorable business conditions. |
| Sensitivity Analysis | Measuring how changes in assumptions affect results. |
| Contingency Planning | Preparing specific actions for potential disruptions. |
| Trigger Point | A predefined condition that signals the need for action or reassessment. |
| Risk Exposure | Potential consequences of uncertain business events. |

### B. Important Formulas

| Calculation | Formula |
|---|---|
| Scenario demand | \(D_b(1+r)\) |
| Feasible production | \(\min(C,M,L)\) |
| Available supply | \(I_b+P\) |
| Ending inventory | \(I_b+P-F\) |
| Supply-demand gap | \(D-S\) |
| Capacity utilization | \(\frac{P}{C}\times100\) |
| Revenue | \(F\times SP\) |
| Variable cost | \(F\times UC\) |
| Simplified operating profit | \(Revenue-VC-FC\) |
| Percentage change | \(\frac{Alternative-Base}{Base}\times100\) |
| Expected value | \(\sum(P_i\times Result_i)\) |

### C. Five Principles to Remember

1. Never rely entirely on one forecast. Prepare alternative scenarios for significant business uncertainties.
2. Always establish a consistent baseline before changing assumptions.
3. Evaluate operational and financial impacts together.
4. Every important scenario should have identifiable risks, trigger points, and potential response actions.
5. Scenario Planning prepares the company for uncertainty. It does not guarantee that the company can predict which situation will occur.

### D. How Topic 6.5 Connects to Topic 6.6

Let's establish the distinction before we proceed.

**Scenario Planning answers:**

"What could happen, and what are our possible response options?"

**Trade-off Analysis answers:**

"Considering the costs, benefits, risks, and operational consequences, which option should we choose?"

Example:

Your company has a projected shortage of 1,300 units.

You have three possible responses:

- Option A: Expedite raw materials.
- Option B: Add production overtime.
- Option C: Prioritize customer orders and accept delayed deliveries for other customers.

Scenario Planning helps establish the possible business conditions and response alternatives.

Trade-off Analysis will help evaluate which response provides the best overall business outcome.
