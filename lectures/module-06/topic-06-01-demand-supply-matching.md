# MODULE 6 – DEMAND-SUPPLY RECONCILIATION
## Topic 6.1: Demand-Supply Matching

**Learning objective:** Understand how a company determines whether its available and planned supply is sufficient to fulfill customer demand, identify shortages and surpluses, and establish the information needed for S&OP decision-making.

---

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

## 1.1 What is Demand-Supply Matching?

Imagine na may manufacturing company na gumagawa ng three products: Product A, Product B, and Product C.

For December, ito ang situation:

- Customers need 10,000 units of Product A.
- The company currently has 2,000 units in finished goods inventory.
- Production is expected to deliver another 6,000 units.
- Suppliers and production schedules will provide additional units later in the month.

The important question is:

**Will the company have enough products to fulfill the 10,000-unit customer demand?**

Hindi sapat na alam natin kung gaano karami ang gusto ng customers. Kailangan din nating malaman kung gaano karaming products ang kayang i-supply ng company, at kung kailan magiging available ang mga ito.

Ito ang purpose ng Demand-Supply Matching.

**Technical definition:**

Demand-Supply Matching is the process of comparing expected customer demand against available and planned supply across products and time periods to determine whether the organization can fulfill its demand requirements.

In simple terms:

- Demand tells us how much product is needed.
- Supply tells us how much product can be provided.
- Demand-Supply Matching tells us whether the two are aligned.

## 1.2 Why is it important?

Without demand-supply matching, companies may encounter:

| Problem | Possible consequence |
|---|---|
| Demand exceeds supply | Stockouts and lost sales |
| Supply exceeds demand | Excess inventory and higher carrying costs |
| Supply arrives too late | Late deliveries and customer dissatisfaction |
| Production exceeds capacity | Overtime and additional operating costs |
| Materials are unavailable | Production delays |
| Incorrect product allocation | Shortages in priority products |

The objective is not simply to produce as much as possible.

The objective is to establish a realistic supply plan that can satisfy demand while considering operational and financial limitations.

## 1.3 Why is this included in Module 6?

Let's connect the previous modules.

| Module | What you learned | Connection to Topic 6.1 |
|---|---|---|
| Module 3: Demand Planning | How much customers are expected to need | Establishes demand requirements |

Think of the first three modules as preparing the ingredients. Module 6 brings them together to evaluate whether the business plan is feasible.

## 1.4 Relationship with succeeding topics

Demand-Supply Matching establishes the overall comparison.

Topic 6.2, Identifying Supply Gaps, will take this further by examining why mismatches happen, how significant they are, and what actions can resolve them.

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

## 2.1 Think of a restaurant preparing meals

Suppose you operate a restaurant.

For tomorrow, you expect:

- 100 customers ordering chicken meals.
- 80 customers ordering beef meals.
- 50 customers ordering fish meals.

Your kitchen currently has:

- 60 chicken meals prepared.
- 40 beef meals prepared.
- 20 fish meals prepared.

Your kitchen also plans to prepare:

- 50 additional chicken meals.
- 30 additional beef meals.
- 40 additional fish meals.

Let's compare.

| Product | Customer demand | Available + planned supply | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Chicken | 100 | 110 | +10 |
| Beef | 80 | 70 | -10 |
| Fish | 50 | 60 | +10 |
| **Total** | **230** | **240** | **+10** |

At first glance, mukhang okay ang situation.

You have 240 meals for 230 customers. There is even a surplus of 10 meals.

Pero may problema.

You have 10 fewer beef meals than required.

Kahit may 10 excess chicken meals at 10 excess fish meals, hindi mo basta maipapalit ang mga iyon sa beef meals.

**This is one of the most important principles of demand-supply matching:**

Having enough total supply does not necessarily mean having enough supply of the correct products.

And there is another consideration: timing.

Kung available lang ang additional beef meals after lunch, hindi pa rin nito matutugunan ang lunch demand.

This means we must examine two things:

1. **Quantity:** Do we have enough?
2. **Timing:** Will it be available when needed?

Sa manufacturing, exactly the same principle applies.

A company may have sufficient total inventory but still experience shortages because the inventory is in the wrong product, location, or time period.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

## 3.1 Three important planning activities

Although related, Demand Planning, Supply Planning, and Demand-Supply Reconciliation have different responsibilities.

### 1. Demand Planning: What does the market need?

Determines expected customer demand using sales forecasts, customer orders, historical demand, market intelligence, and business assumptions.

**Output: Demand Plan**

### 2. Supply Planning: How can the company provide it?

Determines how much the company can produce, procure, and deliver based on inventory, materials, production capacity, and supplier availability.

**Output: Supply Plan**

### 3. Demand-Supply Reconciliation: Can the supply plan satisfy the demand plan?

Compares demand and supply, identifies mismatches, evaluates alternatives, and prepares decisions for management.

**Output: Reconciled Plan, identified gaps, and proposed actions**

Important clarification: These activities are not necessarily performed in a strict one-way sequence. In an actual S&OP cycle, they interact and are updated as new information becomes available.

## 3.2 How demand and supply plans connect in S&OP

A simplified S&OP process looks like this:

1. Demand Planning establishes the expected demand.
2. Supply Planning determines the available and achievable supply.
3. Demand-Supply Reconciliation compares the two.
4. The organization identifies gaps and evaluates possible solutions.
5. Management reviews trade-offs and approves decisions.
6. The agreed plan becomes the basis for execution.

The key concept is that **the demand plan and supply plan must eventually represent one coordinated business plan.**

For example:

The Demand Planning team may forecast 50,000 units for January.

However, Supply Planning may determine that only 42,000 units can be produced because of limited production capacity.

The difference is 8,000 units.

Hindi ibig sabihin na automatic na babawasan ang forecast to 42,000.

Instead, the company must evaluate possible actions:

- Increase production capacity.
- Add overtime or additional shifts.
- Outsource production.
- Expedite raw material deliveries.
- Allocate available inventory to priority customers.
- Negotiate delivery schedules with customers.

This is where S&OP becomes a decision-making process rather than simply a reporting activity.

## 3.3 Understanding Demand Requirements

Before comparing demand and supply, we need to understand exactly what constitutes demand.

### A. Customer Orders

Customer Orders are actual orders submitted by customers.

Example:

A customer places an order for 5,000 units of Product A with a requested delivery date of December 15.

This represents a known demand requirement.

Important characteristics:

- Specific quantity.
- Identified customer.
- Requested delivery date.
- Usually associated with a sales order or purchase order.

### B. Sales Forecast

A Sales Forecast represents the expected future demand based on available information.

Example:

The company forecasts that customers will purchase 8,000 units of Product A in January.

Hindi pa necessarily actual orders ang 8,000 units. Expected demand pa lang ito.

Forecasts help companies prepare production and procurement before customers actually place orders.

**Important distinction:**

| Customer Orders | Sales Forecast |
|---|---|
| Actual customer commitments | Expected future demand |
| Known quantities | Estimated quantities |
| Usually have requested delivery dates | Based on forecast periods |
| Can change through order modifications | Can change through forecast updates |

One important planning consideration: Avoid double-counting customer orders and forecasts.

For example, if the January forecast is 10,000 units and actual orders already account for 4,000 units of that forecast, the company must understand whether the forecast represents total demand or additional demand.

Otherwise, it might mistakenly plan for 14,000 units.

### C. Independent and Dependent Demand

These two concepts are particularly important in manufacturing.

**Independent Demand**

Demand that originates from external customers or the market.

Examples:

- Customers ordering finished products.
- Distributors replenishing their inventory.
- Forecasted demand for finished goods.

It is generally forecasted or derived from actual customer requirements.

**Dependent Demand**

Demand that is derived from the requirement for another product or component.

Example:

A company manufactures 1,000 finished units.

Each finished unit requires:

- 2 capacitors
- 1 resistor
- 1 housing

Therefore:

| Component | Requirement per unit | Total requirement |
|---|---:|---:|
| Capacitor | 2 | 2,000 |
| Resistor | 1 | 1,000 |
| Housing | 1 | 1,000 |

The demand for these components depends on the production requirement of the finished products.

This is called Dependent Demand.

In manufacturing, Material Requirements Planning (MRP) commonly calculates dependent demand using the Bill of Materials (BOM).

**Why does this matter in demand-supply matching?**

A company may have enough finished goods to satisfy customer orders but insufficient raw materials to support future production.

Therefore, matching cannot stop at finished goods inventory. The organization must also consider the materials needed to generate future supply.

### D. Demand by Product, Product Family, and Time Period

Demand can be analyzed at different levels.

| Level | Example | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Model A100 | Determine exact product requirements |
| Product Family | Industrial Capacitors | Analyze demand for related products |
| Business Unit | Industrial Components | Evaluate larger business requirements |
| Monthly | January 2027 | Determine monthly supply requirements |
| Weekly | Week 1 to Week 4 | Identify timing constraints |

This introduces two important planning dimensions:

- **Product dimension:** What specific product is required?
- **Time dimension:** When is the product required?

A demand plan without product details may hide individual product shortages.

A demand plan without timing details may hide delivery problems.

For example, 10,000 units required in January cannot automatically be treated as equivalent to 10,000 units required in March.

The company must satisfy demand when it is required, not merely sometime within the year.

## 3.4 Understanding Supply Availability

Supply availability represents the quantity of products that can be used to fulfill demand during a particular planning period.

There are several major sources.

### A. Beginning Inventory

Beginning Inventory is the quantity of usable inventory available at the start of a planning period.

Example:

January 1:

- Finished goods inventory = 2,000 units.

Those 2,000 units can potentially be used to fulfill January demand.

However, we must consider whether the inventory is actually usable.

For example:

- Some units may be allocated to existing customer orders.
- Some may be under quality inspection.
- Some may be defective or on hold.
- Some may be reserved for another location.

Therefore, physical inventory and available inventory are not necessarily the same.

### B. Planned Production

Planned Production represents the quantity expected to be manufactured during a particular period.

Example:

January production plan:

| Product | Planned production |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 |
| Product B | 3,000 |
| Product C | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** |

But there is an important distinction:

Planned production is not automatically guaranteed supply.

It depends on:

- Available production capacity.
- Machine availability.
- Labor availability.
- Material availability.
- Production yield.
- Planned downtime.
- Production schedule adherence.

For example, a production plan of 5,000 units may be unrealistic if the available machine capacity only supports 4,000 units.

### C. Scheduled Receipts

Scheduled Receipts represent incoming supply expected from previously established production or procurement orders.

Examples:

- Purchase orders scheduled for delivery.
- Production orders scheduled for completion.
- Transfers from another warehouse.

Example:

The company has an open purchase order for 2,000 units of raw material, scheduled to arrive on January 15.

That quantity may support production after January 15, but it cannot be assumed available on January 1.

Timing is important.

**Avoid double-counting:** If a production order is already included in planned production, do not count it again as an additional scheduled receipt.

The same principle applies to purchase orders already incorporated into a supply plan.

### D. Procurement and Supplier Deliveries

Procurement provides the raw materials and components needed to support manufacturing.

For example:

A manufacturing company needs 10,000 kg of raw material for February production.

However:

- Supplier A can deliver only 6,000 kg.
- Supplier B can deliver 2,000 kg.
- The remaining 2,000 kg will arrive in March.

Even if the production plan requires 10,000 kg, only 8,000 kg will be available in February.

This creates a material constraint.

The company must consider supplier lead times, delivery schedules, and material availability when evaluating supply feasibility.

### E. Production Capacity Constraints

Capacity determines how much production the company can realistically accomplish.

Example:

A production line has:

- Available operating time: 400 hours.
- Production rate: 100 units per hour.

Maximum theoretical output:

\[
400 \times 100 = 40,000\text{ units}
\]

However, actual production may be lower because of:

- Machine breakdowns.
- Changeovers.
- Maintenance.
- Quality losses.
- Labor shortages.

If expected efficiency is 90%, illustrative achievable output becomes:

\[
40,000 \times 90\% = 36,000
\]

Therefore, a production plan requiring 45,000 units cannot be considered feasible under these assumptions.

### F. Available Supply vs. Committed Supply

This distinction is important when preparing a supply plan.

| Available Supply | Committed Supply |
|---|---|
| Supply that is usable or expected to become available | Supply already allocated or promised to specific requirements |
| Can potentially serve new demand | Has an existing obligation |
| Includes unallocated inventory and feasible incoming supply | Includes quantities reserved for customers or other commitments |

Example:

A company has 5,000 units of finished goods.

However:

- 3,000 units are already allocated to customer orders.
- 500 units are under quality hold.
- 1,500 units are unallocated and usable.

The physical inventory is 5,000 units, but only 1,500 units are available for new allocation.

**Professional reminder:** Always clarify whether the supply figure being used is gross inventory, unrestricted inventory, available-to-promise inventory, or net uncommitted supply. These are not interchangeable.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION: DEMAND-SUPPLY COMPARISON

This is the core of Topic 6.1.

## 4.1 How Demand and Supply Are Compared

The simplest comparison is:

\[
\text{Supply} - \text{Demand}
\]

Interpretation:

| Result | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positive | Supply exceeds demand |
| Zero | Supply exactly matches demand |
| Negative | Demand exceeds supply |

Example:

- Demand = 10,000 units.
- Supply = 8,000 units.

\[
8,000-10,000=-2,000
\]

The company has a shortage of 2,000 units.

However, this simple comparison is only the starting point.

We also need to determine how inventory moves from one period to another.

## 4.2 Understanding Supply-Demand Gaps

A Supply-Demand Gap represents the difference between the supply required and the supply available.

There are two possible conditions.

**Supply Shortage**

\[
Demand > Supply
\]

Example:

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Demand | 10,000 |
| Supply | 8,000 |
| Shortage | 2,000 |

Possible consequences:

- Unfulfilled customer orders.
- Backlog accumulation.
- Delayed shipments.
- Lost sales.

**Supply Surplus**

\[
Supply > Demand
\]

Example:

| Item | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Demand | 10,000 |
| Supply | 12,000 |
| Surplus | 2,000 |

Possible consequences:

- Higher inventory.
- Increased storage requirements.
- Higher inventory carrying costs.
- Potential excess and obsolete inventory.

Remember: A surplus is not automatically a good thing. It depends on whether the additional inventory supports future demand.

## 4.3 Projected Inventory

Projected Inventory estimates how much inventory the company expects to have after considering planned supply and expected demand.

For example:

Beginning inventory = 2,000 units.

Expected incoming supply = 5,000 units.

Expected demand = 6,000 units.

\[
2,000+5,000-6,000=1,000
\]

Projected ending inventory = 1,000 units.

This means the company expects to finish the period with 1,000 units.

Projected inventory helps analysts determine whether the company is likely to experience shortages or accumulate excess stocks.

## 4.4 Understanding Backlog

Backlog represents demand that has not yet been fulfilled.

Example:

A customer requires 5,000 units.

The company can deliver only 4,000 units.

\[
5,000-4,000=1,000
\]

Unfulfilled demand = 1,000 units.

That 1,000 units may become backlog if the customer order remains open and is expected to be fulfilled later.

**Important distinction:**

- Shortage is a supply-demand imbalance.
- Backlog is outstanding demand that remains unfulfilled.

A shortage may create backlog, but not every forecast shortage becomes backlog.

For example, a forecasted shortage may lead to lost sales or a revised customer order instead of an actual backlog.

## 4.5 Why Aggregate Totals Can Be Misleading

Let's revisit the restaurant example.

| Product | Demand | Supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | 1,500 | +500 |
| B | 2,000 | 1,500 | -500 |
| C | 1,000 | 1,000 | 0 |
| **Total** | **4,000** | **4,000** | **0** |

The aggregate supply-demand gap is zero.

Does that mean the company has no problem?

No.

Product B has a shortage of 500 units, while Product A has an excess of 500 units.

The excess of Product A cannot automatically fulfill the shortage of Product B.

The same issue occurs across different periods.

| Month | Demand | Supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 12,000 | +2,000 |
| February | 15,000 | 12,000 | -3,000 |
| March | 10,000 | 11,000 | +1,000 |
| **Total** | **35,000** | **35,000** | **0** |

The total demand and supply are equal.

But February still has a 3,000-unit shortage.

January's excess supply cannot automatically solve February's shortage unless the products are available in the correct quantity and can be carried over as inventory.

**Key principle:**

Demand-supply matching must be performed at the appropriate product and time-period level, not only at the aggregate business level.

# 5. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

Let's now formalize the calculations.

## 5.1 Important Variables

| Variable | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| BI | Beginning Inventory | Units |
| PP | Planned Production | Units |
| SR | Scheduled Receipts | Units |
| D | Demand | Units |
| AS | Available Supply | Units |
| NS | Net Supply after Demand | Units |
| EI | Ending Inventory | Units |
| SS | Supply Surplus | Units |
| SH | Supply Shortage | Units |
| BL | Backlog | Units |

For our examples, assume:

1. All quantities refer to the same product.
2. All quantities use the same unit of measurement.
3. Supply is usable and available within the stated period.
4. Demand is measured by its required fulfillment period.
5. No yield losses or quality holds unless specifically stated.
6. We use an end-of-period inventory convention.
7. Negative projected inventory indicates a shortage or unfulfilled demand, not physically negative inventory.

### Formula 1: Total Available Supply

\[
AS=BI+PP+SR
\]

Where:

- BI = Beginning Inventory
- PP = Planned Production
- SR = Scheduled Receipts

Example:

| Component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Beginning inventory | 2,000 |
| Planned production | 5,000 |
| Scheduled receipts | 1,000 |

\[
AS=2,000+5,000+1,000
\]

\[
\boxed{AS=8,000\text{ units}}
\]

Interpretation:

The company expects to have 8,000 units available to fulfill demand during the period.

This assumes the production and receipts are available before the relevant demand must be fulfilled.

### Formula 2: Net Supply After Demand

\[
NS=AS-D
\]

Example:

- Available supply = 8,000 units.
- Demand = 6,500 units.

\[
NS=8,000-6,500
\]

\[
\boxed{NS=1,500\text{ units}}
\]

Interpretation:

The company expects to have 1,500 units remaining after fulfilling demand.

If the result is negative:

\[
NS=8,000-9,000=-1,000
\]

The company has a potential shortage of 1,000 units.

### Formula 3: Supply-Demand Gap

There are two commonly used ways to express the gap.

**A. Supply minus demand**

\[
Gap=Supply-Demand
\]

- Positive = Surplus.
- Negative = Shortage.

**B. Demand minus supply**

\[
Gap=Demand-Supply
\]

- Positive = Shortage.
- Negative = Surplus.

Both conventions are valid, provided the analyst clearly identifies which convention is being used.

For this learning program, we will use:

\[
\boxed{Gap=Supply-Demand}
\]

Example:

\[
Gap=8,000-9,500=-1,500
\]

Interpretation: 1,500-unit supply shortage.

### Formula 4: Projected Ending Inventory

\[
EI=BI+PP+SR-D
\]

This is the basic projected inventory balance.

Example:

- Beginning inventory = 3,000.
- Planned production = 4,000.
- Scheduled receipts = 1,000.
- Demand = 6,500.

\[
EI=3,000+4,000+1,000-6,500
\]

\[
\boxed{EI=1,500\text{ units}}
\]

Interpretation:

The company expects to finish the period with 1,500 units of inventory.

Notice that this formula is mathematically equivalent to Net Supply After Demand.

The difference is mainly how we interpret the result.

### Formula 5: Unfulfilled Demand and Backlog

First, calculate the shortage:

\[
Shortage=\max(0,D-AS)
\]

Example:

- Demand = 9,000.
- Available supply = 7,000.

\[
Shortage=\max(0,9,000-7,000)
\]

\[
\boxed{Shortage=2,000\text{ units}}
\]

For backlog:

\[
Ending\ Backlog=
Beginning\ Backlog+New\ Demand-Fulfilled\ Demand
\]

Example:

- Beginning backlog = 500.
- New demand = 2,000.
- Fulfilled demand = 1,800.

\[
Ending\ Backlog=500+2,000-1,800
\]

\[
\boxed{Ending\ Backlog=700\text{ units}}
\]

Interpretation:

The company has 700 units of outstanding demand.

Important: If demand represents forecast rather than actual customer orders, use terms such as projected unfulfilled demand or projected shortage unless the company formally classifies it as backlog.

### Formula 6: Cumulative Demand-Supply Balance

This formula is particularly useful when analyzing multiple periods.

\[
CB_t=CB_{t-1}+PP_t+SR_t-D_t
\]

Where:

- \(CB_t\) = Cumulative balance at the end of the current period.
- \(CB_{t-1}\) = Previous period's ending balance.
- \(PP_t\) = Current period's planned production.
- \(SR_t\) = Current period's scheduled receipts.
- \(D_t\) = Current period's demand.

Initial condition:

\[
CB_0=Beginning\ Inventory
\]

Example:

Beginning inventory = 1,000 units.

| Month | Production | Receipts | Demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 2,000 | 500 | 2,500 |
| February | 1,500 | 0 | 2,000 |
| March | 2,000 | 500 | 2,500 |

**January**

\[
CB_1=1,000+2,000+500-2,500
\]

\[
CB_1=1,000
\]

**February**

\[
CB_2=1,000+1,500+0-2,000
\]

\[
CB_2=500
\]

**March**

\[
CB_3=500+2,000+500-2,500
\]

\[
CB_3=500
\]

| Month | Ending cumulative balance |
|---|---:|
| January | 1,000 |
| February | 500 |
| March | 500 |

The company can fulfill all projected demand and expects to retain 500 units after March.

**A critical timing reminder:** This is a simplified period-end balance. If production or supplier receipts arrive after customer demand is due, the company may experience a temporary shortage even if the ending monthly balance is positive. Weekly or daily analysis may be necessary.

# 6. PRACTICAL MANUFACTURING APPLICATION

Now let's apply everything to a realistic manufacturing scenario.

For this example, imagine a manufacturing company producing three finished goods:

- Product A
- Product B
- Product C

The company needs to prepare its January to March supply plan.

The Demand Planning team has provided the monthly forecast, while the Supply Planning team has provided the production and incoming supply schedules.

Our responsibility as an S&OP analyst is to determine whether the planned supply can support the demand.

## 6.1 Establish the Demand Plan

The Demand Planning team provides the following forecast:

**Table 1. Monthly Demand Forecast**

| Product | January | February | March | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,000 | 1,200 | 1,100 | 3,300 |
| B | 700 | 750 | 650 | 2,100 |
| C | 500 | 600 | 700 | 1,800 |
| **Total** | **2,200** | **2,550** | **2,450** | **7,200** |

Interpretation:

- Product A has the highest total demand.
- Product B has relatively stable demand.
- Product C experiences increasing demand from January to March.

The company needs to fulfill 7,200 units over three months.

## 6.2 Establish Beginning Inventory

Next, obtain the available finished goods inventory at the beginning of January.

**Table 2. Beginning Inventory**

| Product | Beginning Inventory |
|---|---:|
| A | 300 |
| B | 250 |
| C | 400 |
| **Total** | **950** |

The company starts January with 950 units of available finished goods.

For this example, assume these quantities are unrestricted, usable, and available for customer demand.

## 6.3 Establish the Supply Plan

The Supply Planning team provides the planned production quantities.

**Table 3. Planned Production**

| Product | January | February | March | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 800 | 1,000 | 900 | 2,700 |
| B | 500 | 500 | 600 | 1,600 |
| C | 400 | 300 | 500 | 1,200 |
| **Total** | **1,700** | **1,800** | **2,000** | **5,500** |

The company plans to manufacture 5,500 units over three months.

## 6.4 Identify Scheduled Receipts

The company also expects additional finished goods from previously scheduled production or inventory transfers.

**Table 4. Scheduled Receipts**

| Product | January | February | March | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 100 | 0 | 0 | 100 |
| B | 100 | 150 | 0 | 250 |
| C | 0 | 100 | 0 | 100 |
| **Total** | **200** | **250** | **0** | **450** |

The scheduled receipts total 450 units.

For simplicity, all scheduled receipts and planned production in this example are assumed to become available before the respective month's demand is fulfilled.

## 6.5 Calculate Total Available Supply

Let's combine the supply sources.

\[
AS=BI+PP+SR
\]

For January:

| Product | Beginning inventory | Production | Receipts | Available supply |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 300 | 800 | 100 | 1,200 |
| B | 250 | 500 | 100 | 850 |
| C | 400 | 400 | 0 | 800 |
| **Total** | **950** | **1,700** | **200** | **2,850** |

January available supply:

\[
950+1,700+200=2,850
\]

The company has 2,850 units available against demand of 2,200 units.

Initial supply surplus:

\[
2,850-2,200=650
\]

At this point, January appears healthy.

But we still need to calculate the ending inventory of each product.

## 6.6 Calculate Projected Ending Inventory

The formula:

\[
EI=BI+PP+SR-D
\]

Let's calculate January.

| Product | Available supply | Demand | Ending inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1,200 | 1,000 | 200 |
| B | 850 | 700 | 150 |
| C | 800 | 500 | 300 |
| **Total** | **2,850** | **2,200** | **650** |

The company finishes January with 650 units.

Now, these 650 units become the beginning inventory for February.

This is important. Hindi natin dapat i-reset sa zero ang inventory every month.

### February calculation

| Product | Beginning inventory | Production | Receipts | Demand | Ending inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 200 | 1,000 | 0 | 1,200 | 0 |
| B | 150 | 500 | 150 | 750 | 50 |
| C | 300 | 300 | 100 | 600 | 100 |
| **Total** | **650** | **1,800** | **250** | **2,550** | **150** |

February total:

\[
650+1,800+250-2,550=150
\]

The company finishes February with 150 units.

### March calculation

The February ending inventory becomes March beginning inventory.

| Product | Beginning inventory | Production | Receipts | Demand | Ending inventory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 0 | 900 | 0 | 1,100 | -200 |
| B | 50 | 600 | 0 | 650 | 0 |
| C | 100 | 500 | 0 | 700 | -100 |
| **Total** | **150** | **2,000** | **0** | **2,450** | **-300** |

March total:

\[
150+2,000-2,450=-300
\]

The projected cumulative balance is negative 300 units.

This means the company cannot fulfill all March demand under the current plan.

More specifically:

- Product A has a projected shortage of 200 units.
- Product B can fulfill its demand.
- Product C has a projected shortage of 100 units.

The total projected shortage is 300 units.

## 6.7 Consolidated Demand-Supply Comparison

Now let's put everything together.

**Table 5. Consolidated Monthly Demand-Supply Plan**

| Metric | January | February | March |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Beginning inventory | 950 | 650 | 150 |
| Planned production | 1,700 | 1,800 | 2,000 |
| Scheduled receipts | 200 | 250 | 0 |
| **Total available supply** | **2,850** | **2,700** | **2,150** |
| Demand | 2,200 | 2,550 | 2,450 |
| **Ending balance** | **650** | **150** | **-300** |
| Supply condition | Surplus | Surplus | Shortage |

**Monthly demand versus total available supply (units):**

| Month | Available supply | Demand |
|---|---:|---:|
| January | 2,850 | 2,200 |
| February | 2,700 | 2,550 |
| March | 2,150 | 2,450 |

Notice something important:

January has a surplus of 650 units.

February has a surplus of 150 units.

March has a shortage of 300 units.

This illustrates why we need to track inventory continuously across periods.

The business does not simply compare each month's production against that month's demand. It must account for the inventory carried over from previous months.

## 6.8 Product-Level Analysis

The aggregate result tells us that the company has a 300-unit shortage in March.

But what products are causing the problem?

**March projected inventory by product (units):**

| Product | Projected ending balance |
|---|---:|
| Product A | -200 |
| Product B | 0 |
| Product C | -100 |

This reveals that Product A and Product C are the sources of the shortage.

Product B is fully covered.

This is important because the company does not necessarily need to increase production for all three products.

It needs to investigate the specific products that have supply gaps.

## 6.9 What If We Look Only at the Three-Month Total?

Let's compare total demand and total supply over the entire planning horizon.

| Metric | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Beginning inventory | 950 |
| Planned production | 5,500 |
| Scheduled receipts | 450 |
| **Total supply** | **6,900** |
| Total demand | 7,200 |
| **Net balance** | **-300** |

Even though the overall three-month shortage is also 300 units, the monthly analysis gives us something that the aggregate calculation cannot:

**It identifies when the shortage occurs and which products are affected.**

In another situation, the total three-month supply might exceed demand while a temporary shortage still occurs in February.

That is why time-phased analysis is essential.

## 6.10 What Should the S&OP Analyst Recommend?

As an S&OP analyst, your job is not necessarily to tell production to manufacture more immediately.

Your responsibility is to understand the gap and provide management with reliable information for decision-making.

For this scenario, you might investigate:

| Identified issue | Possible investigation |
|---|---|
| Product A shortage of 200 | Can production increase by 200 units? |
| Product C shortage of 100 | Can supplier or material availability support additional production? |
| March demand increase for C | Is the demand increase supported by customer orders or forecast assumptions? |
| February inventory declining | Can production be rescheduled earlier? |

Possible corrective actions:

1. Advance some April production into March, if capacity and materials permit.
2. Increase production quantities for Products A and C.
3. Expedite scheduled supplier deliveries if raw materials are constrained.
4. Reallocate available inventory, subject to customer commitments.
5. Negotiate delivery dates with customers if additional supply is not feasible.

The final decision must consider costs, capacity, customer priorities, and financial implications.

# 7. DEMAND-SUPPLY MATCHING IN S&OP

Now let's move from calculations to actual business responsibilities.

This is where the work of an S&OP analyst becomes more analytical.

## 7.1 How Analysts Identify Mismatches

An analyst normally investigates several dimensions.

| Dimension | What to examine |
|---|---|
| Product | Which products have shortages or surpluses? |
| Time | When will the imbalance occur? |
| Quantity | How large is the shortage or surplus? |
| Inventory | Is there sufficient usable beginning inventory? |
| Production | Can the manufacturing schedule support the requirement? |
| Materials | Are raw materials available? |
| Capacity | Are machines and labor sufficient? |
| Supplier | Will incoming materials arrive on time? |
| Customer | Which orders or customers will be affected? |

A good analyst does not stop at reporting:

"March has a shortage of 300 units."

A better analysis is:

"March has a projected supply shortage of 300 units, consisting of 200 units of Product A and 100 units of Product C. Product A production is constrained by available machine capacity, while Product C requires validation of raw material availability. The team should evaluate production rescheduling and supplier expediting."

That is the difference between simply reporting numbers and providing useful business analysis.

## 7.2 Temporary Timing Gap vs. Structural Supply Shortage

Not all supply shortages have the same root cause.

This distinction is extremely important.

### A. Temporary Timing Gap

A temporary timing gap occurs when sufficient supply exists or is expected, but it becomes available later than the demand requirement.

Example:

- Customer demand: 5,000 units in January.
- Available supply in January: 4,000 units.
- Additional scheduled receipt: 1,000 units in February.

The company has sufficient total supply across the two months, but the January requirement cannot be fully satisfied.

Possible causes:

- Late supplier delivery.
- Delayed production completion.
- Incorrect production scheduling.
- Transportation delays.

Possible actions:

- Expedite deliveries.
- Advance production.
- Use available inventory from another location.
- Negotiate delivery schedules.

### B. Structural Supply Shortage

A structural shortage occurs when the company's available supply capability is insufficient to support demand over a sustained period.

Example:

| Month | Demand | Maximum achievable supply | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 10,000 | 8,000 | -2,000 |
| February | 10,000 | 8,000 | -2,000 |
| March | 11,000 | 8,000 | -3,000 |

The company consistently cannot produce enough.

Possible causes:

- Insufficient production capacity.
- Long-term supplier limitations.
- Persistent material shortages.
- Workforce limitations.
- Equipment constraints.

Possible actions:

- Add production shifts.
- Invest in additional equipment.
- Qualify alternative suppliers.
- Outsource production.
- Reevaluate demand commitments.

### How to distinguish them

| Question | Temporary gap | Structural gap |
|---|---|---|
| Is sufficient supply available later? | Usually yes | Not necessarily |
| Does the problem persist? | Usually short-term | Often recurring |
| Main issue | Timing | Capability or sustained availability |
| Typical solution | Rescheduling or expediting | Capacity or sourcing changes |

Important: A temporary gap can become a structural problem if delays continue. Root-cause investigation is necessary before classifying the problem.

## 7.3 Demand Review: What Should Be Communicated?

Demand Review primarily focuses on the demand side.

The analyst should help establish whether the demand plan is reasonable and properly understood.

Important questions:

- Are customer orders increasing?
- Is the sales forecast realistic?
- Are there new customer programs?
- Are there unusual demand spikes?
- Are forecast assumptions still valid?
- Are there changes in customer delivery requirements?

Example:

Product A demand increases from 1,000 units to 1,500 units.

The analyst should determine whether the increase comes from:

- Actual customer orders.
- A new forecast assumption.
- A one-time customer requirement.
- An unexpected market development.

The objective is to establish a credible demand plan before evaluating the supply response.

## 7.4 Supply Review: What Should Be Communicated?

Supply Review focuses on the organization's ability to meet the established demand plan.

Important questions:

- Is production capacity sufficient?
- Are raw materials available?
- Are supplier deliveries on schedule?
- Is finished goods inventory adequate?
- Are there production constraints?
- Can the supply plan be adjusted?

Example:

The demand plan requires 15,000 units, but available production and inventory can support only 12,000 units.

The analyst should communicate:

- The 3,000-unit shortage.
- Which products are affected.
- When the shortage will occur.
- The operational constraint causing it.
- Available alternatives.
- The impact of each alternative.

## 7.5 How Matching Results Support Management Decisions

The purpose of demand-supply matching is to provide a factual basis for business decisions.

| Situation | Possible management decision |
|---|---|
| Demand exceeds capacity | Approve additional shifts or equipment |
| Raw materials are insufficient | Approve supplier expediting or alternative sourcing |
| Excess supply is projected | Reduce or reschedule production |
| Customer demand is prioritized | Allocate limited supply to strategic customers |
| Supply will arrive late | Negotiate customer delivery dates |
| Persistent shortage exists | Evaluate capacity expansion or outsourcing |

There is also a financial dimension.

Increasing production may solve a shortage, but it can increase:

- Labor costs.
- Overtime expenses.
- Manufacturing overhead.
- Inventory investment.

On the other hand, reducing production to prevent excess inventory may result in:

- Lower inventory holding costs.
- Lower working capital requirements.
- Potentially higher stockout risk.

S&OP must balance these competing considerations.

**The objective is not simply to maximize production or minimize inventory. It is to establish the best feasible balance between customer demand, operational capability, inventory, and business performance.**

# 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS

## 8.1 Fundamental Concepts

| Concept | What you should remember |
|---|---|
| Demand | What customers are expected to require |
| Supply | What the company can provide |
| Demand Planning | Determines expected customer requirements |
| Supply Planning | Determines achievable production and procurement |
| Demand-Supply Matching | Compares demand and supply |
| Supply Gap | Difference between available supply and demand |
| Projected Inventory | Expected inventory after supply and demand |
| Backlog | Outstanding unfulfilled customer demand |
| Temporary Gap | Supply exists but is available at the wrong time |
| Structural Shortage | Sustained supply capability is insufficient |

## 8.2 Six Formulas to Remember

**1. Total Available Supply**

\[
AS=BI+PP+SR
\]

**2. Net Supply After Demand**

\[
NS=AS-D
\]

**3. Supply-Demand Gap**

\[
Gap=Supply-Demand
\]

**4. Projected Ending Inventory**

\[
EI=BI+PP+SR-D
\]

**5. Unfulfilled Demand**

\[
Shortage=\max(0,D-AS)
\]

**6. Cumulative Balance**

\[
CB_t=CB_{t-1}+PP_t+SR_t-D_t
\]

Remember that these are simplified planning formulas. Actual company systems may incorporate allocations, quality restrictions, lead times, safety stock, yield, and order-priority rules.

## 8.3 The Most Important Lessons

There are five things I want you to remember:

1. Demand and supply must be evaluated together. A demand plan alone does not establish whether customer requirements can be fulfilled.
2. Supply must be evaluated by product and time period. Aggregate totals can hide individual shortages.
3. Inventory carries forward between planning periods. Beginning inventory, production, and receipts must be incorporated into the inventory balance.
4. A shortage does not always mean insufficient production capacity. It may be caused by supplier delays, scheduling, material availability, or other timing issues.
5. Demand-Supply Matching supports decisions. The objective is to identify mismatches, understand their causes, and evaluate possible solutions.
