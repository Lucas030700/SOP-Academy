# Module 1 – S&OP Fundamentals
## Topic 1.5: S&OP vs. Other Planning Processes

**Level 1 → Level 2 → Level 3**

**Beginner to Professional**

**Central learning question:**

Why does a company need Demand Planning, Supply Planning, S&OP, MPS, MRP, Production Scheduling, and S&OE when all of them are involved in planning the same business?

The answer is that **they solve different planning problems, at different levels of detail, and over different time horizons.**

Hindi sila magkakahiwalay na activities na walang connection. They are interconnected processes that translate business objectives into actual products, materials, production activities, and customer deliveries.

### 1.1 What will we discuss?

Sa previous topics, pinag-aralan natin ang:

| Business question | Planning process |
|---|---|
| Where do we want the company to be in five years? | Strategic Business Planning |
| What are our business and financial targets for next year? | Annual Operating Plan |
| How many products do customers expect to buy? | Demand Planning |

Imagine na ikaw ang S&OP Analyst sa isang manufacturing company.

Your company receives a significant increase in customer orders.

Several questions immediately arise:

1. Is the increased demand sustainable?
2. Do we have enough production capacity?
3. Do we have enough raw materials?
4. Can suppliers deliver on time?
5. Should we increase inventory?
6. Should we add overtime or another production shift?
7. Will the additional production generate enough revenue and profit?
8. What should production manufacture tomorrow?

Hindi kayang sagutin ng isang planning process lang ang lahat ng questions na ito.

That's why companies establish different planning processes with different responsibilities.

**The key objective of this lesson is to understand where S&OP fits within the entire business planning system.**

# 2. Layman's Explanation

Let's start with an analogy.

## 2.1 Imagine that you own a manufacturing company

Let's say you own a company manufacturing electronic components.

Your company produces three product families:

- Product A
- Product B
- Product C

Your business wants to increase annual sales by 15%.

How do you turn that objective into actual production?

Hindi puwedeng sabihin mo lang sa production department:

"Increase production by 15%."

Kailangan may sequence of decisions.

### Step 1: Decide where the business wants to go

**Strategic Planning**

Management decides to expand the business and increase market share over the next three years.

### Step 2: Establish the annual targets

**Annual Operating Plan**

Management establishes annual revenue, profit, and production targets.

### Step 3: Estimate what customers will buy

**Demand Planning**

Sales and Demand Planning estimate that customers will need 100,000 units next month.

### Step 4: Determine whether the company can support the demand

**Supply Planning**

Operations evaluates available capacity, labor, materials, inventory, and supplier capabilities.

### Step 5: Agree on one business plan

**S&OP**

Sales, Operations, Procurement, Finance, and other departments agree on a feasible plan that balances demand, supply, and financial objectives.

### Step 6: Determine which finished products to manufacture

**MPS**

Production planning determines how many units of each finished product should be manufactured and when.

### Step 7: Calculate the required materials

**MRP**

The system calculates how much raw material, components, and other materials are needed.

### Step 8: Assign actual production activities

**Production Scheduling**

The production team determines which machines, orders, and time slots will be used.

### Step 9: Handle immediate operational problems

**S&OE**

The team responds to machine breakdowns, material shortages, urgent customer orders, and other short-term disruptions.

## 2.2 The easiest way to remember the differences

Think of planning as answering nine different questions:

| Process | Simple question |
|---|---|
| Strategic Planning | Where are we going? |
| AOP | What are our targets? |
| Demand Planning | What will customers need? |
| Supply Planning | What can we provide? |
| S&OP | What business plan will we agree on? |
| MPS | What finished products will we make? |
| MRP | What materials do we need? |
| Production Scheduling | What will each machine produce and when? |
| S&OE | What needs immediate attention? |

**Important distinction:** S&OP is not a replacement for all the other processes. It is the integration and alignment point between business objectives, demand, supply, and financial considerations.

# 3. Technical Explanation

Now let's introduce the professional terminology.

## 3.1 Three major levels of planning

Before comparing individual processes, you must first understand the three planning levels.

### LEVEL 1: Strategic Planning

**Typical horizon: Years**

Long-term direction and business objectives.

Examples:

- Market expansion.
- New manufacturing facilities.
- Capital investments.
- Long-term business growth.

### LEVEL 2: Tactical Planning

**Typical horizon: Months to approximately 1–2 years**

Translate business objectives into coordinated plans.

Examples:

- S&OP.
- Demand Planning.
- Supply Planning.
- Inventory strategy.
- Aggregate capacity planning.

### LEVEL 3: Operational Planning and Execution

**Typical horizon: Days to weeks, sometimes months**

Detailed activities and actual execution.

Examples:

- MPS.
- MRP.
- Production Scheduling.
- Daily production control.
- S&OE.

These time horizons are illustrative, not universal. Actual ranges depend on the industry, company structure, product complexity, and planning frequency.

### A. Strategic Planning

**Definition:** Strategic Planning establishes the long-term direction, objectives, and major business decisions of an organization.

Examples:

- Entering a new market.
- Building a new manufacturing plant.
- Investing in additional production equipment.
- Developing new product categories.
- Increasing market share.

**Main concern:** What should the company achieve in the long term, and what resources or capabilities will be needed?

### B. Tactical Planning

**Definition:** Tactical Planning translates strategic objectives into coordinated medium-term plans that guide business functions.

Examples:

- Monthly sales forecasts.
- Production volume plans.
- Inventory targets.
- Workforce requirements.
- Supplier capacity arrangements.

S&OP belongs primarily to this level.

**Main concern:** How will the company coordinate its departments to achieve its business objectives?

### C. Operational Planning

**Definition:** Operational Planning translates higher-level plans into detailed activities, schedules, and execution requirements.

Examples:

- Daily production orders.
- Machine assignments.
- Material replenishment.
- Shift allocation.
- Production sequencing.

**Main concern:** What exactly needs to happen, when, where, and using which resources?

### 3.2 Why are these levels interconnected?

Let's say management decides to expand production capacity by 20%.

That strategic decision creates consequences at lower planning levels:

1. Strategic Planning establishes the expansion objective.
2. AOP allocates the annual investment budget.
3. S&OP evaluates future demand and capacity requirements.
4. Supply Planning identifies the capacity gap.
5. MPS determines the required production quantities.
6. MRP calculates additional material requirements.
7. Production Scheduling allocates available machines and shifts.

A decision made at the strategic level can therefore affect thousands of operational activities.

Likewise, actual operational problems can reveal that the original business assumptions need to change.

Planning is not a one-way process. It involves continuous feedback.

# 4. Detailed Discussion

Now let's compare the individual planning processes. This is the most important section because maraming processes ang may overlapping responsibilities.

## 4.1 S&OP vs. Demand Planning

### A. What is Demand Planning?

**Demand Planning** is the process of estimating future customer demand for a company's products or services.

In simple terms:

Demand Planning tries to answer:

"How many products are customers likely to buy in the future?"

It considers information such as:

- Historical sales.
- Customer orders.
- Sales forecasts.
- Market trends.
- Seasonality.
- Promotions.
- New product introductions.
- Customer business plans.

### B. How is a demand forecast developed?

Let's say your company manufactures electronic components.

Historical monthly demand:

| Month | Actual demand |
|---|---:|
| January | 8,000 |
| February | 9,000 |
| March | 10,000 |
| April | 11,000 |

Your Demand Planner may observe that demand is increasing.

Using historical data, sales input, and other assumptions, the planner estimates that May demand will be 12,000 units.

**Demand Planning output**

**12,000 units**

*Illustrative May demand forecast.*

But remember:

A forecast is an estimate, not a confirmed customer order.

### C. What does S&OP do differently?

Suppose the forecast indicates 12,000 units, but the factory can only produce 10,000 units using its currently available resources.

Demand Planning identifies the expected demand.

S&OP evaluates what the company should do about it.

Possible decisions:

1. Increase production capacity.
2. Use overtime.
3. Build inventory in advance.
4. Negotiate delivery schedules with customers.
5. Prioritize specific customers or products.
6. Accept a temporary demand-supply gap.

| Demand Planning | S&OP |
|---|---|
| Estimates future demand | Aligns demand with supply and business objectives |
| Focuses on customer requirements | Considers the entire business |
| Produces a demand forecast | Produces an agreed business plan |
| Primarily demand-focused | Cross-functional and financially aligned |
| Provides demand assumptions | Makes decisions about demand-supply imbalances |

**Key distinction:**

Demand Planning tells the company what customers are expected to need.

S&OP determines how the company will respond to that expected demand.

---

## 4.2 S&OP vs. Supply Planning

### A. What is Supply Planning?

**Supply Planning** is the process of determining how the company can provide the products required to meet expected demand.

It evaluates the availability and utilization of resources.

These include:

- Production capacity.
- Labor availability.
- Raw materials.
- Supplier capacity.
- Inventory.
- Manufacturing lead times.
- Transportation and distribution capabilities.

Its central question is:

"Can we produce and deliver what the business expects customers to need?"

### B. Example

Let's assume Demand Planning provides this forecast:

| Product | Forecast demand |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 |
| Product B | 3,000 |
| Product C | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** |

Supply Planning evaluates the available resources.

| Product | Demand | Available production capacity |
|---|---:|---:|
| Product A | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| Product B | 3,000 | 2,500 |
| Product C | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **9,500** |

There is a production capacity shortage of 500 units.

Supply Planning identifies this constraint.

But it does not necessarily have the authority to decide the final business trade-off.

### C. What does S&OP do?

S&OP brings together the relevant departments to evaluate the available alternatives.

| Alternative | Possible consequence |
|---|---|
| Add overtime | Higher labor cost |
| Add another shift | Higher operating expenses |
| Subcontract production | Additional supplier cost and quality considerations |
| Prioritize Product B customers | Possible customer service impact |
| Increase inventory before peak demand | Higher inventory carrying cost |

The S&OP team evaluates which alternative provides the best overall business outcome.

| Supply Planning | S&OP |
|---|---|
| Determines available supply capability | Reconciles supply with demand |
| Evaluates capacity and materials | Evaluates business trade-offs |
| Identifies resource constraints | Facilitates decisions on constraints |
| Develops supply scenarios | Approves or agrees on a feasible plan through the appropriate governance |
| Provides supply assumptions | Integrates operational and financial considerations |

**Key distinction:**

Supply Planning determines what the company can supply under certain assumptions.

S&OP determines how the business will balance that supply capability against demand and business objectives.

### Important professional concept: Constrained vs. unconstrained planning

You may encounter these terms in actual S&OP work.

- **Unconstrained Demand:** Expected customer demand without considering the company's supply limitations.
- **Constrained Supply:** The amount the company can realistically produce and deliver based on available resources.

For example:

- Unconstrained demand: 10,000 units.
- Available supply: 9,500 units.
- Demand-supply gap: 500 units.

The purpose of S&OP is not simply to make the forecast match available supply.

It is to identify the gap, evaluate alternatives, and agree on the appropriate business response.

---

## 4.3 S&OP vs. Production Planning and Production Scheduling

This distinction is especially important in manufacturing.

Both processes deal with production, but they operate at different levels of detail.

### A. Production Planning

**Production Planning** determines the required production quantities and resources needed to meet demand.

It answers questions such as:

- How many units should we manufacture?
- What production capacity is required?
- How much labor is needed?
- When should production take place?
- Do we need additional shifts?

It translates business requirements into manufacturing plans.

### B. Production Scheduling

**Production Scheduling** determines the specific sequence and timing of production activities.

It answers:

- Which production order should run first?
- Which machine will process the order?
- When will the machine start?
- How long will the production run take?
- When should the machine be changed over?
- Which operator or shift will handle the work?

### C. Example: Electronics manufacturing

Suppose the agreed monthly production plan is:

| Product family | Monthly production target |
|---|---:|
| Product A | 50,000 |
| Product B | 30,000 |
| Product C | 20,000 |
| **Total** | **100,000** |

At the production planning level, these are the required production volumes.

But the factory still needs to determine the actual machine assignments.

Suppose Machine 1 can produce Products A and B, while Machine 2 can produce Products B and C.

Production Scheduling might create this schedule:

| Day | Machine | Product | Production quantity |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Monday | Machine 1 | Product A | 5,000 |
| Monday | Machine 2 | Product C | 2,000 |
| Tuesday | Machine 1 | Product B | 3,000 |
| Tuesday | Machine 2 | Product C | 2,000 |
| Wednesday | Machine 1 | Product A | 5,000 |

These are illustrative figures.

Notice the difference:

The higher-level plan establishes the production requirements.

The detailed schedule determines how the factory will physically execute them.

### D. Aggregate production planning vs. detailed production scheduling

**Aggregate Production Planning**

- Focuses on product families or groups.
- Uses summarized production quantities.
- Evaluates overall capacity and resource requirements.
- Usually works over a medium-term horizon.

**Detailed Production Scheduling**

- Focuses on individual products or production orders.
- Assigns machines and time slots.
- Considers changeover time, machine availability, and sequencing.
- Works at a much more detailed level.

### E. Why doesn't S&OP determine every machine's daily sequence?

Because S&OP is designed to make business-level decisions, not manage every production transaction.

Imagine a factory with:

- 50 production machines.
- 20 product models.
- 3 shifts per day.
- Hundreds of production orders per week.

It would be inefficient to discuss every individual production order during an S&OP meeting.

Instead, S&OP establishes the agreed production volume and capacity requirements.

Production Planning and Scheduling translate these requirements into executable activities.

**Remember:** S&OP decides the business-level production direction. Detailed production planning and scheduling determine how that direction will be executed.

---

## 4.4 S&OP vs. MPS (Master Production Scheduling)

This is one of the most important relationships in manufacturing planning.

### A. What is MPS?

**MPS stands for Master Production Schedule.**

It is a detailed production plan that specifies which finished products should be manufactured, how many units are needed, and when they should be available.

In simple terms:

S&OP might say:

"We need to produce 100,000 units of this product family next month."

MPS translates that into:

"We need to produce 20,000 units of Model A in Week 1, 25,000 in Week 2, and so on."

### B. Example

Suppose S&OP establishes this monthly plan:

| Product family | Monthly volume |
|---|---:|
| MLCC Family A | 100,000 |
| MLCC Family B | 60,000 |
| **Total** | **160,000** |

At this level, the company is managing product families.

However, Product Family A contains three individual models:

- Model A1
- Model A2
- Model A3

The MPS may distribute the volume as follows:

| Model | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A1 | 10,000 | 12,000 | 10,000 | 8,000 | 40,000 |
| A2 | 15,000 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 10,000 | 50,000 |
| A3 | 2,000 | 3,000 | 2,000 | 3,000 | 10,000 |
| **Total** | **27,000** | **25,000** | **27,000** | **21,000** | **100,000** |

This is a simplified illustration of how an aggregate product-family plan can be translated into individual end-item requirements.

### C. S&OP vs. MPS

| S&OP | MPS |
|---|---|
| Manages aggregate business plans | Manages detailed finished-product plans |
| May plan at product-family level | Plans at individual end-item level |
| Evaluates demand, supply, capacity, and finance | Determines production quantities and timing |
| Supports management-level decisions | Supports detailed manufacturing planning |
| Usually medium-term | Usually shorter-term and more detailed |

### D. How does MPS connect to S&OP?

The relationship works in both directions.

**Downward flow:**

S&OP establishes aggregate production requirements.

MPS translates these into detailed finished-product requirements.

**Upward feedback:**

MPS may identify that the proposed production plan is not feasible because of:

- Machine capacity limitations.
- Production lead times.
- Product-specific constraints.
- Material availability.
- Production changeover requirements.

These constraints may require changes to the supply plan or escalation to S&OP.

### Important distinction

S&OP and MPS must remain aligned.

If S&OP establishes a production target of 100,000 units, but the MPS consistently plans 120,000 units without an approved reason, the planning system has become misaligned.

That can lead to unnecessary inventory, excess production, and additional working capital requirements.

## 4.5 S&OP vs. MRP (Material Requirements Planning)

Now that we understand MPS, we can introduce MRP.

### A. What is MRP?

**MRP stands for Material Requirements Planning.**

MRP is a system or planning process that calculates the materials and components required to support a production plan.

Its primary question is:

"What materials do we need, how much do we need, and when do we need them?"

It uses three fundamental inputs:

1. **Master Production Schedule:** What finished products must be manufactured?
2. **Bill of Materials (BOM):** What materials and components are required to manufacture each product?
3. **Inventory Records:** How much material is currently available or already expected to arrive?

### B. Understanding the Bill of Materials

A **Bill of Materials (BOM)** is a structured list of materials and components required to manufacture a product.

Let's assume one finished product requires:

| Material | Quantity per finished unit |
|---|---:|
| Material A | 2 pieces |
| Material B | 3 pieces |
| Material C | 1 piece |

If the MPS requires 1,000 finished products, the gross material requirements are:

| Material | Quantity per product | Production requirement | Gross requirement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 2 | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| B | 3 | 1,000 | 3,000 |
| C | 1 | 1,000 | 1,000 |

But the company already has materials in inventory.

Suppose:

| Material | Gross requirement | Available inventory | Net requirement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 2,000 | 500 | 1,500 |
| B | 3,000 | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| C | 1,000 | 200 | 800 |

MRP calculates the net requirements.

This helps determine what needs to be purchased or manufactured.

### C. MRP also considers timing

MRP does not only calculate quantities.

It also considers:

- Material lead times.
- Scheduled receipts.
- Planned order receipts.
- Planned order releases.
- Lot-sizing rules.
- Safety stock and other inventory parameters.

For example:

If Material A requires 10 days of procurement lead time, and the production order needs the material by December 20, the purchase order may need to be released around December 10, subject to the company's planning rules and calendar.

### D. S&OP vs. MRP

| S&OP | MRP |
|---|---|
| Aligns business demand and supply | Calculates detailed material requirements |
| Works with aggregate volumes | Works with individual materials and components |
| Evaluates overall material availability and constraints | Calculates quantities and timing of replenishment |
| Supports strategic and tactical trade-offs | Supports procurement and production orders |
| Cross-functional management process | Detailed material planning process |

### E. How does S&OP influence MRP?

Let's say S&OP approves an increase in production from 100,000 to 120,000 units.

That increase affects:

1. The MPS production requirements.
2. The BOM-based material requirements.
3. The procurement requirements.
4. The supplier delivery schedules.
5. The inventory replenishment plan.

In other words, a business-level decision made through S&OP can create thousands of additional material requirements in MRP.

**Important:** MRP calculates requirements based on its planning inputs and settings. It does not independently decide whether increasing production is financially or commercially desirable.

## 4.6 S&OP vs. S&OE (Sales and Operations Execution)

This distinction is particularly important for an S&OP Analyst.

### A. What is S&OE?

**S&OE stands for Sales and Operations Execution.**

It is the short-term process of monitoring and managing actual business execution against the agreed plans.

Its main question is:

"Are we executing the plan as expected, and what should we do about deviations?"

While S&OP focuses on medium-term alignment, S&OE focuses on short-term execution.

### B. Example

Suppose the agreed S&OP plan is:

- Monthly production target: 100,000 units.
- Monthly demand: 100,000 units.
- Planned delivery: 100,000 units.

However, during Week 2:

- A critical machine breaks down.
- Production loses 2,000 units.
- A supplier delays a raw material delivery.
- One important customer requests an earlier shipment.

These issues require immediate attention.

The S&OE team evaluates:

- Which production orders are affected?
- Which customer deliveries are at risk?
- Is there available inventory?
- Can another machine produce the affected products?
- Can the production sequence be changed?
- Does the customer need to be informed?

These are execution-level decisions.

### C. When does an issue become an S&OP issue?

Not every operational problem needs to be discussed in S&OP.

Consider two situations:

**Situation 1: Temporary machine breakdown**

A machine is unavailable for four hours, but the factory can recover the lost production within the same week.

This is typically an S&OE or production-control issue.

**Situation 2: Persistent capacity shortage**

A production line is consistently unable to meet demand for the next six months.

This is no longer just a daily execution issue.

It may require:

- Additional equipment.
- Overtime arrangements.
- Outsourcing.
- Inventory strategy changes.
- Customer allocation decisions.

This becomes a potential S&OP issue.

### D. S&OP vs. S&OE

| S&OP | S&OE |
|---|---|
| Tactical planning | Short-term execution |
| Focuses on future business alignment | Focuses on actual operational performance |
| Evaluates medium-term demand and supply | Handles immediate deviations |
| Involves management-level trade-offs | Involves operational coordination |
| Establishes an agreed plan | Protects and executes the agreed plan |

### E. How do they work together?

The relationship is continuous:

1. S&OP establishes the agreed plan.
2. S&OE monitors actual performance.
3. S&OE identifies deviations.
4. The team resolves immediate issues.
5. Persistent or significant issues are escalated.
6. S&OP incorporates relevant information into future planning cycles.

**Remember this distinction:**

S&OP asks, "What should our business plan be?"

S&OE asks, "How do we execute that plan despite what is happening right now?"

## 4.7 S&OP vs. Business Planning and Financial Planning

S&OP is not exclusively an operational planning process.

It also needs to connect operational decisions with financial objectives.

### A. Strategic Business Planning

This establishes the company's long-term direction.

Example:

- Increase revenue by 20% over three years.
- Expand into new markets.
- Increase production capability.
- Improve profitability.

### B. Annual Operating Plan (AOP)

**AOP stands for Annual Operating Plan.**

It translates the company's strategic direction into an annual operating plan with specific targets.

These may include:

- Annual revenue.
- Sales volume.
- Production volume.
- Operating expenses.
- Headcount.
- Capital expenditures.
- Profit targets.

AOP is typically an annual planning exercise, although companies may revise it.

### C. Financial Planning

Financial Planning evaluates the monetary implications of business activities.

It considers:

- Revenue.
- Cost of goods sold.
- Operating expenses.
- Gross profit.
- Operating profit.
- Cash flow.
- Working capital.
- Inventory value.

### D. How does S&OP connect to financial planning?

Let's say the company expects to sell 100,000 units.

Assumptions:

- Selling price: ₱100 per unit.
- Variable production cost: ₱60 per unit.

Expected revenue:

\[
100,000 \times ₱100 = ₱10,000,000
\]

Expected variable production cost:

\[
100,000 \times ₱60 = ₱6,000,000
\]

Expected contribution before other costs:

\[
₱10,000,000-₱6,000,000=₱4,000,000
\]

Now suppose the company needs additional overtime to meet demand.

The overtime increases the production cost by ₱300,000.

The revised contribution becomes:

\[
₱4,000,000-₱300,000=₱3,700,000
\]

Operationally, the company can meet demand.

Financially, however, the additional production has a cost.

This is why Finance must participate in S&OP.

### E. Operational volume vs. financial value

A volume plan might look good because it meets customer demand.

But it may not be financially attractive.

For example:

| Business indicator | Scenario A | Scenario B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales volume | 100,000 | 110,000 |
| Revenue | ₱10M | ₱11M |
| Production cost | ₱6M | ₱6.9M |
| Additional overtime | ₱0 | ₱0.3M |
| Contribution | ₱4M | ₱3.8M |

Scenario B sells more units but generates a lower contribution under these assumptions.

This demonstrates why S&OP must consider more than volume.

**Key distinction:**

- Business Planning establishes where the company wants to go.
- Financial Planning evaluates the financial targets and implications.
- S&OP connects those objectives with an operationally feasible plan.

# 5. Practical Examples and Applications

Let's combine everything using one realistic manufacturing scenario.

## 5.1 Scenario: Electronic Component Manufacturing Company

Imagine a company manufacturing MLCC products.

The company has three product families:

- Family A
- Family B
- Family C

Management wants to increase annual revenue and prepare for higher customer demand.

The company uses different planning processes to translate this objective into actual manufacturing activities.

### Step 1: Strategic Business Planning

Management decides:

- Increase market share.
- Expand manufacturing capacity.
- Improve customer service.
- Increase annual revenue.

These are long-term business objectives.

### Step 2: Annual Operating Plan

The company establishes the following annual targets:

| Indicator | Annual target |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | ₱120 million |
| Production volume | 1.2 million units |
| Customer service target | 98% |
| Inventory value | ₱15 million |

These are illustrative management targets.

### Step 3: Demand Planning

The Demand Planning team forecasts the next month's requirements.

| Product family | Forecast demand |
|---|---:|
| Family A | 50,000 |
| Family B | 30,000 |
| Family C | 20,000 |
| **Total** | **100,000** |

The forecast is based on historical demand, customer input, and other relevant assumptions.

### Step 4: Supply Planning

Operations evaluates whether the factory can support 100,000 units.

| Resource | Required | Available |
|---|---:|---:|
| Production capacity | 100,000 | 95,000 |
| Raw materials | 100,000 equivalent units | 105,000 equivalent units |
| Labor capacity | 100,000 equivalent units | 100,000 equivalent units |

The main constraint is production capacity.

There is a shortage of 5,000 units.

### Step 5: S&OP

The cross-functional team reviews the situation.

Possible alternatives:

- Increase overtime.
- Use available alternative production equipment.
- Build inventory in advance.
- Negotiate customer delivery dates.

After evaluating costs, capacity, and customer priorities, the authorized management team agrees to use overtime to increase available capacity to 100,000 units.

The agreed S&OP plan is now:

**Agreed S&OP plan**

**100,000 units**

Monthly production and supply plan:

- Family A: 50,000
- Family B: 30,000
- Family C: 20,000

*Illustrative plan after capacity adjustment.*

### Step 6: MPS

The Master Production Schedule converts the family-level plan into individual finished-product requirements.

For example:

| Model | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 | Week 4 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A1 | 10,000 | 12,000 | 13,000 | 15,000 |
| A2 | 5,000 | 5,000 | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| B1 | 8,000 | 7,000 | 8,000 | 7,000 |
| C1 | 5,000 | 5,000 | 5,000 | 5,000 |

The example is a simplified product-level distribution.

### Step 7: MRP

The MRP system uses:

- The MPS.
- Product BOMs.
- Available inventory.
- Scheduled material receipts.
- Procurement lead times.

It calculates the material requirements and generates planned replenishment recommendations.

### Step 8: Production Scheduling

The production team assigns:

- Specific production machines.
- Production orders.
- Manufacturing sequences.
- Start and completion times.
- Shift assignments.

For example, Machine 1 produces A1 first, followed by A2, while Machine 2 handles B1.

### Step 9: S&OE

During execution, a supplier delays Material A by two days.

The S&OE team evaluates whether the affected production orders can be rescheduled.

If available inventory can cover the shortage, the issue may be resolved operationally.

If the shortage affects the entire month's production plan, it may need escalation to S&OP.

### Step 10: Actual performance feedback

At the end of the month, the company reviews its performance.

| Indicator | Plan | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Production | 100,000 | 96,000 |
| Demand | 100,000 | 100,000 |
| Production shortfall | 0 | 4,000 |

The company investigates the 4,000-unit shortfall.

Possible causes:

- Machine downtime.
- Material shortages.
- Lower production yield.
- Unplanned changeovers.
- Labor constraints.

These findings become valuable inputs for the next planning cycle.

The next S&OP cycle can then consider whether its capacity assumptions need to change.

# 6. Formulas and Calculations

This topic is primarily about understanding planning processes, so we do not need to introduce complicated mathematical models.

However, several simple calculations help demonstrate the relationship between planning levels.

## 6.1 Demand-Supply Gap

**Formula:**

\[
\text{Gap}=\text{Demand}-\text{Available Supply}
\]

Example:

- Demand = 100,000 units.
- Available supply = 95,000 units.

\[
100,000-95,000=5,000
\]

**Supply shortage**

The company has a 5,000-unit shortage.

This information is used in Supply Planning and S&OP.

## 6.2 Gross Material Requirements

**Formula:**

\[
\text{Gross Requirements}
=
\text{Production Quantity}
\times
\text{BOM Quantity}
\]

Example:

- Production requirement = 10,000 units.
- Material requirement = 3 pieces per unit.

\[
10,000\times3=30,000
\]

Gross material requirement = 30,000 pieces.

## 6.3 Net Material Requirements

Simplified formula:

\[
\text{Net Requirements}
=
\text{Gross Requirements}
-
\text{Available Supply}
\]

Example:

- Gross requirement = 30,000.
- Available inventory and applicable scheduled receipts = 8,000.

\[
30,000-8,000=22,000
\]

Net requirement = 22,000 pieces.

In actual MRP, the calculation also considers timing, safety stock, reservations, and other planning parameters.

## 6.4 Plan Attainment

This helps measure actual production performance against the agreed plan.

\[
\text{Plan Attainment}
=
\frac{\text{Actual Production}}{\text{Planned Production}}
\times100
\]

Example:

- Planned production = 100,000 units.
- Actual production = 96,000 units.

\[
\frac{96,000}{100,000}\times100=96\%
\]

Plan attainment = 96%.

Operational interpretation:

The factory achieved 96% of its production target.

However, this does not automatically mean that customer service was 96%. Production attainment and customer delivery performance are different KPIs.

# 7. Business Perspective and Decision-Making

Now let's move from understanding the processes to understanding how an S&OP Analyst uses them.

## 7.1 Where does an S&OP Analyst fit?

An S&OP Analyst often works across the boundaries of these planning processes.

The analyst may not personally create every demand forecast, release every purchase order, or prepare every production schedule.

Instead, the analyst helps ensure that information from different departments is consistent, accurate, and aligned.

Typical responsibilities may include:

| Planning process | How an S&OP Analyst may support it |
|---|---|
| Strategic Planning | Understand business priorities and assumptions |
| AOP | Compare current plans against annual targets |
| Demand Planning | Consolidate forecasts and analyze demand changes |
| Supply Planning | Analyze capacity, inventory, and supply constraints |
| S&OP | Prepare reports, reconcile plans, and support decisions |
| MPS | Check alignment between aggregate and detailed production plans |
| MRP | Monitor material constraints and their business impact |
| Production Scheduling | Understand execution constraints and deviations |
| S&OE | Track short-term performance and escalations |

The exact responsibilities depend on the company's organizational structure.

## 7.2 How planning misalignment affects the business

Consider these examples:

| Planning misalignment | Potential business consequence |
|---|---|
| Demand forecast is too high | Excess inventory and unnecessary production |
| Demand forecast is too low | Stockouts and lost sales |
| S&OP plan exceeds actual capacity | Production delays |
| MPS is inconsistent with S&OP | Unplanned production and inventory imbalance |
| MRP uses incorrect inventory records | Incorrect purchase recommendations |
| Production schedule ignores material availability | Machine idle time |
| S&OE does not escalate recurring problems | Repeated operational failures |
| Financial plan is disconnected from S&OP | Revenue and profitability targets may become unrealistic |

## 7.3 The importance of planning alignment

A company can have excellent individual departments but still perform poorly if their plans are disconnected.

For example:

- Sales forecasts 120,000 units.
- Operations can produce only 90,000.
- Procurement purchases materials for 130,000.
- Finance budgets for 100,000.

Every department may have completed its own planning activity, but the company does not have one aligned business plan.

This is one of the central problems S&OP is designed to address.

**Professional insight:** S&OP is not simply another planning spreadsheet or monthly meeting. It is a cross-functional management process that creates alignment, exposes trade-offs, and supports decisions across the business.

# 8. Comparison of Planning Processes

This is your reference table for the entire topic. I recommend becoming familiar with these differences because you will encounter these terms frequently in actual S&OP work.

## 8.1 Comprehensive comparison

| Process | Main purpose | Typical horizon | Level of detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic Business Planning | Establish long-term direction | Several years | Business-wide |
| AOP | Establish annual targets and budgets | Annual | Department/business |
| Demand Planning | Forecast customer demand | Months to over a year | Product/customer |
| Supply Planning | Determine feasible supply | Weeks to months or longer | Product family/resource |
| S&OP | Align demand, supply, and financial plans | Commonly 12–24 months | Aggregate/product family |
| MPS | Plan finished-product production | Weeks to months | Individual end item |
| MRP | Calculate material requirements | Weeks to months | Individual material/component |
| Production Scheduling | Sequence production activities | Hours to weeks | Machine/order |
| S&OE | Manage short-term execution | Days to weeks | Operational |

These are illustrative planning horizons, not universal standards. Some companies use shorter or longer horizons depending on their business model, product lifecycle, supply chain complexity, and industry.

## 8.2 Responsibilities, outputs, and S&OP relationships

| Process | Primary participants | Main output | Relationship with S&OP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic Business Planning | Executive Management, Strategy, Finance | Long-term strategy | Provides strategic direction |
| AOP | Management, Finance, Department Heads | Annual targets and budget | Provides financial and operating targets |
| Demand Planning | Sales, Marketing, Demand Planners | Demand forecast | Provides demand input |
| Supply Planning | Operations, Manufacturing, Procurement | Supply and capacity plan | Provides supply input and constraints |
| S&OP | Sales, Operations, Finance, Procurement, Management | Agreed integrated business plan | Integrates and reconciles plans |
| MPS | Production Planning, Operations | Master Production Schedule | Translates S&OP volumes into end-item requirements |
| MRP | Material Planners, Procurement, Production Planning | Material requirements and planned orders | Converts production requirements into material needs |
| Production Scheduling | Production Control, Supervisors, Operations | Detailed production schedule | Executes lower-level production requirements |
| S&OE | Sales, Operations, Planning, Procurement | Short-term recovery and execution actions | Provides execution feedback and escalations |

### 8.3 Important terminology clarification

Different companies may use different names for similar activities.

For example:

- Some companies combine Demand Planning and Supply Planning under one planning organization.
- Some use Integrated Business Planning (IBP) instead of, or as an expanded form of, S&OP.
- Some integrate S&OE within daily production control.
- Some companies perform detailed production planning and scheduling within one department.

The terminology and reporting structure may vary.

What matters is understanding the purpose, decision authority, level of detail, and output of each process.

# 9. How All Planning Processes Work Together

Let's now connect everything into one complete planning architecture.

## 9.1 The overall information and decision flow

1. **Strategic Business Planning:** Long-term direction and business objectives.
2. **Annual Operating Plan (AOP):** Annual financial and operating targets.
3. **Demand Planning:** What customers are expected to need.
4. **Supply Planning:** What the company can provide.
5. **S&OP:** Integrated business plan, combining demand, supply, financial alignment, and management decisions.
6. **MPS:** Finished-product production requirements.
7. **MRP:** Material and component requirements.
8. **Production Scheduling:** Machine assignments, production orders, and timing.
9. **Production Execution:** Actual manufacturing and delivery activities.
10. **S&OE:** Monitor deviations, coordinate recovery, and escalate problems.
11. **Feedback into future planning cycles:** Actual demand, production, inventory, delivery, and financial performance.

Important: This is a simplified conceptual flow, not a rigid software workflow. Actual systems often run MPS, MRP, supply planning, and execution activities in parallel or through iterative feedback.

## 9.2 Understanding the information flow

Let's break down the flow.

**A. Strategic to Tactical**

Management establishes business objectives.

These objectives influence the AOP and provide direction to S&OP.

**B. Demand and Supply to S&OP**

Demand Planning provides the expected demand.

Supply Planning evaluates whether the business can support that demand.

Finance evaluates the financial implications.

S&OP brings these inputs together and facilitates management decisions.

**C. S&OP to Detailed Planning**

The agreed aggregate production and inventory plans guide detailed planning.

MPS translates product-family requirements into individual finished-product requirements.

MRP calculates the corresponding material requirements.

**D. Detailed Planning to Execution**

Production Scheduling assigns actual production activities.

Manufacturing executes the plan.

**E. Execution to S&OE**

S&OE monitors short-term performance and coordinates corrective actions.

**F. Execution Feedback to S&OP**

Actual performance, recurring constraints, and changes in demand become inputs for future planning cycles.

This is how the company maintains alignment between its business objectives and actual operations.

# 10. Key Takeaways

Before proceeding to the exercises, let's consolidate the most important concepts.

## 10.1 The nine processes you must understand

| Process | Remember this |
|---|---|
| Strategic Planning | Establishes where the business wants to go. |
| AOP | Establishes annual business and financial targets. |
| Demand Planning | Estimates what customers will need. |
| Supply Planning | Evaluates what the company can provide. |
| S&OP | Aligns demand, supply, and financial objectives into one agreed plan. |
| MPS | Determines which finished products to manufacture and when. |
| MRP | Calculates the materials required for production. |
| Production Scheduling | Determines which machines and production orders will run and when. |
| S&OE | Manages short-term deviations and execution issues. |

## 10.2 Five important distinctions

**1. Forecasting is not the same as planning.**

Demand Planning forecasts customer requirements. S&OP uses that forecast to develop an agreed business plan.

**2. Supply Planning is not the same as S&OP.**

Supply Planning evaluates available supply capability. S&OP reconciles that capability with demand and business objectives.

**3. Aggregate production planning is not detailed production scheduling.**

S&OP establishes aggregate requirements. Production Scheduling determines actual machine assignments and production sequences.

**4. MPS is not MRP.**

MPS plans finished products. MRP calculates the materials needed to manufacture those products.

**5. S&OP is not S&OE.**

S&OP aligns the medium-term business plan. S&OE manages short-term execution.

## 10.3 The most important concept

**Think of the entire planning system as a translation process.**

- Strategic Planning translates business vision into objectives.
- AOP translates objectives into annual targets.
- Demand and Supply Planning translate market expectations and resource capabilities into planning inputs.
- S&OP translates these inputs into an agreed business plan.
- MPS and MRP translate the plan into production and material requirements.
- Production Scheduling translates requirements into actual activities.
- S&OE ensures that execution stays aligned despite operational changes.

**Every planning process has a different responsibility, but all must work toward the same business objectives.**
