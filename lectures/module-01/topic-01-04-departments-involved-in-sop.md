# Module 1 – S&OP Fundamentals
## Topic 1.4: Departments Involved in Sales & Operations Planning (S&OP)

**Level 1 → Level 2 → Level 3** · **Beginner to Professional**

**Main Learning Objective:** Understand how different departments work together to create one aligned, realistic, and financially achievable business plan.

---

# 1. Topic Overview

## 1.1 What are we going to learn?

Sa previous topics, na-establish natin ang foundation ng Sales & Operations Planning:

- **Topic 1.1:** What is S&OP? Na-introduce natin ang S&OP as a business planning process that aligns demand, supply, and financial objectives.
- **Topic 1.2:** Why does S&OP exist? We discussed how S&OP helps companies improve coordination, manage conflicts, and make better business decisions.
- **Topic 1.3:** Understanding Demand and Supply. We learned that demand represents what customers need or want, while supply represents the company's ability to fulfill that demand.

Ngayon, ang next question natin:

**Sino-sino ang mga taong responsible for making S&OP work?**

Hindi kasi isang department lang ang may complete information para gumawa ng effective business plan.

For example:

- Sales knows what customers want.
- Manufacturing knows how much the factory can produce.
- Procurement knows whether materials are available.
- Finance knows whether the proposed plan makes financial sense.
- Management decides which business priorities should take precedence.

Each department has a different perspective, different information, and different objectives.

Ang challenge ay pagsamahin ang lahat ng ito into one coordinated plan.

## 1.2 Why is this topic important?

Imagine a company that receives a sudden increase in customer orders.

Sales immediately wants to accept the additional orders because this means more revenue.

However:

- Manufacturing might not have enough production capacity.
- Procurement might not have enough raw materials.
- Warehouse might not have enough inventory.
- Finance might not have enough budget for additional materials and overtime.

If Sales makes a decision without consulting these departments, the company might promise something it cannot deliver.

This is exactly why S&OP requires cross-functional collaboration.

## 1.3 Connection to the overall S&OP process

The relationship is straightforward:

| Previous topic | Connection to Topic 1.4 |
|---|---|
| 1.1 What is S&OP? | Establishes the planning process. |
| 1.2 Purpose and Benefits | Explains why departments must coordinate. |
| 1.3 Demand and Supply | Establishes what the departments need to balance. |
| 1.4 Departments Involved | Identifies who contributes information and makes decisions. |

**The central idea:** S&OP is not simply a planning activity. It is a coordinated business process involving multiple functions.

# 2. Layman's Explanation

## 2.1 Understanding S&OP through a simple example

Imagine that you own an electronic components manufacturing company.

Your company manufactures electronic components used in different consumer and industrial products.

One of your biggest customers suddenly informs you:

"We need 20,000 additional units next month. Can you supply them?"

Your company now needs to determine whether it can fulfill that request.

Let's see how different departments respond.

### The customer's request

**+20,000 units**

*Illustrative additional customer demand for next month*

1. **Sales**

   "The customer needs 20,000 additional units. We need to accommodate the order."

2. **Demand Planning**

   "We need to update our demand forecast to reflect the additional requirement."

3. **Manufacturing**

   "We need to check if our machines, manpower, and production schedule can support the additional volume."

4. **Procurement**

   "We need to check whether suppliers can provide the additional raw materials on time."

5. **Warehouse / Inventory**

   "Let's check our existing inventory. We might already have some units available."

6. **Finance**

   "How much additional cost will this create? Will the additional revenue justify the expense?"

7. **Senior Management**

   "Considering all the information, what is the best business decision?"

Notice something?

Walang department na kayang sagutin nang mag-isa ang question:

"Can the company fulfill the additional 20,000 units?"

Sales knows the customer requirement, but not necessarily the production capability.

Manufacturing knows production capability, but not necessarily the customer's priorities.

Finance understands the financial impact, but cannot independently determine whether the production line can accommodate the additional demand.

Kailangan nilang magtulungan.

## 2.2 What is cross-functional collaboration?

Let's introduce two important terms.

**Cross-functional** means involving different departments or business functions within an organization.

**Collaboration** means working together, sharing information, and coordinating activities to achieve a common objective.

Therefore:

**Cross-functional collaboration** means different departments working together, combining their information and expertise to achieve a shared business objective.

In S&OP, the shared objective is to develop a business plan that balances:

- Customer demand
- Production and supply capabilities
- Inventory requirements
- Financial objectives
- Overall business priorities

## 2.3 Why can't one department handle everything?

Let's consider what happens if each department works independently.

| Department | If working independently |
|---|---|
| Sales | May promise unrealistic delivery dates. |
| Manufacturing | May produce products that are not aligned with actual customer demand. |
| Procurement | May purchase too many or too few materials. |
| Warehouse | May experience excess inventory or shortages. |
| Finance | May identify financial problems too late. |
| Management | May receive inconsistent information from different departments. |

This creates what we call **departmental silos**.

A departmental silo happens when departments operate independently and do not sufficiently share information or coordinate decisions.

The result?

Each department might achieve its own target, but the company as a whole may perform poorly.

For example, Manufacturing might achieve excellent production efficiency by producing large batches, but if customers need a different product mix, the company could end up with excess inventory.

This is an important S&OP principle:

**Optimizing one department does not automatically optimize the entire business.**

# 3. Technical Explanation

Now that we understand the basic concept, let's introduce the professional terminology used in actual S&OP environments.

## 3.1 The cross-functional structure of S&OP

In professional organizations, S&OP typically involves several interconnected business functions.

These functions can be grouped into five broad responsibilities:

| Functional group | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| Commercial | Understand and communicate market and customer demand. |
| Demand Planning | Translate available demand information into a consolidated demand plan. |
| Supply Planning | Determine how the company can fulfill the demand. |
| Financial Planning | Evaluate the financial implications of the proposed plans. |
| Executive Leadership | Resolve major conflicts and approve business direction. |

Other functions, such as Manufacturing, Procurement, Inventory Management, and Product Management, provide specialized information that supports these activities.

### Important terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Functional Department | A department responsible for a particular business activity. |
| Stakeholder | A person or group affected by, or involved in, a business decision. |
| Process Owner | The person accountable for managing and maintaining the overall S&OP process. |
| Demand Plan | A consolidated projection of expected customer demand. |
| Supply Plan | A plan describing how the company will meet the expected demand. |
| Capacity Constraint | A limitation that prevents operations from producing or supplying more. |
| Demand-Supply Gap | The difference between expected demand and available supply. |
| Financial Reconciliation | Evaluating whether the operational plans are consistent with financial objectives. |
| Executive Review | A management-level discussion where major issues and decisions are addressed. |

One important distinction:

**S&OP is a business process, not simply a meeting attended by department representatives.**

The meetings are only part of the process. The actual work involves gathering data, preparing plans, evaluating alternatives, and implementing decisions.

# 4. Detailed Discussion: Main Departments Involved in S&OP

Let's discuss each department individually.

For consistency, we will use four questions:

1. What does the department do?
2. What information does it contribute?
3. What decisions or responsibilities does it have?
4. Why is its participation important to S&OP?

## 4.1 A. Sales Department

**Commercial Function**

### What does Sales do?

Sales is responsible for understanding customer requirements and generating business revenue through product and service sales.

In S&OP, Sales provides information about what customers are expected to purchase.

Think of Sales as the department closest to the customers.

### Information contributed by Sales

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Customer Orders | Actual orders already received from customers. |
| Sales Forecast | Expected future sales based on customer and market information. |
| Customer Requirements | Required quantities, specifications, and delivery dates. |
| Customer Commitments | Agreements or promises made to customers. |
| New Customers | Expected demand from newly acquired accounts. |
| Promotions | Activities that may increase product demand. |
| Market Changes | Changes in customer behavior or purchasing patterns. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Communicate customer requirements**

Sales informs the planning team about what customers need and when they need it.

Example:

A customer normally purchases 10,000 units monthly but now expects to purchase 15,000 units next month.

Sales communicates this anticipated increase.

**2. Explain changes in demand**

Hindi lahat ng forecast changes ay random.

There may be specific reasons:

- A customer launched a new product.
- A competitor experienced a supply shortage.
- A customer increased its production capacity.
- A customer is preparing for a seasonal sales peak.

Sales helps explain the business reason behind the changes.

**3. Communicate customer priorities**

For example, two customers require products from the same production line.

- Customer A requires 10,000 units next week.
- Customer B requires 10,000 units next month.

Sales helps communicate which customer commitments are more urgent or commercially important.

### Why is Sales important?

Without Sales, the company might create a production plan based only on historical information.

However, historical sales do not always represent future customer requirements.

Sales provides forward-looking market intelligence that helps improve demand planning.

### Common mistake

Sales should not independently promise delivery dates or quantities without considering supply capabilities.

A customer commitment creates an operational obligation.

That is why Sales needs to coordinate with Supply Planning and Operations.

---

## 4.2 B. Demand Planning Department

**Demand Planning Function**

### What does Demand Planning do?

Demand Planning consolidates different sources of demand information and develops a structured demand forecast.

In simple terms:

Sales tells the company what customers might need.

Demand Planning organizes, analyzes, and translates that information into a consolidated demand plan.

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Historical Sales | Previous customer purchasing patterns. |
| Customer Orders | Existing confirmed demand. |
| Sales Forecast | Commercial team's expectations. |
| Statistical Forecast | Forecast generated using historical data and forecasting methods. |
| Forecast Adjustments | Changes made based on additional business information. |
| Demand Trends | Increasing, decreasing, or changing demand patterns. |
| Forecast Accuracy | Measurement of how close previous forecasts were to actual demand. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Consolidate demand information**

Imagine three Sales representatives provide their forecasts:

| Sales representative | Forecast |
|---|---:|
| Sales A | 10,000 |
| Sales B | 15,000 |
| Sales C | 5,000 |
| **Total** | **30,000** |

Demand Planning consolidates these numbers into one demand view.

**2. Analyze historical demand**

Demand Planners examine historical patterns to identify:

- Increasing demand
- Declining demand
- Seasonal demand
- Unusual demand spikes
- Irregular customer purchasing behavior

**3. Maintain the demand plan**

A demand plan is not created once and forgotten.

It needs to be updated when new information becomes available.

For example:

The original forecast was 30,000 units.

Sales communicates that a major customer increased its expected orders by 5,000 units.

Demand Planning evaluates and incorporates the change into the appropriate planning periods.

**4. Challenge unrealistic assumptions**

Demand Planning should not automatically accept every forecast adjustment.

For example:

Sales increases its forecast by 50%, but there are no confirmed customer orders or supporting market information.

Demand Planning should ask:

- What is the basis of the increase?
- Is it supported by customer information?
- Is it a temporary increase or a permanent trend?
- Has the same demand already been included elsewhere?

### Why is Demand Planning important?

Without a consolidated demand plan, different departments may work using different demand assumptions.

For example:

- Sales plans around 50,000 units.
- Manufacturing plans around 40,000 units.
- Procurement purchases materials for 60,000 units.

The company now has three different versions of expected demand.

Demand Planning helps establish a common demand reference for the organization.

---

## 4.3 C. Operations / Manufacturing Department

**Supply Execution Function**

### What does Manufacturing do?

Manufacturing is responsible for converting raw materials into finished products.

In S&OP, Manufacturing evaluates whether the production organization can support the demand plan.

Remember:

**Demand Planning determines what the company expects to sell. Manufacturing determines what the factory can realistically produce.**

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Production Capacity | Maximum production capability under defined operating assumptions. |
| Available Machine Hours | Machine time available for production. |
| Labor Availability | Available operators and technical personnel. |
| Production Efficiency | Actual production performance relative to expected performance. |
| Production Schedule | Planned production timing and sequence. |
| Downtime | Time when equipment is unavailable. |
| Yield | Percentage of input or production output that meets quality requirements. |
| Production Constraints | Factors limiting production output. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Evaluate production capacity**

Suppose the demand plan requires 50,000 units.

However, the manufacturing facility can only produce 42,000 units using its currently available resources.

There is a production capacity problem.

**2. Identify operational constraints**

Possible constraints include:

- Insufficient machine capacity
- Equipment breakdowns
- Preventive maintenance schedules
- Manpower shortages
- Long setup or changeover times
- Low production yield
- Quality problems

**3. Evaluate production alternatives**

Manufacturing might recommend:

- Additional overtime
- Extra production shifts
- Improved machine utilization
- Reduced changeover time
- Production schedule adjustments
- Outsourcing, if available and appropriate

**4. Assess production feasibility**

Hindi sapat na sabihin na kaya gumawa ng 50,000 units.

Manufacturing must determine whether the required volume can actually be achieved within the planning horizon.

### Why is Manufacturing important?

A demand plan without production feasibility is only an expectation.

Manufacturing helps transform that expectation into an executable production plan.

### Manufacturing example

Suppose your company operates an MLCC manufacturing facility.

A sudden increase in demand requires additional output.

The Manufacturing team needs to evaluate the effects on:

- Casting capacity
- Stacking capacity
- Cutting capacity
- Firing capacity
- Testing and sorting capacity

Even if Casting has sufficient capacity, Firing might become the bottleneck.

This introduces an important technical concept:

**Bottleneck:** The operation or resource that limits the throughput of the entire production system.

A company cannot assume that increasing capacity in one process automatically increases total finished goods output.

---

## 4.4 D. Procurement / Purchasing Department

**Supply Function**

### What does Procurement do?

Procurement is responsible for acquiring the materials, components, and services required by the company.

In S&OP, Procurement determines whether the company can obtain the necessary materials in the required quantities and timeframes.

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Supplier Availability | Whether suppliers can provide the required materials. |
| Supplier Lead Time | Time between placing an order and receiving the material. |
| Purchase Orders | Existing purchasing commitments. |
| Material Costs | Expected purchasing prices. |
| Supplier Capacity | Supplier's ability to fulfill requested quantities. |
| Minimum Order Quantity (MOQ) | Minimum quantity a supplier allows per order. |
| Supplier Risks | Potential supply disruptions or delivery problems. |
| Alternative Suppliers | Other potential sources of materials. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Identify material requirements**

Suppose manufacturing needs an additional 20,000 finished products.

Procurement evaluates the additional raw materials required to support production.

**2. Check supplier availability**

The required material might be available, but the supplier may need eight weeks to deliver it.

If the additional demand is required within three weeks, the normal procurement process might not be sufficient.

**3. Identify procurement alternatives**

Possible alternatives:

- Expedite existing purchase orders.
- Purchase from an alternative qualified supplier.
- Increase supplier allocations.
- Negotiate additional supplier capacity.
- Adjust production priorities.

**4. Evaluate purchasing implications**

Larger material purchases may involve:

- Higher inventory levels
- Additional storage requirements
- Increased cash requirements
- Price discounts
- Potential material obsolescence

### Why is Procurement important?

A company may have sufficient production capacity but still fail to meet demand because the necessary materials are unavailable.

This means production capacity alone does not determine supply capability.

Material availability is equally important.

---

## 4.5 E. Supply Planning Department

**Supply Planning Function**

### What does Supply Planning do?

Supply Planning evaluates how the company can satisfy the expected demand using available production capacity, inventory, procurement, and other supply resources.

This is one of the most important coordinating functions in S&OP.

Think of Supply Planning as the function that brings together the operational supply picture.

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Production Capacity | Available manufacturing capability. |
| Inventory Availability | Existing stock that can support demand. |
| Material Availability | Raw materials available for production. |
| Production Lead Time | Time required to manufacture products. |
| Supply Constraints | Limitations affecting the ability to supply. |
| Planned Production | Expected production quantities and timing. |
| Supply Alternatives | Different ways to satisfy demand. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Consolidate supply information**

Supply Planning collects information from:

- Manufacturing
- Procurement
- Inventory Management
- Warehousing
- Logistics, where relevant

**2. Evaluate supply feasibility**

Suppose the demand plan requires 60,000 units.

The company has:

- 10,000 units of available finished goods.
- Capacity to produce 40,000 units.
- Materials available for only 35,000 units.

Supply Planning needs to evaluate the actual supply position.

**3. Identify demand-supply gaps**

The difference between demand and available supply must be identified.

For example:

Demand = 60,000 units.

Available supply = 45,000 units.

The company has a shortage of 15,000 units.

**4. Develop alternative supply plans**

Possible solutions include:

- Increasing production capacity.
- Purchasing additional materials.
- Using available inventory.
- Adjusting the production schedule.
- Reallocating supply among customers.
- Negotiating revised delivery dates.

### Why is Supply Planning important?

It prevents the organization from developing a demand plan that cannot be supported by available resources.

### Important distinction: Manufacturing vs. Supply Planning

These functions are related but not identical.

| Manufacturing | Supply Planning |
|---|---|
| Focuses on factory operations. | Focuses on overall supply feasibility. |
| Evaluates machine and labor capability. | Evaluates production, inventory, and procurement together. |
| Determines operational production possibilities. | Coordinates the supply response to demand. |
| Focuses on execution within manufacturing. | Focuses on balancing supply across the planning horizon. |

In some organizations, Manufacturing Planning and Supply Planning may be handled by the same team.

In others, they are separate functions.

## 4.6 F. Inventory / Warehouse Management

**Inventory and Logistics Function**

### What does Inventory / Warehouse Management do?

This function manages the company's physical inventory and provides visibility into available materials and finished goods.

In simple terms:

It tells the company what it actually has, where it is located, and whether it is available for use or shipment.

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Raw Material Inventory | Available production materials. |
| Work-in-Process (WIP) | Products currently undergoing manufacturing. |
| Finished Goods Inventory | Completed products available for distribution. |
| Inventory Accuracy | Agreement between physical inventory and system records. |
| Inventory Aging | How long inventory has remained in stock. |
| Inventory Status | Available, reserved, blocked, or quality-hold inventory. |
| Storage Capacity | Available warehouse space. |
| Obsolete Inventory | Stock that is no longer needed or usable. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Provide inventory visibility**

Suppose the system shows 15,000 units of finished goods.

However, 3,000 units are currently under quality hold.

The actual available quantity is not necessarily 15,000 units.

The company needs to distinguish between physical stock and usable inventory.

**2. Identify inventory risks**

Examples:

- Excess inventory
- Material shortages
- Expiring materials
- Obsolete products
- Slow-moving inventory
- Inaccurate inventory records

**3. Support inventory allocation**

If there is insufficient stock for all customers, inventory information helps Supply Planning and Sales evaluate allocation decisions.

### Why is Inventory Management important?

Without accurate inventory information, the company may overestimate its available supply.

For example:

The system reports 20,000 units, but 5,000 units are defective or blocked.

The company cannot automatically promise all 20,000 units to customers.

**Important distinction:** Physical inventory is not always equivalent to available inventory.

## 4.7 G. Finance Department

**Financial Function**

### What does Finance do?

Finance evaluates the financial implications of the demand and supply plans.

It ensures that the proposed operational plan is financially reasonable and supports the company's financial objectives.

In simple terms:

Operations asks, "Can we produce it?"

Finance asks, "What will it cost, how much revenue will it generate, and is it financially worthwhile?"

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Revenue Forecast | Expected sales revenue. |
| Production Costs | Manufacturing expenses. |
| Material Costs | Costs of purchased materials. |
| Labor Costs | Regular and overtime labor expenses. |
| Inventory Value | Financial value of inventory. |
| Operating Expenses | Other expenses required to operate the business. |
| Profitability | Expected financial return. |
| Budget | Approved financial spending limits. |
| Cash Requirements | Expected cash needed for operations and purchasing. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Evaluate expected revenue**

Suppose Sales forecasts 100,000 units.

Finance estimates the expected revenue based on selling prices and applicable assumptions.

**2. Evaluate additional operating costs**

If Manufacturing proposes additional overtime, Finance evaluates the additional labor cost.

If Procurement proposes buying additional materials, Finance evaluates the required spending.

**3. Evaluate inventory impact**

Higher inventory generally means more money tied up in stock.

This is commonly referred to as **working capital**.

**Working capital** represents funds tied up in the company's short-term operating assets and liabilities.

Inventory is an important component of working capital.

**4. Evaluate profitability**

An increase in sales does not necessarily mean an increase in profit.

For example:

A customer requests additional products but requires a significant price discount.

The company must evaluate whether the additional volume is financially attractive.

**5. Perform financial reconciliation**

Finance translates operational plans into financial terms.

This allows management to evaluate whether the proposed plan supports:

- Revenue targets
- Gross margin targets
- Cost targets
- Inventory objectives
- Budget requirements

### Why is Finance important?

A plan can be operationally feasible but financially unattractive.

For example:

The company can fulfill an additional customer order by using expensive air freight and extensive overtime.

But will the additional revenue justify the cost?

Finance helps management answer that question.

## 4.8 H. Product Management / Marketing

**Product and Market Function**

### What does Product Management / Marketing do?

This function manages product-related strategies and communicates market activities that may influence future demand.

It helps the company understand not only how much customers may purchase, but also which products are expected to become important.

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Product Launches | New products entering the market. |
| Product Phase-outs | Products scheduled for discontinuation. |
| Promotions | Marketing activities expected to influence sales. |
| Product Lifecycle | Product introduction, growth, maturity, and decline. |
| Market Trends | Changes in market demand and preferences. |
| Product Strategy | Business priorities involving specific products. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Communicate new product launches**

A new product launch may create demand that does not appear in historical sales data.

Demand Planning needs to consider this information.

**2. Communicate product phase-outs**

A product scheduled for discontinuation may require a different inventory strategy.

The company may need to:

- Reduce future production.
- Manage remaining inventory.
- Avoid unnecessary material purchases.
- Plan final customer deliveries.

**3. Explain product demand changes**

Marketing campaigns and market developments can significantly change demand patterns.

### Why is Product Management important?

Historical demand alone may not capture the impact of future product strategies.

Product Management helps the organization anticipate demand changes associated with the product lifecycle.

## 4.9 I. Senior Management / Executive Leadership

**Decision and Approval Function**

### What does Senior Management do?

Senior Management provides strategic direction and makes important decisions when departments cannot resolve conflicts independently.

It is generally responsible for ensuring that the final S&OP plan supports the overall business strategy.

### Information contributed

| Information | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Business Strategy | Long-term organizational direction. |
| Revenue Targets | Expected financial performance. |
| Growth Priorities | Markets, customers, or products the company wants to prioritize. |
| Resource Priorities | Where the company should allocate resources. |
| Risk Appetite | Level of risk the organization is willing to accept. |
| Strategic Constraints | Major business limitations or requirements. |

### Responsibilities in S&OP

**1. Establish business priorities**

Management may prioritize:

- Customer service
- Profitability
- Market expansion
- Inventory reduction
- Production efficiency
- Cash preservation

**2. Resolve major conflicts**

Suppose Sales wants to prioritize a large customer order, while Operations wants to maintain production stability.

Management evaluates the business implications and determines the priority.

**3. Approve major decisions**

Management may approve:

- Additional production shifts
- Capital expenditure
- Alternative sourcing
- Significant inventory investments
- Customer allocation decisions

**4. Approve the agreed S&OP plan**

The executive team reviews the consolidated plan and makes decisions regarding unresolved gaps.

### Why is Management important?

Not every conflict can be solved by exchanging information.

Some decisions require authority to allocate resources, accept risks, or change business priorities.

This is where executive leadership becomes essential.

# 5. Roles and Responsibilities in the S&OP Process

Now let's answer an important organizational question:

**Does every department have the same level of responsibility in S&OP?**

No.

Some departments provide information. Others develop plans. Some review and challenge those plans. Others have the authority to make final decisions.

We need to distinguish these responsibilities.

## 5.1 Participating vs. owning a planning activity

Being an S&OP participant does not automatically mean that you own the entire S&OP process.

For example:

- Sales participates in demand planning but does not necessarily own the consolidated demand plan.
- Manufacturing provides capacity information but does not necessarily own the overall supply plan.
- Finance evaluates financial implications but does not necessarily own production planning.
- Senior Management approves strategic decisions but does not necessarily prepare the underlying forecasts.

This is the distinction between **participation** and **functional ownership**.

## 5.2 Who does what?

| Responsibility | Typical owner or contributor |
|---|---|
| Provide customer information | Sales |
| Consolidate demand information | Demand Planning |
| Develop demand plan | Demand Planning, with Sales input |
| Evaluate manufacturing capacity | Manufacturing |
| Provide supplier information | Procurement |
| Develop consolidated supply plan | Supply Planning |
| Validate inventory | Inventory / Warehouse |
| Evaluate financial implications | Finance |
| Challenge business assumptions | Cross-functional stakeholders |
| Resolve major business conflicts | Senior Management |
| Approve the overall plan | Executive Leadership |

These are typical arrangements. Actual ownership depends on the company's organizational structure and S&OP maturity.

## 5.3 What is an S&OP Leader or Process Owner?

This is an important role.

The S&OP Leader or Process Owner is responsible for coordinating the overall S&OP process.

Think of this person as the conductor of an orchestra.

Hindi siya ang tumutugtog ng lahat ng instruments. Instead, siya ang nag-e-ensure na lahat ng participants ay coordinated and working toward the same output.

### Typical responsibilities

1. Establish the S&OP calendar and planning cycle.
2. Coordinate information collection from departments.
3. Ensure that planning assumptions are consistent.
4. Monitor completion of departmental planning activities.
5. Identify unresolved issues and planning gaps.
6. Facilitate cross-functional discussions.
7. Prepare management review materials.
8. Track decisions and action items.
9. Monitor implementation of the agreed plan.

### Who can be the S&OP Process Owner?

Depending on the company, it may be:

- S&OP Manager
- Supply Chain Manager
- Demand Planning Manager
- Integrated Business Planning (IBP) Manager
- Supply Chain Planning Director

Some organizations assign the responsibility to a dedicated S&OP team.

Others assign it to an existing Supply Chain function.

**Important:** The S&OP Process Owner manages the process, but the functional departments remain responsible for the accuracy and quality of their respective inputs.

# 6. Departmental Interactions and Information Flow

This is where everything comes together.

Understanding each department individually is important, but understanding how they interact is even more important.

## 6.1 How does information flow between departments?

Let's use a manufacturing company that experiences an increase in customer demand.

### Illustrative S&OP information flow

1. **SALES**

   Communicates customer demand changes and order expectations.

2. **DEMAND PLANNING**

   Consolidates information and updates the demand plan.

3. **SUPPLY PLANNING**

   Evaluates available supply and identifies demand-supply gaps.

   Supply Planning coordinates with:
   - **Manufacturing:** Production capacity and constraints.
   - **Procurement:** Materials and supplier availability.
   - **Inventory:** Available stock and inventory risks.
   - **Finance:** Cost, revenue and profitability.

4. **CROSS-FUNCTIONAL REVIEW**

   Departments review assumptions, constraints, alternatives and trade-offs.

5. **EXECUTIVE MANAGEMENT**

   Resolves major conflicts and approves business decisions.

6. **FINAL OUTPUT: ONE ALIGNED BUSINESS PLAN**

   Demand, supply, inventory, capacity and financial plans aligned with business priorities.

*Simplified conceptual flow. Actual S&OP is iterative, with information moving back and forth between departments rather than following only one direction.*

## 6.2 Why is the information flow iterative?

Let's say Demand Planning initially receives a forecast of 50,000 units.

Supply Planning evaluates the demand and discovers that the company can only support 40,000 units.

Supply Planning communicates the shortage.

Sales then reviews customer priorities and discovers that some customers can accept revised delivery dates.

Demand Planning may revise the timing of demand.

Manufacturing updates the production schedule.

Finance recalculates the financial impact.

This process continues until the organization develops an agreed plan or identifies issues requiring executive decisions.

Notice that information does not simply move downward.

It also moves backward and sideways.

That is why S&OP is a collaborative and iterative process.

## 6.3 The importance of information transparency

Consider this situation:

Manufacturing knows that one machine will undergo maintenance next month.

However, the information is not communicated to Supply Planning.

Supply Planning develops a plan assuming the machine will be available.

The result is an unrealistic supply plan.

The problem is not necessarily the planning methodology. The problem is missing information.

This introduces three essential principles:

- **Information accuracy:** Data must reflect actual business conditions.
- **Information transparency:** Departments must communicate important changes, risks, and limitations.
- **Timeliness:** Information must be available early enough to influence decisions.

A correct report delivered too late can still result in a poor business decision.

# 7. Common Departmental Conflicts in S&OP

Now let's discuss something that happens frequently in actual organizations.

Departments have different objectives.

These objectives are not necessarily wrong. In fact, each department may be doing exactly what it is supposed to do.

The problem occurs when individual departmental objectives conflict with the overall business objective.

## 7.1 Conflict 1: Sales vs. Finance on Inventory Levels

**Situation:**

Sales wants higher inventory to ensure customer orders can be fulfilled immediately.

Finance wants lower inventory to reduce working capital.

| Sales perspective | Finance perspective |
|---|---|
| More inventory improves product availability. | More inventory ties up company cash. |
| Reduces risk of lost sales. | Increases inventory carrying costs. |
| Improves customer service. | Increases exposure to obsolete stock. |

**Why does this conflict happen?**

Sales is primarily concerned with customer satisfaction and revenue opportunities.

Finance is concerned with profitability, cash flow, and efficient use of company resources.

**How does S&OP resolve this?**

The departments evaluate the inventory requirements against actual demand patterns and customer service priorities.

For example:

Instead of increasing inventory across all products, the company may prioritize higher inventory for products with more predictable demand or critical customer requirements.

**Business trade-off:**

Higher inventory can improve service levels but may increase carrying costs and financial exposure.

Lower inventory can improve cash efficiency but may increase shortage risk.

The goal is not automatically to minimize or maximize inventory.

The goal is to maintain an appropriate inventory level based on business requirements.

## 7.2 Conflict 2: Sales vs. Manufacturing on Customer Commitments

**Situation:**

Sales accepts an order requiring 15,000 units next week.

Manufacturing's available production capacity only supports 10,000 units.

| Sales | Manufacturing |
|---|---|
| Wants to satisfy the customer. | Wants a realistic production schedule. |
| Protects revenue opportunities. | Protects operational stability. |
| Prioritizes delivery commitments. | Considers capacity and production constraints. |

**Why does this conflict happen?**

Sales focuses on customer expectations.

Manufacturing focuses on what can realistically be produced.

**How does S&OP resolve this?**

The company evaluates alternatives:

- Can overtime provide the additional capacity?
- Can existing finished goods inventory cover the shortage?
- Can another production line support the order?
- Can the customer accept partial delivery?
- Can another customer's delivery be rescheduled?

**Business trade-off:**

Accepting the additional order may generate revenue but could require overtime, increased costs, or disruption to other customer commitments.

## 7.3 Conflict 3: Manufacturing vs. Sales on Production Flexibility

**Situation:**

Manufacturing prefers producing large batches of the same product.

Sales wants the factory to frequently change products according to customer requirements.

| Manufacturing | Sales |
|---|---|
| Prefers longer production runs. | Prefers flexibility in product mix. |
| Reduces setup and changeover time. | Wants faster response to customer demand. |
| Improves production efficiency. | Wants to avoid shortages of specific products. |

**Why does this conflict happen?**

Manufacturing wants stable and efficient production.

Sales wants responsiveness to changing customer demand.

**How does S&OP resolve this?**

The company evaluates:

- Product demand patterns
- Production changeover costs
- Inventory levels
- Customer priorities
- Production capacity

It may establish production priorities or inventory buffers for selected products.

**Business trade-off:**

Long production runs can improve efficiency but may create excess inventory.

Shorter runs improve flexibility but may increase setup time and production costs.

## 7.4 Conflict 4: Procurement vs. Finance on Purchasing Quantities

**Situation:**

Procurement wants to purchase 100,000 units of raw materials because the supplier offers a bulk discount.

Finance wants Procurement to purchase only 50,000 units to reduce inventory investment.

| Procurement | Finance |
|---|---|
| Wants to secure material availability. | Wants to control spending and cash usage. |
| Wants bulk purchasing discounts. | Wants lower inventory investment. |
| Wants protection against supplier shortages. | Wants to minimize inventory exposure. |

**Why does this conflict happen?**

Procurement considers supplier economics and material availability.

Finance considers cash requirements and inventory carrying costs.

**How does S&OP resolve this?**

The company evaluates the total cost rather than considering the purchasing price alone.

It should consider:

- Bulk discounts
- Storage costs
- Material consumption rates
- Supplier lead times
- Obsolescence risk
- Working capital requirements

**Business trade-off:**

Bulk purchasing may reduce unit costs but increase inventory and financial exposure.

## 7.5 Conflict 5: Finance vs. Operations on Additional Resources

**Situation:**

Manufacturing requests additional manpower and overtime to increase production output.

Finance wants to reduce operating expenses.

| Operations | Finance |
|---|---|
| Wants sufficient resources. | Wants cost control. |
| Wants to meet production requirements. | Wants to protect profitability. |
| May require overtime or additional equipment. | Evaluates financial justification. |

**Why does this conflict happen?**

Operations prioritizes production capability.

Finance prioritizes cost efficiency and profitability.

**How does S&OP resolve this?**

The company evaluates whether additional resources create enough business value.

For example, additional overtime may be justified if:

- Customer demand is confirmed.
- The additional sales generate sufficient contribution margin.
- Existing capacity cannot support the demand.
- The additional cost is lower than the financial consequences of missing the customer commitment.

### 7.6 What is the common solution to all these conflicts?

The answer is **business trade-off evaluation**.

A trade-off means accepting that improving one business objective may require compromising another.

S&OP provides a structured environment where departments can evaluate these competing objectives using shared information.

The question changes from:

"Which department should win?"

To:

"Which decision produces the best overall business outcome?"

That is a major difference between departmental management and integrated business planning.

# 8. Practical Manufacturing Business Scenario

Let's apply everything to a realistic electronic components manufacturing company.

For this example, imagine a manufacturer producing MLCC products.

The numbers below are illustrative assumptions, not actual company data or industry standards.

## 8.1 Business situation

A major customer suddenly increases its expected demand.

### Customer demand increase

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Original monthly demand | 50,000 units |
| New monthly demand | 70,000 units |
| **Additional demand** | **20,000 units** |

*40% increase compared with the original forecast.*

The customer wants the additional quantity within the next month.

The company now needs to determine whether it can fulfill the request.

## 8.2 Department-by-department evaluation

### A. Sales

Sales communicates:

- Customer requires 70,000 units.
- The increase is supported by the customer's new production requirement.
- The additional 20,000 units are expected to be recurring for at least three months.
- Customer prefers complete delivery within the month.

Sales asks:

"Can we accommodate the additional demand without compromising the customer's delivery schedule?"

### B. Demand Planning

Demand Planning evaluates the revised demand.

| Item | Original | Revised |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly demand | 50,000 | 70,000 |
| Increase | - | 20,000 |

The planner updates the demand plan and checks whether the increased forecast is supported by customer information.

Questions:

- Is the demand increase confirmed?
- Is it temporary or recurring?
- Does it affect other customers?
- Does it change demand in succeeding months?

### C. Manufacturing

Manufacturing evaluates its available production capacity.

| Production information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Current available monthly capacity | 55,000 |
| Required demand | 70,000 |
| Capacity shortage | 15,000 |

Manufacturing identifies a capacity shortage of 15,000 units.

Possible solutions:

- Additional shifts
- Overtime
- Improved machine utilization
- Reduced changeover time

Manufacturing estimates that overtime can increase monthly capacity to 65,000 units.

However, that still leaves 5,000 units unsupported.

### D. Procurement

Procurement checks raw material availability.

| Material information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Current available materials | 55,000 units' worth |
| Additional material requirement | 15,000 units' worth |
| Total material requirement | 70,000 units' worth |

Procurement identifies that materials are insufficient for the revised demand.

The supplier normally requires six weeks to deliver additional materials.

However, the company needs the materials sooner.

Procurement investigates whether the supplier can expedite delivery or allocate existing stock.

### E. Inventory / Warehouse

Warehouse checks existing finished goods inventory.

| Inventory information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Physical finished goods stock | 12,000 |
| Quality-hold stock | 2,000 |
| Reserved for other customers | 3,000 |
| Available uncommitted stock | 7,000 |

The company has 12,000 units physically in stock, but only 7,000 units are available for the additional requirement.

This is an important distinction.

Physical stock does not automatically mean available-to-promise inventory.

### F. Finance

Finance evaluates the financial implications.

Assume the following:

| Financial information | Illustrative amount |
|---|---:|
| Selling price per unit | ₱10 |
| Additional sales volume | 20,000 |
| Additional revenue | ₱200,000 |
| Additional overtime cost | ₱15,000 |
| Additional expedited procurement cost | ₱8,000 |

Finance needs to evaluate whether the additional revenue and expected contribution justify the additional costs.

It also needs to account for material costs, other manufacturing expenses, and applicable overheads.

### G. Product Management / Marketing

Product Management confirms whether the demand increase is connected to:

- A new product launch
- A customer design change
- A product demand trend
- A temporary market opportunity

This helps Demand Planning determine whether the forecast increase should continue in succeeding months.

### H. Senior Management

Management receives the consolidated situation:

- Demand increased by 20,000 units.
- Manufacturing has limited available capacity.
- Raw materials are insufficient.
- Existing inventory can cover part of the shortage.
- Expediting materials and increasing production would incur additional costs.

Management now evaluates which alternatives provide the best overall outcome.

## 8.3 Consolidated business assessment

Let's summarize the initial supply position.

| Supply component | Units |
|---|---:|
| Available uncommitted finished goods | 7,000 |
| Current production capacity | 55,000 |
| **Total potential supply** | **62,000** |
| Revised demand | 70,000 |
| **Initial supply gap** | **8,000** |

Important assumption: The 55,000 units of production capacity can be supported by existing materials, and the 7,000 finished goods are available for the customer.

The initial supply gap is 8,000 units.

Now Manufacturing proposes overtime, increasing production capacity from 55,000 to 65,000 units.

However, Procurement must first confirm sufficient raw materials for the additional production.

## 8.4 Possible management decision

After reviewing the alternatives, the company might agree to:

1. Allocate the 7,000 available finished goods to the customer.
2. Increase production capacity to 65,000 units through overtime.
3. Expedite additional raw materials.
4. Prioritize the customer in the production schedule.
5. Negotiate partial delivery if the remaining quantity cannot be completed on time.
6. Monitor the following two months because the demand increase may be recurring.

### Illustrative revised supply plan

| Supply source | Units |
|---|---:|
| Available finished goods | 7,000 |
| Planned production | 63,000 |
| **Total supply** | **70,000** |
| Demand | 70,000 |
| **Remaining gap** | **0** |

**Proposed plan: Demand fully covered**

*This assumes Procurement successfully secures enough materials and Manufacturing can achieve the proposed 63,000-unit production quantity within the required period.*

### What did we learn from this scenario?

Notice that no single department independently solved the problem.

- Sales identified the opportunity.
- Demand Planning quantified the demand.
- Manufacturing identified capacity constraints.
- Procurement identified material constraints.
- Inventory provided existing stock information.
- Finance evaluated the cost implications.
- Product Management provided market context.
- Management made the final business decision.

This is the practical value of cross-functional S&OP.

**One department identifies a problem, but multiple departments contribute to solving it.**

# 9. Business Perspective and Decision-Making: The Role of an S&OP Analyst

Now let's connect this lesson to your actual responsibilities as an S&OP professional.

An S&OP Analyst is not expected to become an expert in every department's technical operations.

However, an effective analyst must understand what each department does, what information it owns, and how its decisions affect the overall plan.

Your responsibility is to help convert departmental information into meaningful business insights.

## 9.1 How an S&OP Analyst works with different departments

### Step 1: Gather information

You collect the relevant information from different functions.

| Department | Information to request |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer orders, forecast changes, demand assumptions |
| Demand Planning | Current forecast, historical demand, forecast adjustments |
| Manufacturing | Production capacity, planned output, downtime |
| Procurement | Supplier lead times, material availability, purchase commitments |
| Inventory | Stock levels, inventory status, shortages |
| Finance | Revenue forecast, cost assumptions, budget |
| Product Management | Product launches, phase-outs, lifecycle changes |
| Management | Business priorities and strategic assumptions |

The objective is to obtain a complete and consistent business picture.

### Step 2: Validate the information

Never assume that every submitted number is automatically correct.

For example:

Sales reports 70,000 units of expected demand.

But the current demand planning report shows only 60,000 units.

You need to investigate the difference.

Possible questions:

- Was the Sales forecast updated?
- Are the numbers based on the same planning period?
- Are customer orders already included?
- Are there duplicate forecast entries?
- Are both reports using the same units of measurement?

This is where data validation becomes important.

### Step 3: Identify conflicting assumptions

Suppose:

- Sales expects 70,000 units.
- Manufacturing plans for 55,000 units.
- Procurement has materials for only 50,000 units.
- Finance has budgeted production for 60,000 units.

These are not necessarily data errors.

They may represent different assumptions and constraints.

Your job is to identify these differences and make them visible.

### Step 4: Quantify the business gaps

Do not simply report:

"Manufacturing cannot meet demand."

Instead, quantify the problem.

Example:

| Metric | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Demand | 70,000 |
| Available supply | 55,000 |
| Supply gap | 15,000 |
| Supply coverage | 78.57% |

Now management can understand the magnitude of the problem.

### Step 5: Communicate possible alternatives

An S&OP Analyst should help present the available options.

For example:

| Alternative | Potential benefit | Potential concern |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime | Additional production | Higher labor costs |
| Additional supplier | Better material availability | Qualification and sourcing risks |
| Existing inventory | Faster fulfillment | May affect other customers |
| Revised delivery schedule | Reduces immediate capacity pressure | Customer dissatisfaction |
| Additional equipment | Long-term capacity improvement | Capital investment |

Your responsibility is to present the evidence and implications clearly.

The relevant decision-makers then evaluate and approve the appropriate action.

## 9.2 What skills does an S&OP Analyst need?

| Skill | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Data Analysis | Understand trends, changes, and discrepancies. |
| Excel / Reporting | Consolidate and communicate planning information. |
| Forecast Understanding | Interpret demand assumptions and forecast changes. |
| Supply Chain Knowledge | Understand operational constraints. |
| Communication | Coordinate information across departments. |
| Critical Thinking | Identify inconsistencies and unrealistic assumptions. |
| Business Acumen | Understand cost, revenue, inventory, and customer implications. |
| Presentation Skills | Translate complex information into management insights. |

A particularly important skill is knowing how to ask the right questions.

For example, instead of asking:

"Why is production lower than demand?"

Ask:

"Which specific constraint is limiting production output, and what alternatives are available to close the 15,000-unit supply gap?"

The second question is more analytical and action-oriented.

## 9.3 From Level 1 to Level 3: How your thinking should develop

### Level 1: Understanding

**Know the departments and their responsibilities.**

Example: Manufacturing handles production capacity, while Procurement handles material availability.

### Level 2: Technical Application

**Understand how departmental information affects the plan.**

Example: Calculate the supply gap and determine whether production capacity and material availability can support the revised demand.

### Level 3: Professional Analysis

**Evaluate alternatives and recommend business decisions.**

Example: Determine whether overtime, alternative sourcing, inventory allocation, or revised delivery schedules provide the best overall business outcome.

Your goal is to progress from simply understanding departmental responsibilities to analyzing how those departments influence business performance.

# 10. Formulas and Calculations

This topic is primarily about organizational responsibilities and collaboration, so we do not need to introduce complicated mathematical formulas.

However, an S&OP Analyst should understand a few simple calculations used to communicate departmental planning gaps.

### 10.1 Demand-Supply Gap

\[
\text{Supply Gap}=\text{Demand}-\text{Available Supply}
\]

Example:

- Demand = 70,000 units
- Available supply = 55,000 units

\[
70,000-55,000=15,000
\]

**Result: 15,000 units shortage**

Interpretation: The company needs an additional 15,000 units of supply or an appropriate demand adjustment.

### 10.2 Supply Coverage

\[
\text{Supply Coverage}=
\frac{\text{Available Supply}}{\text{Demand}}\times100
\]

Using the same example:

\[
\frac{55,000}{70,000}\times100
=78.57\%
\]

Interpretation: The available supply covers approximately 78.57% of expected demand.

### 10.3 Percentage Demand Increase

\[
\text{Demand Increase \%}=
\frac{\text{New Demand}-\text{Original Demand}}
{\text{Original Demand}}\times100
\]

Original demand = 50,000 units.

New demand = 70,000 units.

\[
\frac{70,000-50,000}{50,000}\times100
=40\%
\]

Interpretation: Customer demand increased by 40%.

**Important calculation consideration:** Always make sure that demand and supply figures refer to the same product, location, unit of measurement, and planning period. Otherwise, the calculated gap may be misleading.

# 11. Key Takeaways

Let's consolidate the most important concepts from this lesson.

## 11.1 The nine functions and their responsibilities

| Department | Remember this |
|---|---|
| Sales | Understands customer requirements. |
| Demand Planning | Develops the consolidated demand plan. |
| Manufacturing | Evaluates production capability. |
| Procurement | Secures the required materials. |
| Supply Planning | Develops a feasible supply plan. |
| Inventory / Warehouse | Provides inventory visibility and availability. |
| Finance | Evaluates financial implications. |
| Product Management / Marketing | Communicates product and market changes. |
| Senior Management | Establishes priorities and makes major decisions. |

## 11.2 Five concepts you should remember

**1. S&OP is cross-functional.**

It requires multiple departments because no single department has all the information needed to develop an effective business plan.

**2. Every department has a different objective.**

Sales prioritizes customers and revenue. Manufacturing prioritizes production efficiency. Finance prioritizes financial performance.

These objectives must be aligned with the overall business strategy.

**3. Information must be shared.**

Missing, inaccurate, or delayed information can create unrealistic plans and poor decisions.

**4. S&OP helps resolve departmental conflicts.**

The objective is not to make one department win. It is to determine the best overall business decision.

**5. The final output is one aligned business plan.**

All departments should work using consistent planning assumptions and agreed business priorities.

## 11.3 Important distinctions

These distinctions are particularly important for your future S&OP work.

| Do not confuse | Actual distinction |
|---|---|
| Sales vs. Demand Planning | Sales provides customer and market information; Demand Planning consolidates and manages the demand plan. |
| Manufacturing vs. Supply Planning | Manufacturing evaluates factory capability; Supply Planning coordinates overall supply feasibility. |
| Procurement vs. Inventory | Procurement secures materials; Inventory Management monitors available stock. |
| Participation vs. Ownership | A department can contribute to a plan without owning the entire planning activity. |
| Physical Stock vs. Available Stock | Some physical inventory may be reserved, defective, blocked, or on quality hold. |
| S&OP Leader vs. Executive Management | The process owner coordinates S&OP; executive management makes major business decisions. |
