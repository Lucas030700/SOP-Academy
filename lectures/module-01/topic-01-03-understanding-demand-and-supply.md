# Module 1 – S&OP Fundamentals
## Topic 1.3: Understanding Demand and Supply

**Learning level:** Level 1: Understanding; Level 2: Technical Application; Level 3: Professional Analysis

Welcome to Topic 1.3!

Sa topic na ito, pag-uusapan natin ang dalawang pinaka-importanteng elements ng S&OP: **Demand and Supply**.

Kung sa previous topics, na-establish natin kung ano ang S&OP at bakit ito ginagamit ng companies, ngayon naman ay aalamin natin kung ano talaga ang kailangang i-balance ng S&OP.

Ang central question natin:

**Can the company supply what customers want, when they want it, and in the quantity they need?**

Ito ang isa sa pinakamahalagang business questions na kailangang masagot ng S&OP.

---

# 1. TOPIC OVERVIEW

## 1.1 What are Demand and Supply?

Sa pinakasimpleng explanation:

- **Demand** refers to how much of a product customers want or are expected to purchase.
- **Supply** refers to how much of that product a company can make available to meet customer demand.

Halimbawa, may manufacturing company na gumagawa ng electronic components.

Sa isang buwan:

- Customers want 100,000 units.
- The company can only produce 80,000 units.

May problema.

### Basic Demand vs. Supply

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer Demand | 100,000 units |
| Available Supply | 80,000 units |
| **Supply Gap** | **20,000 units** |

Illustrative figures. Assumes no additional inventory or other supply sources.

Ang 20,000 units na difference ay tinatawag nating **Demand-Supply Gap**.

Bakit mahalaga ito?

Dahil kapag hindi kayang i-meet ng company ang demand, possible consequences include:

- Lost sales
- Delayed customer deliveries
- Customer dissatisfaction
- Missed revenue
- Potential loss of customers

On the other hand, kung mas marami naman ang supply kaysa demand, possible consequences include:

- Excess inventory
- Higher inventory holding costs
- Unnecessary production expenses
- Cash tied up in unsold products

Ito ang reason kung bakit kailangan ang S&OP: para magkaroon ng coordinated business plan na nagba-balance ng customer requirements at operational capabilities.

# 2. LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION

Bago tayo pumasok sa manufacturing terminology, imagine natin na may maliit kang bakery.

You sell bread.

Every morning, may customers na bumibili.

Let's assume:

- 100 customers want to buy bread.
- Each customer wants 2 pieces.
- Your bakery only has 150 pieces available.

Ano ang situation?

Your customers want:

\[
100 \times 2 = 200 \text{ pieces}
\]

But your bakery only has 150 pieces.

Therefore:

\[
200-150=50 \text{ pieces}
\]

May 50 pieces na hindi mo kayang ibigay sa customers.

This is a supply shortage.

Now, change natin ang situation.

You prepared 300 pieces, but only 150 pieces were purchased.

May 150 pieces na hindi nabenta.

This is excess supply relative to actual demand.

### What does this tell us?

Hindi sapat na alam lang ng company kung gaano karami ang kayang gawin.

Kailangan din nitong malaman kung gaano karami ang kailangan ng customers.

Imagine a bakery that keeps producing 300 pieces every day without checking customer demand.

Kung 150 pieces lang pala ang nabibili, araw-araw itong magkakaroon ng unsold products.

At the same time, imagine a bakery that only prepares 100 pieces even though customers regularly buy 200 pieces.

It will continuously lose potential sales.

**The goal is not to produce as much as possible. The goal is to produce and make available what the business reasonably needs to satisfy customer demand.**

Ito ang basic principle na gusto nating ma-establish bago tayo mag-discuss ng actual S&OP methodologies.

# 3. TECHNICAL EXPLANATION

Now, let's introduce the professional terminology.

## 3.1 Understanding Demand

**Demand** is the quantity of a product or service that customers want or are expected to purchase during a particular period.

In supply chain management, demand represents the market requirement that the company needs to understand and plan for.

For example:

A customer is expected to require 50,000 electronic components in December.

That quantity becomes an important input for the company's demand planning process.

However, may iba't ibang types of information na ginagamit natin kapag pinag-uusapan ang demand.

Importanteng hindi natin sila paghaluin.

### A. Customer Needs

Ito ang actual requirement or need ng customer.

Example:

A customer manufactures electronic devices and requires 50,000 capacitors for its December production.

The customer needs 50,000 capacitors.

Pero hindi ibig sabihin na nag-place na siya ng official order.

**Customer needs represent the underlying requirement.**

### B. Customer Orders

Ito naman ang quantity na formally requested or committed by the customer through a purchase order or sales order.

Example:

The customer needs 50,000 units but has only placed an official order for 40,000 units.

| Information | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer requirement | 50,000 |
| Official customer order | 40,000 |
| Unordered requirement | 10,000 |

Important distinction:

Hindi lahat ng customer needs ay immediately reflected sa official customer orders.

May customers na nagbibigay muna ng expected requirements bago mag-place ng actual purchase orders.

### C. Actual Sales

Actual sales refer to the quantity of products sold or recorded as sales during a particular period.

Example:

A company received orders for 40,000 units.

However, because of limited inventory, it was only able to deliver and sell 35,000 units during the month.

Depending on the company's sales recognition policy:

- Customer orders: 40,000
- Actual sales: 35,000

The remaining 5,000 units may be fulfilled later.

Note that sales recognition and physical shipment are not always recorded at the same time. For this introductory discussion, we will use delivered units as a simplified illustration of sales.

### D. Forecasted Demand

**Forecasted demand** is the estimated quantity of products customers are expected to require or purchase in the future.

Example:

Based on historical sales, customer forecasts, market trends, and other information, the company estimates that December demand will be 55,000 units.

That 55,000 becomes the forecasted demand.

Hindi pa ito guaranteed order.

It is an estimate used for planning purposes.

### Comparing the Four Concepts

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---:|
| Customer needs | What the customer requires | 50,000 |
| Customer orders | What the customer formally ordered | 40,000 |
| Actual sales | What was actually sold | 35,000 |
| Forecasted demand | What the company expects customers to require | 55,000 |

**Important distinction**

These four quantities can be different because they represent different stages or perspectives of customer requirements.

Never automatically assume that:

\[
\text{Demand}=\text{Customer Orders}=\text{Actual Sales}
\]

They may be equal in some situations, but they are not interchangeable terms.

## 3.2 Sources of Demand Information

Saan nanggagaling ang demand information?

A company does not rely on just one source.

Common sources include:

| Source | Information provided |
|---|---|
| Customer purchase orders | Confirmed customer requirements |
| Customer forecasts | Expected future purchases |
| Historical sales | Previous purchasing patterns |
| Sales team | Market feedback and customer expectations |
| Market trends | Changes in industry demand |
| New product launches | Expected demand from new products |
| Promotions | Potential increase in customer purchases |
| Seasonality | Recurring changes in demand depending on the time of year |

### Manufacturing example

Imagine an electronic component manufacturer.

Its Sales department receives information from three major customers:

| Customer | November requirement |
|---|---:|
| Customer A | 30,000 |
| Customer B | 25,000 |
| Customer C | 20,000 |
| **Total** | **75,000** |

The company also analyzes historical sales and market trends.

Based on all available information, it estimates that December demand will increase to 90,000 units.

This information becomes an input to the Demand Plan.

## 3.3 Factors That Influence Demand

Demand is not constant.

Maraming factors ang nakakaapekto sa quantity na gustong bilhin ng customers.

**Internal and commercial factors:**

- Product pricing
- Sales promotions
- Customer contracts
- Product availability
- Product quality
- Customer relationships

**External and market factors:**

- Economic conditions
- Competitor activities
- Market growth or decline
- Industry trends
- Seasonality
- Technological changes
- Changes in customer preferences

### Example

A company manufactures electronic components used in smartphones.

A new smartphone model is scheduled for release.

The smartphone manufacturer expects higher production volume, which increases its component requirements.

As a result, demand for the electronic components may increase.

Notice the relationship:

New smartphone launch → Higher smartphone production → Higher component requirements → Higher demand for electronic components.

This is an example of how changes in a customer's business can influence your company's demand.

## 3.4 Why Demand Is Not Always Equal to Actual Sales

This is one of the most important concepts in this lesson.

Let's say:

- Customers want 100,000 units.
- Your company only has 70,000 units available.
- You deliver 70,000 units.

Actual sales or fulfilled quantity, in our simplified example, is only 70,000.

Does this mean demand was only 70,000?

No.

The original demand was 100,000.

The company simply failed to fulfill 30,000 units.

This situation is known as **Unmet Demand**.

### Demand versus fulfilled quantity

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Customer demand | 100,000 |
| Fulfilled | 70,000 |
| Unmet demand | 30,000 |

This creates an important planning problem.

If the company uses actual sales alone to estimate future demand, it might underestimate demand because some customers were unable to purchase due to product shortages.

This is why demand planners need to distinguish between actual market requirements and the quantity the company successfully fulfilled.

## 3.5 Understanding Supply

Now let's move to the other side.

**Supply** refers to the quantity of products that a company can make available to meet customer requirements within a particular period.

Sa manufacturing, hindi lang ito tungkol sa kung ilang units ang kayang gawin ng production line.

It includes the company's entire ability to produce and deliver products.

For example, a company wants to produce 100,000 electronic components.

Can it do so?

We need to check several things.

### A. Production Capacity

Production capacity is the amount of output that a production system can produce within a given period under specified operating conditions.

Example:

A production line can produce 5,000 units per day.

If it operates for 20 production days:

\[
5,000 \times 20=100,000
\]

Its theoretical available production capacity is 100,000 units for that period, assuming the stated daily output is achievable throughout.

But capacity can be affected by:

- Machine downtime
- Changeovers
- Maintenance
- Production efficiency
- Product mix
- Operating schedules

### B. Raw Material Availability

A company cannot manufacture products without the required materials.

Example:

The production plan requires 100,000 units.

However, available raw materials are only sufficient to produce 75,000 units.

Even if the machines can produce 100,000 units, the company cannot achieve that output without additional materials.

### C. Manpower

Production requires people with the appropriate skills and availability.

Examples:

- Machine operators
- Technicians
- Quality inspectors
- Production supervisors
- Maintenance personnel

If manpower is insufficient, production output may be reduced.

### D. Equipment and Machines

Machines determine how much production can be performed.

Examples:

- Number of available production lines
- Machine cycle time
- Equipment condition
- Maintenance requirements
- Equipment utilization

If one critical machine is unavailable, the production process may become a bottleneck.

### E. Finished Goods Inventory

Finished Goods (FG) Inventory refers to products that have already been manufactured and are ready for sale or delivery.

Example:

A company has 15,000 finished units in its warehouse.

If customers require 50,000 units, the company may use the existing inventory to fulfill part of the demand.

This means it does not necessarily need to manufacture all 50,000 units during the current planning period.

### F. Supplier Availability

A company also depends on its suppliers.

Even if internal production capacity is available, a supplier may not be able to deliver the required materials on time.

Example:

- Required raw material: 10,000 kg
- Available raw material: 6,000 kg
- Supplier lead time: 30 days

The company has a shortage of 4,000 kg.

If production is scheduled to begin next week, the supplier's lead time becomes a major constraint.

---

## 3.6 Available Inventory vs. Total Supply Capability

This distinction is especially important.

Many beginners assume that supply is equivalent to available inventory.

That is incorrect.

**Available inventory is what the company already has. Supply capability includes what the company can make available through inventory, production, and replenishment.**

Let's compare:

| Supply component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Existing finished goods | 20,000 |
| Planned production | 60,000 |
| Confirmed incoming supply | 10,000 |
| **Potential total supply** | **90,000** |

The company has only 20,000 units currently available in inventory.

But its potential supply for the planning period is 90,000 units, assuming production and incoming deliveries are feasible and the quantities are not double-counted.

### Supply capability breakdown

| Component | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Existing inventory | 20,000 |
| Planned production | 60,000 |
| Incoming supply | 10,000 |
| **Total potential supply** | **90,000** |

However, hindi automatic na 90,000 units ang actual available supply.

Kailangan pa ring i-check:

- Is the planned production achievable?
- Are all raw materials available?
- Will incoming materials arrive on time?
- Are the machines operational?
- Is the finished goods inventory already allocated to other customers?
- Will the products be completed before the required delivery dates?

These are the types of questions that Supply Planning must answer.

## 3.7 Why a Company Cannot Always Supply Everything Customers Demand

Imagine that customers require 120,000 units.

The company has:

- 20,000 finished goods
- Production capacity for 80,000 units
- Raw materials sufficient for only 60,000 units

What is the problem?

The company has enough production capacity to manufacture 80,000 units, but it only has materials for 60,000.

Therefore, its actual production capability is constrained by material availability.

Its total potential supply is:

\[
20,000+60,000=80,000
\]

Demand is 120,000.

The company has a supply gap of 40,000 units.

**Important:** Supply is limited by the most restrictive resource or constraint in the production and fulfillment process.

Having available machines does not automatically mean the company can produce.

Having raw materials does not automatically mean the company can produce if equipment or manpower is unavailable.

# 4. DETAILED DISCUSSION

## 4.1 Demand vs. Supply: Key Differences

Let's consolidate what we have discussed.

| Aspect | Demand | Supply |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | What do customers need? | What can the company provide? |
| Main focus | Customer requirements | Operational capability |
| Main source | Customers and market | Production and supply chain |
| Key information | Orders, forecasts, customer needs | Inventory, capacity, materials |
| Primary department inputs | Sales, Marketing, Customer Service | Operations, Production, Procurement |
| Main risk | Demand uncertainty | Supply constraints |

The two functions have different responsibilities, but they must work together.

Sales cannot simply say, "Customers need 100,000 units, so produce 100,000."

Operations cannot simply say, "We can only produce 60,000 units, so that's the plan."

Both departments need to understand the gap and develop a coordinated response.

## 4.2 Three Possible Demand-Supply Situations

There are three basic relationships.

### Situation A: Demand Greater Than Supply

\[
Demand>Supply
\]

Example:

- Demand = 100,000 units
- Supply = 80,000 units

Supply shortage:

\[
100,000-80,000=20,000
\]

**Supply Shortage**

Possible consequences:

- Unmet customer demand
- Delayed deliveries
- Lost revenue
- Backorders
- Customer dissatisfaction
- Increased pressure on production

The company needs to determine how to close or manage the gap.

Possible actions include:

- Increase production capacity.
- Expedite raw materials.
- Add overtime or shifts.
- Reallocate inventory.
- Prioritize important customers.
- Negotiate delivery schedules.

### Situation B: Supply Greater Than Demand

\[
Supply>Demand
\]

Example:

- Demand = 80,000 units
- Supply = 100,000 units

Excess supply:

\[
100,000-80,000=20,000
\]

**Excess Supply**

Possible consequences:

- Excess finished goods inventory
- Higher storage costs
- Increased working capital requirements
- Risk of product obsolescence
- Unnecessary production expenses

Possible actions:

- Reduce future production.
- Adjust procurement schedules.
- Delay noncritical production.
- Reallocate inventory to other customers or markets.
- Review demand assumptions.

### Situation C: Demand Equals Supply

\[
Demand=Supply
\]

Example:

- Demand = 100,000 units
- Supply = 100,000 units

**Balanced Supply and Demand**

The company has sufficient supply to meet the expected customer requirements.

Possible benefits:

- Better customer service
- Reduced shortages
- Controlled inventory levels
- More efficient resource utilization
- Better coordination across departments

However, one important reminder:

**Demand and supply being equal does not automatically mean the plan is healthy.**

For example, a company might have exactly 100,000 units of supply and 100,000 units of demand, but its supply depends on a supplier that has not yet confirmed the delivery of critical raw materials.

The numbers match, but the plan may not be feasible.

We need to consider timing, availability, and operational constraints.

## 4.3 Understanding Common Demand-Supply Imbalances

Let's analyze common manufacturing situations.

| Business situation | Operational consequence | Financial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| High demand, insufficient capacity | Production bottlenecks and delivery delays | Lost revenue and possible overtime costs |
| Low demand, excessive production | Finished goods accumulation | Higher inventory holding costs |
| Strong demand, raw material shortage | Production interruption | Expediting costs and missed sales |
| Available capacity, insufficient orders | Idle production resources | Lower utilization and higher fixed cost per unit |
| Unexpected demand increase | Sudden pressure on resources | Higher operating costs |
| Unexpected supply disruption | Reduced output and delayed fulfillment | Revenue loss and recovery expenses |

Let's discuss each one.

### Scenario 1: High Demand but Insufficient Production Capacity

A company receives orders for 100,000 units.

Its production capacity is only 75,000 units.

The company cannot immediately fulfill all orders.

What happens?

- Production schedules become congested.
- Delivery dates may be delayed.
- Sales may need to negotiate with customers.
- Operations may need additional shifts.

Financial impact:

The company may lose revenue if customers cancel orders or purchase from competitors.

### Scenario 2: Low Demand but Excessive Production

A company expects demand of 100,000 units and decides to produce that quantity.

However, actual demand turns out to be only 60,000 units.

The remaining 40,000 units become excess inventory, assuming they remain unsold.

Consequences:

- Warehouse space is consumed.
- Cash is tied up in inventory.
- Storage and handling expenses increase.
- Products may become obsolete.

The mistake was not necessarily poor production execution.

It may have been an inaccurate demand assumption or a failure to adjust production when demand changed.

### Scenario 3: Raw Material Shortage Despite Strong Demand

A company has confirmed customer orders for 100,000 units.

Its production equipment can handle the required volume.

However, critical raw materials are only sufficient for 70,000 units.

The result is a material-constrained supply situation.

The company must work with Procurement and suppliers to determine whether additional materials can arrive in time.

### Scenario 4: Available Production Capacity but Insufficient Customer Orders

A company has enough resources to manufacture 100,000 units.

But confirmed demand is only 50,000 units.

If it produces the full 100,000 units, it risks building unnecessary inventory.

If it produces only 50,000 units, it may leave some capacity unused.

This is where the company needs to consider its production strategy, inventory policy, future demand, and operating economics.

Unused capacity is not automatically bad. Sometimes maintaining spare capacity is a deliberate business decision.

### Scenario 5: Unexpected Changes in Demand or Supply

Imagine that a customer suddenly increases its requirement from 50,000 to 80,000 units.

At the same time, a supplier announces a two-week delay.

The company now faces two simultaneous changes:

- Demand increased by 30,000 units.
- Supply availability decreased.

This is exactly why S&OP needs regular updates and cross-functional coordination.

A plan developed last month may no longer be feasible today.

# 5. PRACTICAL EXAMPLES AND APPLICATIONS

Now let's apply the concepts to a realistic manufacturing company.

## 5.1 Illustrative Manufacturing Scenario

Imagine a company called **ABC Electronics Manufacturing**.

It manufactures electronic components used in various electronic devices.

For simplicity, assume it produces one main product model.

The company is preparing its December supply plan.

### Initial Business Information

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---:|
| Monthly customer demand | 100,000 units |
| Existing finished goods inventory | 15,000 units |
| Monthly production capacity | 90,000 units |
| Raw materials sufficient for | 75,000 units |
| Supplier lead time | 30 days |
| Production days | 20 days |

All figures are illustrative assumptions.

### Step 1: Determine Available Production

The production line has the capacity to manufacture 90,000 units.

However, available raw materials are sufficient for only 75,000 units.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Feasible Production}=\min(90,000,75,000)
\]

\[
=75,000\text{ units}
\]

The company can theoretically produce 90,000 units based on capacity, but material availability restricts actual feasible production to 75,000 units.

### Step 2: Determine Total Potential Supply

Existing finished goods inventory:

15,000 units

Feasible production:

75,000 units

Therefore:

\[
\text{Total Supply}=15,000+75,000
\]

\[
=90,000\text{ units}
\]

### Step 3: Compare Demand and Supply

\[
\text{Demand-Supply Gap}=100,000-90,000
\]

\[
=10,000\text{ units}
\]

### ABC Electronics: December Supply Position

| Measure | Quantity |
|---|---:|
| Demand | 100,000 |
| Potential supply | 90,000 |
| Potential supply coverage | 90% |
| **Unmet demand** | **10,000 units** |

Assumes all existing inventory and feasible production can be used for December demand, with no other allocations or losses.

The company has a demand-supply imbalance.

But the next question is:

**What should the company do?**

## 5.2 Possible Business Responses

### Option A: Increase Raw Material Availability

The company currently has materials for 75,000 units.

It needs an additional 10,000 units of materials to close the supply gap.

But supplier lead time is 30 days.

If the materials will arrive too late, expediting or an alternative sourcing arrangement may be necessary.

### Option B: Increase Production Capacity

The company has a theoretical capacity of 90,000 units.

Even if it increases production capacity, it cannot exceed the available material constraint without obtaining additional raw materials.

This is a critical lesson:

**Increasing machine capacity will not solve a shortage caused by insufficient raw materials.**

### Option C: Reallocate Existing Inventory

The company may review whether finished goods inventory allocated to other customers can be reallocated.

However, this must be evaluated against customer commitments and contractual obligations.

### Option D: Negotiate with Customers

If the supply gap cannot be closed, Sales may coordinate with customers to:

- Adjust delivery schedules.
- Prioritize critical customer orders.
- Deliver available quantities first.
- Arrange partial shipments.

This allows the company to manage customer expectations instead of promising something Operations cannot deliver.

## 5.3 Comparing Three Different Situations

Let's modify the scenario to understand the three demand-supply relationships.

Assume the company can make the following adjustments.

| Situation | Demand | Total feasible supply | Result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| A: Demand exceeds supply | 100,000 | 90,000 | 10,000 shortage |
| B: Supply exceeds demand | 80,000 | 100,000 | 20,000 excess |
| C: Balanced | 100,000 | 100,000 | No quantity gap |

### Situation A: Demand Exceeds Supply

The company needs to investigate the constraint and determine how to close the 10,000-unit gap.

### Situation B: Supply Exceeds Demand

The company needs to evaluate whether producing 100,000 units is justified when expected demand is only 80,000.

Possible action: reduce production by 20,000 units, if there are no other valid requirements for the excess.

### Situation C: Demand and Supply Are Balanced

The company has a feasible plan to satisfy expected customer requirements.

However, it still needs to monitor material delivery, production execution, and customer demand changes.

# 6. FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

For this introductory topic, we will focus on basic demand-supply calculations.

Advanced forecasting, safety stock, inventory policies, capacity calculations, and detailed supply planning will be covered in their respective modules.

## 6.1 Demand-Supply Gap

This measures the difference between customer demand and available supply.

\[
\boxed{\text{Gap}=\text{Demand}-\text{Supply}}
\]

Interpretation:

- Positive result: Demand exceeds supply.
- Negative result: Supply exceeds demand.
- Zero: Demand and supply are equal.

Example:

Demand = 120,000 units

Supply = 100,000 units

\[
120,000-100,000=20,000
\]

There is a shortage of 20,000 units.

## 6.2 Supply Coverage Percentage

This measures how much of the demand can potentially be covered by available supply.

\[
\boxed{\text{Supply Coverage}=\frac{\text{Supply}}{\text{Demand}}\times100}
\]

Example:

Supply = 90,000 units

Demand = 100,000 units

\[
\frac{90,000}{100,000}\times100=90\%
\]

Interpretation:

The company has enough supply to cover 90% of the expected demand.

It has a remaining 10% supply gap.

Important: This is a simple quantity-based coverage measure. It does not guarantee on-time delivery or customer-level fulfillment.

## 6.3 Feasible Production Quantity

For our simplified manufacturing example:

\[
\boxed{\text{Feasible Production}=\min(\text{Production Capacity},\text{Material-Constrained Output})}
\]

Example:

- Production capacity = 100,000 units
- Material-constrained output = 80,000 units

\[
\min(100,000,80,000)=80,000
\]

The feasible production quantity is 80,000 units.

This simplified formula assumes all other production requirements are available. In actual manufacturing, manpower, machine availability, product mix, yield, and other constraints may further reduce output.

## 6.4 Total Potential Supply

For a simplified planning period:

\[
\boxed{\text{Total Supply}=\text{Available Inventory}+\text{Feasible Production}+\text{Confirmed Incoming Supply}}
\]

Example:

- Finished goods inventory = 20,000
- Feasible production = 70,000
- Confirmed incoming finished goods = 10,000

\[
20,000+70,000+10,000=100,000
\]

Total potential supply = 100,000 units.

Be careful not to double-count inventory, planned production, or incoming quantities. Also consider the timing of each quantity.

# 7. BUSINESS PERSPECTIVE AND DECISION-MAKING

Now, let's move from understanding the concepts to thinking like an S&OP analyst.

An S&OP analyst should not simply compare two numbers and report the difference.

The analyst needs to understand:

1. What is driving demand?
2. What determines the available supply?
3. Why is there a difference?
4. What are the consequences?
5. What options are available?
6. What decision should the business make?

## 7.1 How Different Departments View Demand and Supply

Different departments have different priorities.

| Department | Main concern |
|---|---|
| Sales | Can we satisfy customer requirements? |
| Marketing | Will market demand increase or decrease? |
| Production | Can we manufacture the required quantity? |
| Procurement | Are materials available at the right time? |
| Supply Chain | Can we coordinate inventory and deliveries? |
| Finance | What is the financial impact of the plan? |
| Management | What is the best overall business decision? |

This explains why S&OP is cross-functional.

No single department has complete control over both demand and supply.

## 7.2 Understanding the Demand Plan and Supply Plan

Two important concepts to remember:

**Demand Plan**

This represents the company's agreed understanding of expected customer and market requirements over a particular planning horizon.

It considers information such as:

- Customer orders
- Customer forecasts
- Historical demand
- Market expectations
- Sales input

**Supply Plan**

This represents how the company intends to meet the demand, considering available resources and operational constraints.

It considers:

- Existing inventory
- Production capability
- Raw material availability
- Supplier commitments
- Manpower
- Equipment
- Logistics and delivery capability

### How They Connect

**Customer and Market Information**

Orders, forecasts, customer requirements

↓

**Demand Plan**

How much do customers need?

↓

**Supply Plan**

How can the company provide it?

↓

**Demand-Supply Comparison**

Identify shortages, excesses, and constraints

↓

**Management Decisions**

Agree on a feasible business plan

The objective is not necessarily to force supply to equal demand at any cost.

Instead, S&OP helps the business evaluate different options and agree on the most appropriate plan.

## 7.3 What Happens When Demand Cannot Be Fully Satisfied?

Let's say:

- Demand = 150,000 units
- Feasible supply = 120,000 units
- Supply gap = 30,000 units

The company has several options.

| Option | Potential benefit | Potential disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime | Additional output | Higher labor costs |
| Additional shifts | More production time | Additional operating expenses |
| Expedite materials | Earlier material availability | Higher procurement costs |
| Outsourcing | Additional production capacity | Quality and supplier risks |
| Inventory reallocation | Faster fulfillment | May affect other customers |
| Delivery rescheduling | More manageable commitments | Customer dissatisfaction risk |

An S&OP analyst should quantify these alternatives where possible and help management understand their trade-offs.

For example:

Would it be better to spend an additional ₱500,000 on overtime and expedited materials, or accept a delivery delay that may put a ₱2 million customer order at risk?

That is already a business decision, not merely a production calculation.

## 7.4 What Does a Good S&OP Analyst Look For?

A good S&OP analyst looks beyond whether the numbers match.

For example:

| Question | What the analyst investigates |
|---|---|
| Why did demand increase? | New orders, customer forecast changes, market growth |
| Why is supply insufficient? | Capacity constraints, material shortages, downtime |
| Can the shortage be resolved? | Alternative suppliers, overtime, inventory reallocation |
| What happens if it is not resolved? | Revenue exposure, delayed orders, customer impact |
| Is excess production justified? | Future demand, inventory targets, production economics |
| What decision is required? | Management approval and cross-functional commitment |

The goal is to provide management with a clear picture of the business situation and its available choices.

# 8. KEY TAKEAWAYS

Let's consolidate the most important concepts from Topic 1.3.

## 8.1 Demand

- Demand represents what customers want or are expected to require.
- Customer needs, customer orders, actual sales, and forecasted demand are different concepts.
- Actual sales do not always represent the full market demand.
- Demand is influenced by customers, market conditions, pricing, promotions, and industry trends.

## 8.2 Supply

- Supply represents what a company can make available to satisfy customer requirements.
- Supply depends on inventory, production capacity, materials, manpower, equipment, and suppliers.
- Available inventory is not the same as total supply capability.
- A company may have production capacity but still be unable to produce because of material shortages.

## 8.3 Demand-Supply Relationship

| Relationship | Meaning | Potential problem |
|---|---|---|
| Demand greater than supply | Insufficient supply | Shortages and unmet demand |
| Supply greater than demand | Excess supply | Excess inventory |
| Demand equals supply | Quantity balance | Still requires feasibility and timing checks |

## 8.4 S&OP Connection

The central purpose of connecting demand and supply is to establish a feasible business plan.

Remember these three questions:

1. **Demand:** What do our customers need?
2. **Supply:** What can our company provide?
3. **S&OP:** What should our company do to balance the two?

**The most important principle of Topic 1.3**

A company should not plan production based only on what it can manufacture, nor should it promise customers based only on what they want.

It needs to understand both demand and supply, identify the gaps, and make coordinated business decisions.
